## Abstract

Doctorow's essay explains an "AI paradox" — why some users find AI liberating while others find it immiserating — with a single distinction from automation theory. A **centaur** is "a human being who is assisted by a machine (a human head on a strong and tireless body)"; a **reverse centaur** is "a machine that uses a human being as its assistant (a frail and vulnerable person being puppeteered by an uncaring, relentless machine)." The paradox dissolves once you ask *who chose the tool*: centaurs use AI because they decided to, keeping it only "to the extent that it is useful, and throwing it away when it's not"; reverse centaurs have AI imposed on them from above, made to work at the machine's pace and quota. The reverse centaur's real job is to be the AI's "accountability sink" (Dan Davies's phrase) — "paid to take the blame for the AI's mistakes." Doctorow frames resistance as political, against a "vulgar Thatcherism" ("there is no alternative") pushed by tech bosses, and argues AI is a bubble whose burst may still leave a "productive residue" (open-source models like Whisper). The 2026 book extends the metaphor: the reverse centaur is "a human being conscripted as the assistant to a dominant machine" — the delivery driver, the break-less warehouse worker, the programmer reviewing "impossible amounts of AI-produced code."

**Key points**

- **The core definitions (verbatim).** Centaur = "a human being who is assisted by a machine"; reverse centaur = "a machine that uses a human being as its assistant." The book's gloss: "a human being conscripted as the assistant to a dominant machine."
- **The paradox and its resolution.** Same technology, opposite experiences, because what differs is agency: *chosen* AI (centaur) vs *imposed* AI (reverse centaur). "They may make poor choices about the AI, but those choices are theirs… they are not imposed from on high."
- **Accountability sink.** The reverse centaur's function is to absorb blame: the "human in the loop" given a workload "that precluded his being able to do a good job", so really "the AI's 'accountability sink'… paid to take the blame for the AI's mistakes."
- **Anchoring anecdote.** The Hearst summer-reading-guide fiasco — a lone freelancer bylined on a 64-page insert of AI-hallucinated lists, doing "the work of literally dozens of writers, editors and fact-checkers" — as the archetypal reverse centaur; contrasted with Doctorow's own *chosen* use of Whisper to transcribe podcasts ("I was a centaur").
- **Political framing.** Tech bosses practise "vulgar Thatcherism" — Thatcher's "There is no alternative" — to present labour-displacing AI as inevitable; naming the reverse centaur is "a political act of resistance."
- **Bubble thesis.** "AI is a bubble. Bubbles burst." But some bubbles leave "a productive residue" — here, cheap GPUs, better applied-statistics skills, and durable open-source models ("It doesn't matter what happens to OpenAI; Whisper is here to stay"); the collapse will also leave "digital asbestos."

**Takeaways**

- The centaur / reverse-centaur pair is a **memorable one-line frame** for the book's own thesis that AI helps those who can direct it and harms those who cannot — but it is rhetoric, not evidence, and must be cited as such.
- The decisive variable Doctorow isolates — **choice/agency over the tool** — maps directly onto §6.5's "lever multiplies whatever force you bring": imposition without skill or agency is what turns the lever against the worker.

## The paradox

Doctorow opens on a puzzle: "how is it that some AI's users describe their experience as a hellish ordeal, while others delight in the ways that AI is changing their lives for the better?" The essay's whole move is to answer this not by looking at the technology but at the power relation around it.

## Centaur vs reverse centaur (the definitions)

| Term | Doctorow's definition (verbatim) | Who is in charge |
|---|---|---|
| **Centaur** | "a human being who is assisted by a machine (a human head on a strong and tireless body)" | The human directs the machine |
| **Reverse centaur** | "a machine that uses a human being as its assistant (a frail and vulnerable person being puppeteered by an uncaring, relentless machine)" | The machine sets the pace; the human serves it |
| **Reverse centaur (book gloss)** | "a human being conscripted as the assistant to a dominant machine" | The machine dominates |

The metaphor comes "found in automation theory"; Doctorow uses it as a lens, not as a formal model.

## The anchoring example: the AI-hallucinated reading guide

- Hearst published a summer reading guide "full of nonexistent books that had been 'hallucinated' by a chatbot" (widely reported after the *Chicago Sun-Times* ran it).
- 404 Media's Jason Koebler found the bylined freelancer was "practically the sole author of an entire 64-page insert" — tasked with "the work of literally dozens of writers, editors and fact-checkers."
- Koebler's own early career (interning on such lists at the *Washington Monthly*, "part of a team of three interns… backstopped by an extensive fact-checking department") shows how much labour those lists really need.
- Verdict: the writer's job "wasn't to write the article" but to be the "human in the loop" on a schedule that made good work impossible — hence the AI's **"accountability sink"** (Dan Davies's phrase), "paid to take the blame for the AI's mistakes." "He was, in other words, a reverse centaur."

## The contrast: Doctorow as centaur

Doctorow describes downloading Whisper (OpenAI's open-source transcription model) to his own laptop, transcribing 30 hours of podcast audio while doing other work, then searching the transcripts to find and verify a quote. "I was a centaur. I got to decide how to use the AI, and I only had to use it in ways that made my work better and more satisfying."

## The resolution: choice vs imposition

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The paradox resolves on agency. The immiserated are "being immiserated and precaratized by bosses who have been convinced to fire their colleagues and pile their work on the terrorized survivors of the layoffs." The centaurs "choose when and how to use AI… only using AI to the extent that it is useful, and throwing it away when it's not. They may make poor choices about the AI, but those choices are theirs… they are not imposed from on high."

Doctorow's analogy: a cyclist who *chooses* to ride (glorious, or they "end up eating dirt" — but it's their ride) versus a gig-delivery rider "given an impossible quota" with pay "eroded by algorithmic wage discrimination." Same act, opposite experience, because one chose and one was conscripted.

## Political framing: "vulgar Thatcherism"

The naming is "a political act of resistance" against the bully's trick of presenting defeat as a fait accompli — Thatcher's "There is no alternative" ("by which she meant, 'Stop trying to think of alternatives'"). Doctorow lists tech leaders practising "vulgar Thatcherism": Zuckerberg (no private talk without surveillance), Pichai (no search without spying), Cook (no safe computing without his veto on software), Nadella (no editing a Word file without your boss counting your keystrokes). "AI bosses want you to think that the only way to use these tools is to displace and immiserate labor, because that's the promise they raise investment capital on."

## The bubble and its residue

- "AI is a bubble. Bubbles burst." He forecasts "a near-total collapse of the AI investment mania" — most companies failing, data centres never opened or shuttered.
- Some bubbles leave a "productive residue" (his analogy: WorldCom's fraudulently financed dark fibre now powering cheap fibre-optic internet); others, like crypto, leave "nothing much behind of any value."
- AI's likely residue: cheap second-hand GPUs, technical people "much better at applied statistics", and durable **open-source models** — "It doesn't matter what happens to OpenAI; Whisper is here to stay" (already integrated into ffmpeg for auto-captions).
- The harms, meanwhile, are immediate: climate impact, worker immiseration, and — after the burst — "the mass incineration of everyday people's retirement savings" and a lasting loss of societal capability as "defunct foundation models grind to a halt, after the people they replaced are long gone." His image: "filling the walls of society with digital asbestos."

## The book (source 2)

*The Reverse Centaur's Guide to Life After AI: How to Think About Artificial Intelligence Before It's Too Late* (Verso, June 2026, 240pp, ISBN 9781836745525) is the long-form expansion of the column. Its tagline restates the thesis: "It's not enough to ask what the technology does – we must understand who it's doing it *for* and who it's doing it *to*." The blurb defines the reverse centaur as "a human being conscripted as the assistant to a dominant machine" and gives three archetypes: "a driver made to deliver nonstop, all day long; a warehouse worker packing shelves without bathroom or food breaks; or a programmer reviewing impossible amounts of AI-produced code." Its promise: "Life after AI should mean the tools work for us, not the other way around." Brian Eno's cover endorsement calls Doctorow "the most lucid and generous guide I know of."

## Relation to the book

This pair gives §6.5 (*AI and Society*) a sharp, quotable name for the human cost the section already argues from evidence. §6.5's spine is that "AI is a lever, and a lever multiplies whatever force you already bring" — it rewards the person who can already judge its output (Otis et al.'s Kenyan entrepreneurs: +15% for the strong, −8% for the weak) and penalises the one who cannot. Doctorow's **reverse centaur is the lived form of that penalty**: AI *imposed* on those without the skill or agency to direct it, made to serve the machine's pace and to absorb blame for its errors. The **choose-vs-imposed distinction** is the human-scale version of §6.5's discernment argument — the centaur is the discerning user who keeps the tool "only to the extent that it is useful", the reverse centaur is the conscript who cannot. It also connects to §6.4 (*The Future of Work*) on immiseration and layoffs, and echoes the Nadella "human capital only becomes more valuable" material (summarised elsewhere) as its polemical opposite — where Nadella sells the loop, Doctorow names its victim. **Use it as a vivid framing / definitional aside next to the Otis evidence in §6.5, explicitly flagged as an activist critic's coinage, not a finding** — and note the distinct year from any other Doctorow citation (essay 2025, book 2026).
