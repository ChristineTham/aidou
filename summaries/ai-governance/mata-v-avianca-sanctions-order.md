# Mata v. Avianca, Inc. — Opinion and Order on Sanctions

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [mata-v-avianca-sanctions-order.pdf](../../sources/ai-governance/mata-v-avianca-sanctions-order.pdf) — United States District Court, Southern District of New York (P. Kevin Castel, District Judge), *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.*, 678 F. Supp. 3d 443, No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC), signed 22 June 2023. Reported via Thomson Reuters / Westlaw.
> This is the primary judicial opinion. The PDF is the Westlaw reprint; pages 1–7 are West editorial headnotes, and the court's own text (Opinion, Findings of Fact, Conclusions of Law, Conclusion) begins on the reprint's page 7. Appendices A and B (the fake "Varghese" opinion and the ChatGPT screenshots) are reproduced as images and not text-extractable.

## Abstract

This is the landmark sanctions order in which a US federal court penalised lawyers for filing a brief containing fake case law generated by ChatGPT. Attorneys Peter LoDuca and Steven A. Schwartz, and their firm Levidow, Levidow & Oberman P.C., submitted an opposition brief citing at least seven non-existent judicial opinions — complete with fabricated quotes, docket numbers, and invented panels of judges — that the AI tool ChatGPT had made up, then "continued to stand by the fake opinions after judicial orders called their existence into question." Judge Castel imposed a Rule 11 penalty of USD 5,000 jointly and severally, plus mandatory notification of the client and of the real judges whose names were falsely attached to the bogus opinions. The court's central holding is stated plainly at the outset: "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance. But existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings." The sanction rested not on the mere use of AI, but on the failure to verify and the bad-faith doubling-down after the fabrications were exposed.

## Case identification

- **Case name:** *Roberto Mata, Plaintiff, v. Avianca, Inc., Defendant.*
- **Court:** United States District Court, Southern District of New York.
- **Judge:** P. Kevin Castel, United States District Judge.
- **Docket:** 22-cv-1461 (PKC).
- **Reported citation:** 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (2023).
- **Signed / decided:** 22 June 2023 (the "Opinion and Order on Sanctions"; Findings and Conclusions follow the sanctions hearing of 8 June 2023).
- **Respondents (sanctioned):** Peter LoDuca; Steven A. Schwartz; and the law firm Levidow, Levidow & Oberman P.C. (the "Levidow Firm"), collectively the "Respondents".

## Background of the underlying suit

Roberto Mata sued Avianca in New York state court (Verified Complaint filed ~2 February 2022) alleging he was injured when a metal serving cart struck his left knee during a flight from El Salvador to John F. Kennedy Airport. Avianca removed the action to federal court on 22 February 2022, asserting jurisdiction under the Montreal Convention (Convention for the Unification of Certain Rules Relating to International Carriage by Air, done at Montreal, 28 May 1999). On 13 January 2023 Avianca moved to dismiss on the ground that the claim was time-barred under the Montreal Convention. The fake-case brief arose in opposition to that motion.

Schwartz had been listed attorney on the state complaint but is **not admitted to practice in the S.D.N.Y.** LoDuca, who is admitted, filed the notice of appearance and became the sole attorney of record, while Schwartz "continued to perform all substantive legal work."

## The key opening statement of principle (verbatim)

The opinion opens with the passage that is the case's most-cited language:

> "In researching and drafting court submissions, good lawyers appropriately obtain assistance from junior lawyers, law students, contract lawyers, legal encyclopedias and databases such as Westlaw and LexisNexis. Technological advances are commonplace and there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance. But existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings. Rule 11, Fed. R. Civ. P. Peter LoDuca, Steven A. Schwartz and the law firm of Levidow, Levidow & Oberman P.C. ... abandoned their responsibilities when they submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations created by the artificial intelligence tool ChatGPT, then continued to stand by the fake opinions after judicial orders called their existence into question."

The court then enumerates the harms of submitting fake opinions:

> "The opposing party wastes time and money in exposing the deception. The Court's time is taken from other important endeavors. The client may be deprived of arguments based on authentic judicial precedents. There is potential harm to the reputation of judges and courts whose names are falsely invoked as authors of the bogus opinions and to the reputation of a party attributed with fictional conduct. It promotes cynicism about the legal profession and the American judicial system. And a future litigant may be tempted to defy a judicial ruling by disingenuously claiming doubt about its authenticity."

## What happened (Findings of Fact)

- **1 March 2023:** LoDuca filed an "Affirmation in Opposition" (drafted by Schwartz) citing and quoting purported decisions said to be published in the Federal Reporter, Federal Supplement and Westlaw, above the declaration "I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct." LoDuca signed and filed it but was not its author; he reviewed it only "for style" and "did not review any judicial authorities cited."
- **15 March 2023:** Avianca's reply stated counsel "has been unable to locate most of the case law cited" and named seven purported decisions it could not find. No Respondent moved to withdraw the affirmation.
- **11 & 12 April 2023:** The court ordered LoDuca to produce copies of the cited decisions (Varghese, Shaboon, Peterson, Martinez, Durden, Ehrlich, Miller, In re Air Crash Disaster; plus Zicherman), warning that failure would result in dismissal under Rule 41(b).
- **25 April 2023:** LoDuca's affidavit (again drafted by Schwartz) annexed excerpts of all but one decision, admitting he "was unable to locate" Zicherman.
- **Schwartz's use of ChatGPT:** The Levidow Firm practises mainly in New York state courts, uses Fastcase (with limited federal-case access) and has no Westlaw or LexisNexis account. Schwartz "falsely assumed [ChatGPT] was like a super search engine." He had never used it before. Prompts such as "provide case law in support...", "show me specific holdings in federal cases...", "show me more cases" and "give me some cases" caused ChatGPT to fabricate the opinions. His first substantive prompt: "argue that the statute of limitations is tolled by bankruptcy of defendant pursuant to montreal convention."
- **The fabrications:** The fake "Varghese v. China Southern Airlines" was attributed to a fictional Eleventh Circuit panel, bore a docket number belonging to a different case, contained "gibberish" legal analysis, unpaired quotation marks, and an abrupt ending, and internally cited further non-existent cases. "Miller v. United Airlines" falsely listed former US Attorney General Alberto R. Gonzales as defence counsel. "Petersen v. Iran Air" cited itself as its own precedent and confused D.C. with Washington State. Court clerks confirmed the opinions did not exist.
- **Doubling down:** Schwartz testified he "just never thought it could be made up" and, even after the court's orders, asked ChatGPT whether "Varghese" was real; ChatGPT insisted it "does indeed exist" and was on Westlaw/LexisNexis. Schwartz's shifting accounts (that ChatGPT merely "supplemented" his research, when in fact "it was your research") supported the finding of bad faith. LoDuca lied to obtain an extension, falsely claiming he was "out of the office on vacation" (it was Schwartz who was away).
- **No Respondent ever wrote to the court to withdraw the 1 March Affirmation** or advise that it could no longer be relied upon.
- The truth only began to emerge on 25 May 2023, after the court's first Order to Show Cause.

## The court's legal reasoning (Conclusions of Law)

- **Rule 11(b)(2)** certifies, upon presenting a paper "whether by signing, filing, submitting, or later advocating it," that legal contentions are "warranted by existing law." Rule 11 "explicitly and unambiguously imposes an affirmative duty on each attorney to conduct a reasonable inquiry into the viability of a pleading before it is signed."
- **A fake opinion is not "existing law":** "A fake opinion is not 'existing law' and citation to a fake opinion does not provide a non-frivolous ground for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law, or for establishing new law. ... An attempt to persuade a court or oppose an adversary by relying on fake opinions is an abuse of the adversary system."
- **The duty is continuing:** Rule 11 compliance "is not assessed solely at the moment that the paper is submitted." The obligation includes "reaffirming to the court and advocating positions ... after learning that they cease to have any merit." Failure to correct a prior statement in a pending motion "is the later advocacy of that statement and is subject to sanctions."
- **No forgery under 18 U.S.C. § 505:** The fake opinions bore no signature or seal, so they did not violate the criminal statute against forging a judge's signature — though the court noted the conduct "raises similar concerns."
- **Sua sponte sanctions require subjective bad faith,** "a heightened mens rea standard," which may be shown by "conscious avoidance" — awareness of a high probability of a fact while consciously avoiding confirming it. The court found subjective bad faith in **both** individual respondents:
  - **LoDuca:** signing and filing without reading a single cited case; swearing to an affidavit he had never seen in draft "with no basis for doing so"; and lying about being on vacation to get an extension.
  - **Schwartz:** consciously avoiding confirming that "Varghese" and "Zicherman" did not exist despite red flags; the untruthful "supplement" characterisation; and shifting, contradictory explanations.
- **Firm liability:** Under Rule 11(c)(1), "[a]bsent exceptional circumstances, a law firm must be held jointly responsible for a violation committed by its partner, associate, or employee." No exceptional circumstances existed, so the Levidow Firm was jointly and severally liable.
- **28 U.S.C. § 1927 declined:** reliance on fake cases caused several harms "but dilatory tactics and delay were not among them."

## The sanction imposed

The purpose of a Rule 11 sanction is **deterrence, not punishment or compensation**; the court "should impose the least severe sanctions necessary to achieve that goal." Mitigating factors: the firm had already arranged mandatory CLE on technological competence and AI; Avianca sought no fees; the fake cases were "not submitted for any respondent's financial gain" or "personal animus"; and the Respondents had no disciplinary history and expressed credible remorse. The court declined to compel an apology, reasoning "a compelled apology is not a sincere apology."

Ordered sanctions (within 14 days):

1. Respondents must send Roberto Mata a letter attaching the Opinion and Order, the 8 June 2023 hearing transcript, and the 25 April Affirmation with exhibits.
2. Respondents must send a letter to **each judge falsely identified as the author** of the fake "Varghese", "Shaboon", "Petersen", "Martinez", "Durden" and "Miller" opinions, attaching the Opinion and Order, the transcript, and the fake opinion attributed to that judge.
3. Respondents must file copies of the letters with the court.
4. **"A penalty of $5,000 is jointly and severally imposed on Respondents and shall be paid into the Registry of this Court within 14 days"** — "sufficient but not more than necessary to advance the goals of specific and general deterrence."

## Why it matters for AI governance

*Mata v. Avianca* is the first widely reported judicial sanction for AI-fabricated legal authority ("hallucinated" citations), and it establishes the governing principle for professional use of generative AI: the tool is permitted, but the human retains a non-delegable **gatekeeping duty of verification**, and existing professional rules (here Rule 11) already impose the responsibility to check. The sanction attached to the failure to verify and the bad-faith persistence after exposure — not to the act of using AI itself. It is the canonical named case for the accountability-and-oversight discussion in §5.5.
