# Balancing Innovation and Control: The European Union AI Act in an Era of Global Uncertainty

> Peer-reviewed open-access viewpoint article (JMIR AI). This extraction is the PMC mirror (PMCID PMC12574960, PMID 41166703). The paper is framed around **health care AI** specifically, though its innovation-vs-control arguments generalise. It cites concrete cost figures from a European Commission impact-assessment study rather than deriving them itself. The authors do **not** name the Draghi competitiveness report; the "brain drain"/competitiveness concern appears in their own words.

## Abstract

This is a peer-reviewed viewpoint arguing that the EU AI Act, adopted in 2024, is a "landmark regulatory framework" that is genuinely necessary for patient safety but risks stifling innovation unless it is calibrated carefully — the central innovation-vs-control tension. The Act classifies medical AI as "high-risk", imposing "stringent requirements for transparency, data governance, and human oversight". The authors' most citable claim is that compliance can cost roughly **€29,277 (US $34,153) annually per AI unit**, plus **€16,800–23,000 per unit** in certification costs, "a substantial burden for resource-constrained organizations". They argue these costs disproportionately hit SMEs and startups and may entrench dominant "superstar" firms. Critically, the paper does **not** call for deregulation: it endorses **regulatory sandboxes, AI literacy programmes, and international collaboration** as the remedies that let innovation and compliance coexist. Its thesis: "The EU AI Act provides a critical framework for ethical AI in health care, but its success depends on mitigating regulatory burdens and geopolitical risks."

## The tension, stated

The authors frame the Act as a double-edged instrument. On the safety side: "the AI Act's high-risk classification for medical AI could improve patient safety". On the innovation side, the same classification "risks stifling innovation due to compliance costs … and certification burdens". They are explicit that this is a balancing problem, not a case for scrapping the rules — the title itself, *Balancing Innovation and Control*, is the argument.

## The compliance-cost and competitiveness critique

- **Per-unit compliance cost.** Drawing on the European Commission's "Study to Support an Impact Assessment of Regulatory Requirements for Artificial Intelligence in Europe" (Renda et al., 2021), the authors state: "compliance costs for a single AI unit could reach €29,277 (US $34,153) annually—a substantial burden for resource-constrained organizations."
- **Certification cost.** "Startups may face additional challenges due to certification costs (€16,800‐23,000 per unit equivalent to US $19,598-26831 per unit) and the complexity of meeting requirements for human oversight, data governance, and transparency."
- **High-risk capture.** "While the regulation targets only 10% of AI systems as 'high-risk,' health care innovations often fall into this category, potentially stifling innovation or diverting funds from research and development leading to a concentration of AI development in larger, well-funded organizations, potentially stifling competition and innovation."
- **Burden on smaller players.** Compliance "may disproportionately affect smaller health care providers or startups, particularly those developing or using high-risk AI applications". A shortage of certifiers compounds this: "The lack of qualified notified bodies for certification may further delay market entry for smaller players."
- **"Superstar" firm concentration.** The Act's costs "may unintentionally entrench this asymmetry by privileging those with existing infrastructure for regulatory conformity", where "'superstar' firms—large multinational technology corporations with extensive computational resources, proprietary data access, and deep regulatory expertise" already dominate. This "raises important questions about market concentration, regulatory capture, and the diminishing space for entrepreneurial and community-based innovation".
- **Competitiveness / brain-drain risk.** In the geopolitics section: "Europe risks falling behind in the global AI race if its regulatory frameworks are perceived as overly restrictive, potentially leading to a 'brain drain' of AI talent to more innovation-friendly regions." The Act "may also be seen as a barrier to innovation if it is not balanced with sufficient support for research and development." (The Draghi competitiveness report is not cited by name.)

## The counter-balance — why this is not a deregulation paper

The authors deliberately give the other side of the ledger, which is what makes this a *balanced* source rather than a critique:

- **Cost mitigations exist.** "the study notes that existing General Data Protection Regulation compliance (which overlaps with some AI Act requirements) might mitigate costs for some small and medium-sized enterprises, and sector-specific guidance could ease implementation."
- **Industry sees the Act as an enabler, not a barrier.** "several regulatory bodies and major medical technology firms have responded positively to the AI Act, viewing it not as a barrier but as an enabler of innovation and trust." Some manufacturers "have expressed support for classifying AI-based diagnostic tools as high-risk, arguing that clear regulatory requirements reduce legal uncertainty and promote safer, more consistent deployment at scale."
- **Adaptation is feasible.** Firms are adopting "AI lifecycle management platforms and automated conformity assessment tools" and building "dedicated AI regulatory and ethics teams". The authors conclude "successful adaptation to the AI Act is feasible—especially for organizations that proactively invest in regulatory preparedness and multistakeholder collaboration."

## The remedies the paper endorses

The paper's "Call for Reflection and Action" proposes scalable, calibrated strategies — smart regulation, not rollback:

1. **Context-aware multidisciplinary task forces** — advisory groups of "AI experts, clinicians, ethicists, and legal scholars" at national/regional level to find implementation bottlenecks; not replacing the EU's legislative role. Cites the European AI Alliance as precedent.
2. **Regulatory sandboxes** — "controlled testing environments where AI technologies can be piloted under temporary regulatory flexibility, with close oversight from authorities", with safeguards "like mandatory human oversight, real-time bias audits, and strict patient consent protocols". Cites the UK FCA's fintech sandbox and the FDA precertification programme as models that "could accelerate compliance while mitigating risks."
3. **AI literacy and training** — investment so professionals "can confidently integrate AI into their practices while adhering to regulatory requirements", via "a federated approach … modular training curricula that can be localized and adapted by individual Member States", with EU agencies "setting minimum competency standards and offering open-access multilingual materials."
4. **Geopolitically resilient supply chains** — "diversification and strategic redundancy", dual-sourcing, and "EU-based innovation hubs, such as the European Chips Act initiative", to reduce dependence on single points of failure (e.g. semiconductors).
5. **International collaboration without sacrificing sovereignty** — using the WHO, OECD, and G7/G20 AI initiatives as platforms "for aligning regulatory principles without ceding national control", so that "Shared frameworks can lower regulatory friction and support equitable access."
6. **Human-augmented / human-in-the-loop AI** — for "high-stakes clinical applications (eg, diagnosis, triage, treatment decisions)", proposing "risk-tiered guidelines, where human oversight is proportionate to potential patient harm" — a proportionality principle rather than blanket oversight.
7. **Ethical AI development** — "public reporting of algorithmic impact assessments, bias audits, and governance structures—similar to financial disclosures", plus procurement policy favouring ethically compliant vendors.

## Geopolitical framing (context for §5.2)

The authors situate the Act within "global uncertainty": rising European military expenditure (Ukraine, NATO rearmament) diverting funds from health/AI R&D; US technology tariffs and US–China semiconductor tensions creating "supply chain vulnerabilities"; and the US–China "race" pressuring Europe's "technological sovereignty". They also flag second-order effects the Act's debate usually ignores — labour-market disruption ("deaths of despair", after Case & Deaton) and the environmental/energy footprint of large-model training as an indirect public-health risk.

## Conclusion (verbatim thesis)

"The EU AI Act offers a vital framework for ensuring the safe and ethical use of AI in health care but its effectiveness depends on balanced implementation amid geopolitical uncertainty. To avoid stifling innovation, regulatory efforts must be paired with support for adaptation, investment in AI literacy, and resilient infrastructure." The authors "urge policymakers, health care leaders, and industry to collaborate on practical solutions, such as regulatory sandboxes, ethical oversight, and international alignment."
