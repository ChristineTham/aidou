# Understanding the standardisation of the AI Act

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [eu-standardisation-ai-act-faq.md](../../sources/ai-governance/eu-standardisation-ai-act-faq.md) — European Commission (Shaping Europe's digital future / AI Office), *Understanding the standardisation of the AI Act*, General FAQ, last updated 10 March 2026. https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu (Shaping Europe's digital future portal).
> This is a primary, official explanatory document from the European Commission describing how harmonised standards operate under the AI Act. It is explanatory FAQ text, not the AI Act's legislative articles themselves.

## Abstract

This European Commission FAQ explains the machinery by which the EU's AI Act connects technical standards to legal compliance. Under the EU's New Legislative Framework, harmonised standards "translate legal requirements into technical specifications", and compliance with them confers a legally significant benefit: the presumption of conformity. The Commission has requested the European Standardisation Organisations — CEN and CENELEC (which established the joint technical committee JTC 21 in June 2021) — to develop harmonised standards for high-risk AI systems. The single most citable mechanism, stated verbatim: "A product or system is presumed to satisfy the essential requirements of the relevant EU legislation, if it complies with harmonised standards referenced in the Official Journal of the European Union (OJEU). This 'presumption of conformity' shifts the regulatory burden of proof on public authorities to disprove conformity." This is the route that turns ISO-style technical standards into the practical, legally-recognised path to AI Act compliance.

## What harmonised standards are and who develops them

European harmonised standards "translate legal requirements into technical specifications". They are developed by the recognised European Standardisation Organisations — **CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI** — following a request (a "standardisation request") from the European Commission, and can be used by providers/manufacturers of products for the EU market to demonstrate compliance.

In **June 2021**, CEN and CENELEC established a joint technical committee, **JTC 21**, dedicated to drafting AI standards including the harmonised standards for the AI Act. JTC 21 brings together experts from industry, society, and academia, who join via their National Standardisation Bodies.

Harmonised standards are currently being developed for high-risk AI systems requirements such as risk management, data quality, and human oversight.

## The presumption of conformity (core legal mechanism)

The central mechanism, quoted verbatim from the source:

> "A product or system is presumed to satisfy the essential requirements of the relevant EU legislation, if it complies with harmonised standards referenced in the Official Journal of the European Union (OJEU). This 'presumption of conformity' shifts the regulatory burden of proof on public authorities to disprove conformity."

The source adds: "Once harmonised standards for the AI Act are finalised and approved by the Commission, they will provide a clear path to demonstrate compliance for high-risk AI systems providers and reduce the risk of legal challenges."

On why this matters: "Harmonised standards, especially those which are conferred 'presumption of conformity', offer legal certainty under the AI Act, and support AI providers to develop compliant AI systems." The presumption of conformity is described as "unique to harmonised standards" — distinguishing them from voluntary codes of practice.

## Requirements the harmonised standards will cover

The European Commission has requested CEN and CENELEC to draft harmonised standards for the following areas (mapping to the AI Act's high-risk requirements):

- Risk management systems for AI systems
- Governance and quality of datasets of AI systems
- Record keeping through logging capabilities of AI systems
- Transparency and information provisions for users of AI systems
- Human oversight of AI systems
- Accuracy specifications for AI systems
- Robustness specifications for AI systems
- Cybersecurity specifications for AI systems
- Quality management systems for providers of AI systems, including post-market monitoring processes
- Conformity assessment for AI systems

Where referenced in the OJEU, each harmonised standard "will be accompanied by an annex which maps the legal requirements in the AI Act to the clauses in the standard."

## How the Commission ensures alignment

Every harmonised standard "begins with a 'standardisation request' from the Commission to the standardisation bodies", which "sets the process in motion and ensures alignment with the legal text by clarifying which legal requirements the harmonised standards should aim to cover." The Commission does not draft the standards, but registers "European Counsellors" in JTC 21 who attend plenary, working group, and task group meetings as observers/counsellors. After publication, the Commission reviews standards substantively; if they "accurately reflect the legal provisions", the Commission submits them for reference in the OJEU, "giving them presumption of conformity."

## Timeline

The first harmonised standards are "expected to be published by CEN and CENELEC in **2026**." After publication, the Commission begins its review to assess whether references to those standards can be published in the OJEU. CEN and CENELEC are responsible for planning the harmonised standards.

## Relation to codes of practice and to international standards

**Codes of practice:** The Commission has endorsed a General-Purpose AI Code of Practice and is facilitating a Code of Practice on transparent AI systems. These are "voluntary tools" for GPAI models and transparency obligations. Harmonised standards are similar practical tools, but focus specifically on high-risk AI systems and carry "the added benefit of presumption of conformity, which is unique to harmonised standards."

**International standards:** Agreements such as the Vienna Agreement between CEN and ISO allow European standards to reference or adopt international standards in full. "European standardisation efforts rely preferably on international standards. However, if international standards do not align with the legal requirements of the AI Act, new standards will provide guidance on compliance." Concrete example: "although **ISO/IEC 42001:2023** helps to set up an AI management system, its goals and definitions are not aligned with the quality management system that is required under the AI Act" — so the Commission requested a new, AI-Act-specific quality management system standard.

## Funding note

**StandICT.eu** supports European experts in international standards work; through its Fellowship Programme it "has channelled over €7 million to more than 900 European standards experts." The Commission also funds the European Standardisation Organisations via EU action grants, several of which have been awarded to CEN-CENELEC for AI Act harmonised standards.
