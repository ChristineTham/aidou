# The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [eu-ai-act-regulatory-framework.md](../../sources/ai-governance/eu-ai-act-regulatory-framework.md) — European Commission, *AI Act* (overview page, "Shaping Europe's digital future"), Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology, last updated 11 May 2026. https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai
> This is the European Commission's official explainer page *about* the primary instrument, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It is authoritative but secondary to the regulation's own text; where the page paraphrases obligations, treat the wording as the Commission's summary rather than the statute's exact language.

## Abstract

The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence) is, in the Commission's words, "the first-ever comprehensive legal framework on AI worldwide." It takes a risk-based approach, sorting AI systems into four tiers — unacceptable, high, transparency (limited), and minimal/no risk — and attaching obligations proportionate to risk. Eight practices deemed an unacceptable risk are outright prohibited (e.g. social scoring, untargeted scraping of the internet or CCTV to build facial-recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and education, real-time remote biometric identification by law enforcement in public spaces). High-risk systems face strict pre-market obligations (risk management, data quality, logging, documentation, human oversight, robustness and cybersecurity), while general-purpose AI (GPAI) models carry transparency and copyright obligations, with additional systemic-risk assessment for the most capable/widely-used models. The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies on a staged timeline, becoming fully applicable on 2 August 2026 with some obligations earlier (prohibitions and AI literacy from 2 February 2025; GPAI and governance from 2 August 2025) and some later (embedded high-risk products to 2 August 2028).

## What the Act is and why it exists

The AI Act is "the first-ever legal framework on AI, which addresses the risks of AI and positions Europe to play a leading role globally." Its stated aim is "to foster trustworthy AI in Europe." It sets "risk-based rules for AI developers and deployers regarding specific uses of AI" and forms part of a wider package (AI Continent Action Plan, AI Innovation Package, AI Factories). To ease transition, the Commission launched the voluntary **AI Pact** and an **AI Act Service Desk**.

The Commission's rationale: most AI poses limited-to-no risk, but some systems create risks that must be addressed. It cites opacity as an example — "it is often not possible to find out why an AI system has made a decision" — which makes it hard to assess whether someone was unfairly disadvantaged in, e.g., a hiring decision or a public-benefit application.

## The four risk levels

"The AI Act defines 4 levels of risk for AI systems": **unacceptable risk**, **high risk**, **transparency risk** (limited-transparency), and **minimal or no risk**.

### Unacceptable risk — the eight prohibited practices

"All AI systems considered a clear threat to the safety, livelihoods and rights of people are banned." The AI Act prohibits eight practices:

1. harmful AI-based manipulation and deception
2. harmful AI-based exploitation of vulnerabilities
3. social scoring
4. individual criminal offence risk assessment or prediction
5. untargeted scraping of the internet or CCTV material to create or expand facial recognition databases
6. emotion recognition in workplaces and education institutions
7. biometric categorisation to deduce certain protected characteristics
8. real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement purposes in publicly accessible spaces

The prohibitions "became effective in February 2025." To support application, the Commission published the **Guidelines on prohibited AI practices** (legal explanations and practical examples) and the **Guidelines on the AI system definition** (to help determine the Act's scope).

### High risk

"AI use cases that can pose serious risks to health, safety or fundamental rights are classified as high-risk." Listed use cases include: AI safety components in critical infrastructure (e.g. transport); AI in education institutions determining access to education (e.g. exam scoring); AI safety components of products (e.g. robot-assisted surgery); AI tools for employment and worker management (e.g. CV-sorting for recruitment); AI giving access to essential private/public services (e.g. credit scoring); AI for remote biometric identification, emotion recognition and biometric categorisation (e.g. retroactively identifying a shoplifter); AI in law enforcement affecting fundamental rights (e.g. evaluating reliability of evidence); AI in migration, asylum and border control (e.g. automated visa-application examination); and AI in the administration of justice and democratic processes (e.g. preparing court rulings).

High-risk systems face "strict obligations before they can be put on the market":

- adequate risk assessment and mitigation systems
- high-quality datasets to minimise risks of discriminatory outcomes
- logging of activity to ensure traceability of results
- detailed documentation on the system and its purpose for compliance assessment
- clear and adequate information to the deployer
- appropriate human oversight measures
- high level of robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy

In practice, once on the market: authorities handle market surveillance, deployers ensure human oversight and monitoring, providers run a post-market monitoring system, and both providers and deployers report serious incidents and malfunctioning.

### Transparency risk (limited transparency)

Covers "risks associated with a need for transparency around the use of AI." The Act introduces disclosure obligations: humans should be told when they are interacting with a machine (e.g. chatbots); providers of generative AI must ensure AI-generated content is identifiable; and certain content "should be clearly and visibly labelled, namely deep fakes and text published with the purpose to inform the public on matters of public interest." "The transparency rules of the AI Act will come into effect in August 2026."

### Minimal or no risk

"The AI Act does not introduce rules for AI that is deemed minimal or no risk." The Commission states "the vast majority of AI systems currently used in the EU fall into this category" — e.g. AI-enabled video games or spam filters.

## General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models

GPAI models "can perform a wide range of tasks and are becoming the basis for many AI systems in the EU. Some of these models could carry systemic risks if they are very capable or widely used." The Act therefore imposes rules on GPAI providers, including "transparency and copyright-related rules." For models that may carry systemic risks, "providers should assess and mitigate these risks." "The AI Act rules on GPAI became effective in August 2025."

In **July 2025**, the Commission published three instruments to support responsible GPAI development:

1. **Guidelines on the scope of the obligations for providers of GPAI models** — clarify who along the AI value chain must comply.
2. **The GPAI Code of Practice** — "a voluntary compliance tool submitted to the Commission by independent experts," offering practical guidance on obligations related to transparency, copyright, and safety & security.
3. **The Template for the public summary of training content of GPAI models** — requires providers to give an overview of the data used to train their models, including sources (large datasets and top domain names) and data-processing aspects enabling parties with legitimate interests to exercise EU-law rights.

Further transparency support tools are under preparation for publication in **the second quarter of 2026**: a **Code of Practice on marking and labelling of AI-generated content** (voluntary; covers marking AI-generated content and disclosing the artificial nature of images, audio including deepfakes, and text) and **Guidelines on transparent AI systems**. (Note: the page's own news list records that the Commission published the Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026.)

## Governance and enforcement

The **European AI Office** and Member-State authorities are "responsible for implementing, supervising and enforcing the AI Act." The **AI Board**, the **Scientific Panel**, and the **Advisory Forum** "steer and advise the AI Act's governance."

## Application timeline

"The AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, and will be fully applicable 2 years later on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions":

- **Prohibited AI practices and AI literacy obligations** — entered into application from **2 February 2025**.
- **Governance rules and obligations for GPAI models** — applicable from **2 August 2025**.
- **High-risk AI systems embedded into regulated products** — extended transition period until **2 August 2028** (a result of the political agreement on the 'AI omnibus' simplification proposal).

## Simplification — the 'AI omnibus'

The Digital Package on Simplification proposed amendments (the 'AI omnibus') to keep the rules "clear, simple, and innovation-friendly." The page states the proposal "has been adopted on 19 November 2025 and a political agreement was reached on 7 May 2026." Following that agreement, a revised implementation timeline for high-risk systems is set:

- Rules for systems in certain high-risk areas — biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control — "will apply from **2 December 2027**."
- Systems integrated into products such as lifts or toys — rules "will apply from **2 August 2028**."

Additional agreed amendments include: prohibiting AI that generates non-consensual sexually explicit/intimate content or child sexual abuse material (e.g. 'nudification' apps); reinforcing the AI Office's powers and centralising oversight of systems built on GPAI models; extending simplified requirements (e.g. simplified technical documentation) to SMEs and small mid-cap companies (SMCs); widening access to regulatory sandboxes, including an EU-level sandbox; and clarifying the interplay between the AI Act and EU product-safety laws (notably the Machinery Regulation).
