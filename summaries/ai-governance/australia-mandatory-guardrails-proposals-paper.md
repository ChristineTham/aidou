# Safe and responsible AI in Australia: Proposals paper for introducing mandatory guardrails for AI in high-risk settings

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [australia-mandatory-guardrails-proposals-paper.pdf](../../sources/ai-governance/australia-mandatory-guardrails-proposals-paper.pdf) — Australian Government, Department of Industry, Science and Resources (DISR), *Safe and responsible AI in Australia: Proposals paper for introducing mandatory guardrails for AI in high-risk settings*, Commonwealth of Australia, September 2024. https://consult.industry.gov.au/
> This is a primary government consultation ("proposals") paper seeking public feedback — it proposes, rather than enacts, the guardrails. Attributed as *Proposals Paper for Introducing Mandatory Guardrails for AI in High-Risk Settings*, licensed CC BY 4.0.

## Abstract

This is the Australian Government's September 2024 consultation paper setting out how it proposes to mandate preventative "guardrails" on organisations that develop and deploy AI in high-risk settings. It proposes **ten mandatory guardrails** (organised around testing, transparency and accountability) that would apply *across the AI supply chain and throughout the AI lifecycle* to **developers** and **deployers**; a **principles-based** (not list-based) definition of high-risk AI built on **six principles**; and a proposal that the guardrails apply to **all general-purpose AI (GPAI) models** on the reasoning that "all GPAI are inherently high-risk." It presents **three regulatory options** to mandate the guardrails: (1) a domain-specific approach adapting existing frameworks; (2) framework legislation; (3) a new whole-of-economy AI Act. The paper follows the government's January 2024 interim response, which "concluded a broad consensus that voluntary guardrails and commitments from companies are insufficient to prevent harms occurring." The single most citable definition: an AI system is "a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments."

## The ten proposed mandatory guardrails

The paper proposes 10 guardrails for AI in high-risk settings and GPAI models. Organisations developing or deploying high-risk AI would be required to:

1. **Accountability process** — Establish, implement and *publish* an accountability process including governance, internal capability and a strategy for regulatory compliance. Covers a documented approach to regulatory compliance; policies for data and risk management; clear roles, responsibilities and reporting structures for staff (including contractors/third parties); and staff training. Accountability sits "at the organisational level." Aligns with Canada's AIDA accountability frameworks (s.12) and the EU AI Act's quality management system requirement (Art. 17).
2. **Risk management** — Establish and implement a risk management process to identify and mitigate risks. Aim: eliminate or reduce known or foreseeable risks; where elimination isn't possible, contain/mitigate residual risks. "Not just a technical risk assessment" — must consider impacts on people, community groups and society. Mitigations must be proportionate to severity and likelihood; may extend to reconsidering whether to deploy at all. Can be guided by ISO/IEC 42001 and AS ISO/IEC 23894:2023.
3. **Data governance** — Protect AI systems, and implement data governance measures to manage data quality and provenance. Data must be fit-for-purpose, representative, *legally obtained*, and must not contain illegal/harmful material (e.g. child sexual abuse material or non-consensual intimate imagery); data sources must be disclosed. Covers origin/legality of datasets, data provenance documentation, Indigenous Data Sovereignty and ICIP, data quality, labelling, bias identification and mitigation, and cybersecurity. Complements the Privacy Act 1988, Copyright Act 1968, and Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018.
4. **Testing and monitoring** — Test AI models and systems to evaluate model performance and monitor the system once deployed. Test before market and continuously monitor (e.g. for model drift). GPAI developers "must conduct adversarial testing for any emergent or potentially dangerous capabilities." Consistent with EU AI Act post-market monitoring (Art. 72); supported by ISO/IEC TR 29119-11:2020, SA TR ISO/IEC 24027:2022, and NIST red-teaming guidance.
5. **Human oversight/control** — Enable human control or intervention in an AI system to achieve *meaningful* human oversight. Humans must be able to understand, oversee and intervene across the supply chain and lifecycle. Overseers must be "sufficiently qualified to interpret output and understand the core capabilities and limitations" of the model. Notes real-time human involvement "may not always be practical, and may even make a system less reliable" — in which case systems should be designed so a human can review outputs and reverse a decision.
6. **End-user transparency/disclosure** — Inform end-users regarding AI-enabled decisions, interactions with AI and AI-generated content. Three distinct requirements: (i) inform people when AI is used to make/inform decisions affecting their health, safety or human rights; (ii) inform people when directly interacting with an AI system; (iii) apply **"best efforts"** to ensure AI-generated outputs (text, image, audio, video) can be detected as artificially generated or manipulated (e.g. labelling/watermarking; references the C2PA Content Credentials standard). Requirements (ii)–(iii) in the EU/Canada apply to *all* AI, including low-risk.
7. **Contestability/challenge** — Establish processes for people impacted by AI systems to challenge use or outcomes. Includes internal complaint-handling functions, assigned responsibility for human oversight/intervention on complaints, and provision of sufficient information to make contestability meaningful. Works alongside Australian Consumer Law and administrative law.
8. **Supply-chain transparency** — Be transparent with other organisations across the AI supply chain about data, models and systems to help them effectively address risks. Developers must give deployers information (characteristics, data sources, key design decisions, capabilities/limitations/risks), balanced against protecting commercially sensitive information/trade secrets; deployers must report adverse incidents and significant model failures back to developers. Aligns with EU AI Act Art. 13 and Canada's AIDA "model cards" (s.9(1)(c)).
9. **Record-keeping** — Keep and maintain records to allow third parties to assess compliance with the guardrails. Records include: general system description; design specifications and testing methodology/results; datasets and provenance; human-oversight assessment; capabilities/limitations; risk management processes and mitigations (per EU AI Act Art. 12). Notably: "Any organisation training large state-of-the-art GPAI models with potentially dangerous emergent capabilities must disclose these 'training runs' to the Australian Government" (consistent with the US Executive Order).
10. **Conformity assessment** — Undertake conformity assessments to demonstrate and certify compliance with the guardrails. An accountability/quality-assurance mechanism done *before* placing a high-risk AI system on the market, repeated periodically, and re-done if the system is retrained or significantly changed. May be self-assessment, third-party, or government/regulator assessment. Relies on Guardrail 9 records and the Guardrail 1 accountability process; yields a certification of compliance communicable to the public.

The three named focus areas: **testing** (systems perform as intended, meet performance metrics, before and during deployment), **transparency** (about development and use, with end-users, other supply-chain actors and authorities), and **accountability** (governing and managing AI risks). The approach is explicitly *ex ante* (preventative / pre-market) rather than *ex post* (remedial).

## Defining high-risk AI: a principles-based approach with six principles

The paper proposes a **principles-based** definition rather than a fixed list of use cases (contrasting with the EU and Canada's list-based approaches; the paper explicitly asks consultees which they prefer). Rationale: a principles approach gives flexibility, avoids inadvertently catching low-risk applications, and accommodates step-changes in the technology. Two broad categories of high-risk AI are proposed: (1) AI whose uses are **known or foreseeable** (assessed against the principles), and (2) advanced, highly-capable **GPAI models** whose applications cannot all be foreseen.

**The six principles.** In designating an AI system as high-risk due to its use, regard must be given to:

- **a. Human rights** — the risk of adverse impacts to an individual's rights recognised in Australian human rights law without justification, in addition to Australia's international human rights law obligations (e.g. ICCPR, ICESCR). Notes Australia, unlike the EU/Canada/US, has no single federal human rights charter, so the principle links to both domestic and international obligations; incorporates proportionality.
- **b. Physical or mental health or safety** — the risk of adverse impacts to an individual's physical or mental health or safety.
- **c. Legal effects** — the risk of adverse legal effects, defamation or similarly significant effects on an individual (relevant esp. where people cannot opt out or where access to essential services — law enforcement, housing, finance — is affected; cf. GDPR Art. 22(1)).
- **d. Impacts on groups** — the risk of adverse impacts to groups of individuals or collective rights of cultural groups (e.g. First Nations people; gender and intersecting factors).
- **e. Systemic impacts** — the risk of adverse impacts to the broader Australian economy, society, environment and rule of law (e.g. mis/disinformation, deepfakes, election integrity — the paper cites the Home Affairs "7 dimensions of AI's impact on democracy").
- **f. Severity and extent** — the severity and extent of those adverse impacts outlined in principles (a) to (e).

"The principles should be considered as a whole." Defence and national security applications are proposed to be treated separately from civilian applications (aligning with the US and EU). The paper also notes the EU AI Act *prohibits* certain uses outright (exploitative/subliminal/deceptive AI; certain biometric categorisation) and seeks feedback on whether any uses should be banned in Australia.

## GPAI: guardrails would apply to ALL general-purpose AI models

The paper proposes that the mandatory guardrails apply to **all GPAI models**. Proposed definition of GPAI (drawn from Canada's AIDA, "based on but not identical to"): "An AI model that is capable of being used, or capable of being adapted for use, for a variety of purposes, both for direct use as well as for integration in other systems." The reasoning: GPAI poses *unforeseeable* risks because it can be applied in contexts it was not designed for; Canada's draft legislation "assumes that all GPAI are inherently high-risk because of the nature and ability of such models." Because most highly-capable GPAI models are not developed domestically, alignment with international jurisdictions is emphasised to reduce compliance burden.

Consultation Q7 asks what indicators should define GPAI as high-risk — e.g. whether the principles suffice, or whether to use technical capability such as **FLOPS (e.g. a 10^25 or 10^26 threshold)**, a scientific panel's advice, or other indicators. It notes the EU applies a minimum set of guardrails to all GPAI plus extra requirements for GPAI posing "systemic risk" (EU AI Act Art. 51), and the US Executive Order mandates reporting above certain capability thresholds.

## The three regulatory options to mandate the guardrails

1. **Option 1 — Domain-specific approach:** Adapt existing regulatory frameworks to embed the guardrails on a sector-by-sector basis; regulators issue guidance within their remits. Benefit: leverages Australia's mature, familiar laws; incremental; limits duplication for single-sector entities. Limitation: risks exacerbating gaps and inconsistencies, regulatory siloes; pre-market guardrails only possible where existing frameworks already allow pre-market obligations (e.g. therapeutic goods); slower economy-wide coverage. Similar to the UK's (Sunak-era) "pro-innovation" approach.
2. **Option 2 — Framework approach:** Introduce new **framework legislation** providing consistent definitions, high-risk thresholds and guardrails, then activate them through amendments to existing laws (enforced by existing regulators using existing penalties). Cited example of framework legislation: the *Regulatory Powers (Standard Provisions) Act 2014*. Benefit: consistency of definitions, reduces regulatory arbitrage, supports interoperability. Limitation: still confined to the scope/powers of the laws amended to refer to it; retains gaps; needs cross-agency coordination.
3. **Option 3 — Whole-of-economy approach:** Introduce a **new cross-economy AI-specific Act** (e.g. an "Australian AI Act") defining high-risk applications and the guardrails, with a monitoring/enforcement regime overseen by an **independent AI regulator** (new, or an existing regulator with expanded powers), working alongside existing regulators with carve-outs where existing laws already cover the guardrails. Modelled on Canada's AIDA. Unlike framework legislation, it has stand-alone enforceable provisions. Both the EU and Canada took the new-AI-legislation route.

Separately and in parallel with all three options, the government "will continue to strengthen and clarify existing laws" (Privacy Act reform, automated decision-making in government, health/consumer/copyright reviews).

## Key definitions (verbatim / near-verbatim)

- **AI system:** "a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments."
- **AI model** vs **AI system:** the model is "the raw, mathematical essence… the 'engine'"; the system is "the ensemble of several components, including one or more AI models" (example: ChatGPT is the system, GPT-4 the model).
- **Agentic AI:** "AI that is capable of accomplishing multi-step tasks in pursuit of a high-level goal with little or no human oversight." The paper flags "loss of control" concerns and that agentic AI "may cause more accidents as it is increasingly applied."
- **Developer:** "Organisations or individuals who design, build, train, adapt or combine AI models and applications."
- **Deployer:** "Any individual or organisation that supplies or uses an AI system to provide a product or service" (internal or external use).
- **End user:** "any intended or actual individual or organisation that consumes an AI-based product or service, interacts with it or is impacted by it after it is deployed." The guardrails do **not** apply directly to end-users.
- **AI lifecycle:** design → data → train (pre-train / fine-tune) → test → integrate → deploy → monitor.

## Context and rationale

The paper argues AI warrants a distinct regulatory response because of eight distinguishing characteristics: autonomy, general cognitive capabilities, adaptability/learning, speed and scale, opacity/lack of explainability ("black box"), high realism, versatility, and ubiquity. It adopts a **risk-based approach with a focus on ex ante (preventative) measures**, invoking the **precautionary principle** used in domains like climate change, food safety, pharmaceuticals and chemicals. It stresses **interoperability** with the EU AI Act, Canada's AIDA, the UK's approach, the Bletchley Declaration, the Hiroshima AI Process Code of Conduct and the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, while preserving local needs — notably **Caring for Country, Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) and Indigenous Data Sovereignty**. The guardrails "would not replace, or exempt AI firms from" existing obligations under the Privacy Act 1988, Copyright Act 1968, Criminal Code Act 1995, Corporations Act 2001, Fair Work Act 2009, Competition and Consumer Act 2010, and administrative law. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (AI Management System) is highlighted as a supporting standard.
