# Does the Whole Exceed its Parts? The Effect of AI Explanations on Complementary Team Performance — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2006.14779-whole-exceed-parts-explanations.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2006.14779-whole-exceed-parts-explanations.pdf) · Gagan Bansal, Tongshuang Wu, Joyce Zhou, Raymond Fok, Besmira Nushi, Ece Kamar, Marco Tulio Ribeiro & Daniel S. ## Abstract

Large-scale CHI 2021 user studies asking whether AI explanations produce **complementary performance** — human–AI team accuracy exceeding both the human alone and the AI alone. The answer, against the field's expectations: AI assistance helped, but explanations added nothing over simply showing the AI's confidence — they mainly made people more likely to accept the AI's recommendation *whether it was right or wrong*.

**Key points**

- **Complementary performance** is defined as team accuracy higher than either the human or the AI working solo; the authors argue a rational developer would only deploy a human-AI team if it adds utility beyond the best solo agent.
- A literature audit (Table 1) shows every prior study claiming explanations improve team performance used an AI far more accurate than the humans — in all eight surveyed studies (deceptive-review detection, income prediction, loan defaults, hypoxemia risk, nutrition prediction, quiz bowl, house prices), the AI alone beat the best human-AI team, so "improvement" meant dragging humans closer to an AI that should have run solo.
- **Design fix:** the authors matched AI accuracy to human accuracy (e.g. AI 84% vs human ~82–87% on sentiment; AI 65% vs human 67% on LSAT) to open a real complementary zone. Three tasks — beer-review sentiment, Amazon book-review sentiment, LSAT logical-reasoning questions — with 1,626 recruited crowd workers, ~100 participants per condition.
- **Result 1:** every team condition (even recommendation + confidence, no explanation) achieved complementary performance — e.g. Beer team 0.89 vs AI 0.84 and human 0.82.
- **Result 2:** no explanation strategy (Explain-Top-1, Explain-Top-2, Adaptive, or even expert-written explanations) significantly beat the plain confidence baseline.
- **Result 3 (the counterweight finding):** explanations increased acceptance of the AI's recommendation *regardless of its correctness* — raising accuracy when the AI was right, lowering it when the AI was wrong, for minimal net change. Explanations bred blind trust, not appropriate reliance.
- Expert-quality explanations improved *perceived* usefulness (e.g. 53% vs 38% rated them useful on Beer) but not actual team performance — preference and performance dissociate.

**Takeaways**

- Explanations that are merely *convincing* calibrate trust in the wrong direction; the goal should be explanations that *inform* — that help the human catch the AI's mistakes rather than find plausible justifications for them.
- Team accuracy, measured end-to-end against both solo baselines, is the honest metric for human-AI collaboration; proxy measures (subjective trust, preference ratings) can mislead.
- Simple, cheap signals — a calibrated confidence score — did all the work in these studies; this is the standing baseline any explanation technique must beat.

## 1 Introduction

- In high-stakes or legally constrained domains, AI cannot act autonomously; the viable pattern is a **human-AI team** where the AI recommends and the human retains agency and accountability (examples: hospital-readmission prediction for doctors, recidivism estimates for judges deciding bail).
- Such teams should achieve **complementary performance** — better than either alone — because from a decision-theoretic standpoint a rational developer deploys a team only if it adds utility.
- The common hope for explainable AI: explanations help the human know *when* to trust the AI and when to be sceptical — **appropriate reliance** (Lee & See 2004).
- The authors' critical observation: prior studies showing explanation benefits all had the AI outperforming both the human *and* the best team — so the team never exceeded the AI alone (Figure 1A). Their study instead controls AI accuracy to be comparable to human accuracy (Figure 1B) and asks whether explanations beat simply displaying confidence.
- Headline contributions: (1) the critique of prior work and the argument that explanations should aim for complementary performance; (2) a new experimental setup used in studies with **1,626 users on three tasks**, observing complementary performance in every teaming condition; (3) the surprise — no significant team-performance gain from explanations over confidence, because explanations raised accuracy when the AI was correct but lowered it when the AI erred.
- First attempt at a fix: **Adaptive Explanation**, which explains only the predicted class when the AI is confident but also explains the alternative when it is not. It did not significantly improve final team performance, though it nudged human-AI agreement in the desired direction.

## 2 Background and Related Work

- Focus: **AI-assisted decision making** — an AI recommends, a human decides — evaluated by end-to-end team accuracy, for three reasons: complementarity is the deployment criterion; proxy tasks (e.g. guessing model predictions) can mislead (Buçinca et al. 2020); and decision support is the most-cited motivation for explanations.
- The audit of prior studies (Table 1 of the paper, condensed; ✗ = no complementary performance in **any** study):

| Task (study) | Metric | Human alone | AI alone | Team | Complementary? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deceptive review (Lai & Tan 2019) | Accuracy | 51.1% | 87.0% | 74.6% | ✗ |
| Deceptive review (Lai et al. 2020) | Accuracy | 54.6% | 86.3% | 74.0% | ✗ |
| Income category (Zhang et al. 2020) | Accuracy | 65% | 75% | 73% | ✗ |
| Loan defaults (Green & Chen 2019) | Norm. Brier | 0 | 1 | 0.682 | ✗ |
| Hypoxemia risk (Lundberg et al. 2018) | AUC | 0.66 | 0.81 | 0.78 | ✗ |
| Nutrition prediction (Buçinca et al. 2020) | Accuracy | 0.46 | 0.75 | 0.74 | ✗ |
| Quiz bowl (Feng & Boyd-Graber 2019) | — | "AI outperforms top trivia players" | | | ✗ |
| House price (Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. 2019) | Avg. abs. error | $331k | $200k | $232k | ✗ |

- Two candidate causes for the missing complementarity: (1) **task design** — with AI much stronger than humans, the complementary zone is tiny and human errors are likely a superset of the AI's; (2) **collaboration mechanisms** — incentives, explanation format, and uncertainty display may push humans to crude heuristics ("always trust the AI" / "never trust the AI").

## 3 Setup and Pilot Studies

### 3.1 Choice of Tasks and Explanations

- Study focuses on **local explanations** (per-recommendation) rather than global model understanding.
- **Tasks:** two sentiment-classification datasets — beer reviews (McAuley et al.) and Amazon book reviews — plus **LSAT logical-reasoning questions** (4-option multiple choice) as a harder task. All are common-sense tasks amenable to crowdsourcing.
- **Explanation display:** inline saliency highlights for sentiment (highlighting influential words directly in the text); narrative natural-language justifications for LSAT (logical constructs can't be conveyed by highlighting).

### 3.2 Pilot Study on Sentiment Classification

- Between-subject pilot on Beer: 50 reviews, logistic-regression classifier, 3 conditions × 50 workers — confidence only, **Explain-Top-1** (highlight words for the predicted class), **Explain-Top-2** (highlight and colour-code words for both classes).
- Pilot findings: (1) no significant team-accuracy change from either explanation type; (2) Explain-Top-1 did better than Explain-Top-2; (3) explanations increased reliance on recommendations *even when incorrect* — helping when the AI was right, hurting when it was wrong; (4) the effect was weaker in Explain-Top-2, presumably because showing the counter-argument deterred over-reliance; (5) participants wanted higher-quality explanations.

### 3.3 Additional Explanation Strategies/Sources

- **Adaptive Explanations** (new strategy): show Explain-Top-2 only when classifier confidence falls below a threshold, Explain-Top-1 otherwise — an assistant that confesses doubts only when unsure.
- **Expert-generated explanations** (new source): human-authored, serving as an upper bound on explanation quality.

## 4 Final Study

### 4.1 Hypotheses, Conditions, and Interface

- Hypotheses: **H1** Explain-Top-1 beats Explain-Top-2; **H2** Explain-Top-1 still only matches showing confidence; **H3** Adaptive explanations improve performance; **H4** Adaptive does even better with higher-quality (expert) explanations. (LSAT used expert explanations only, dropping H4.)
- Baselines: unassisted **Human**, and **Team (Conf)** — AI recommendation + confidence, no explanation. Confidence was chosen as the stronger, broadly acknowledged baseline since model confidence correlates with likelihood of error and displaying uncertainty is known to help decisions.
- Interface: the AI, personified as "Marvin", hovers over its recommended button with a confidence score; explanation highlights are colour-matched to the label buttons (Figure 2). For LSAT, AI information sat in a separate right-hand pane to discourage blind following (Figure 3).

### 4.2 AI Model, Study Samples and Explanations

- **Sentiment:** fine-tuned **RoBERTa** classifiers (AllenNLP); 50 test examples per dataset selected so AI accuracy was **84%**, comparable to measured unassisted human accuracy (87% Beer, 85% Amzbook), with equal false-positive/false-negative rates. Saliency explanations generated with **LIME** (RoBERTa+LIME ranked best in the authors' explainer-comparison study with human judges). RoBERTa's poorly calibrated confidence was fixed with post-hoc isotonic regression. Adaptive thresholds were the median confidence (89.2% Beer, 88.9% Amzbook), splitting examples 25/25 between Explain-Top-1 and Explain-Top-2 displays.
- **LSAT:** RoBERTa fine-tuned on **ReClor** (a logic-reasoning dataset of LSAT/GMAT-style questions); 20 questions from an LSAT prep book (verified not searchable online or in training data). AI accuracy **65%** vs human 67%. Explanations were expert-only (condensed from the prep book for correct answers; authors hand-crafted matching-tone justifications for alternatives). LSAT questions took ~1 minute each vs ~17 seconds for sentiment.

### 4.3 Study Procedure

- Flow: landing page (payment scheme) → screening test (label 4 of 6 correctly; 2-of-2 for LSAT) → main task (random assignment to condition, all 50/20 examples, immediate correctness feedback) → post-task survey.
- **Participants:** Amazon Mechanical Turk, US-based, ≥97% approval, ≥1,000 approved tasks. Recruited **566 (Beer), 552 (Amzbook), 508 (LSAT)** — 1,626 total; 84% passed screening and post-filtering on the sentiment tasks, 35% on LSAT, leaving ~100 participants per condition (93–101). Data filters removed workers with median labelling time under 2 s (3 s for LSAT) or uniform labels.
- **Incentives:** designed so blind AI-following couldn't earn the bonus — base $0.50 plus $0.05 per correct decision and step bonuses ($0.50 above 90%, $1.00 above 95% accuracy on sentiment; since AI accuracy was fixed at 84%, only complementary behaviour paid). Averages: 13 min and $3.35 (≈$15.77/hr) sentiment; 16 min and $6.30 (≈$23.34/hr) LSAT.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Effect of Explanation on Team Performance

Significance via Student's t-tests with Bonferroni correction.

- **Confidence-only teams achieved complementary performance on all three tasks.** Beer: Team (Conf) 0.89 ± 0.05 vs AI 0.84 and human 0.82 ± 0.09; Amzbook and LSAT showed relative gains of 2.2% and 20.1% over unassisted workers.
- **H1 not supported:** Explain-Top-1 vs Explain-Top-2 not significantly different (Beer p=.40, Amzbook p=.42, LSAT p=.68).
- **H2 could not be rejected:** no explanation condition significantly beat confidence-only (e.g. Beer 0.88 ± 0.06 vs 0.89 ± 0.05, p=.24; Amzbook p=.22, LSAT p=.64).
- **H3/H4 not supported:** Adaptive explanations did not beat Team (Conf) (Beer p=.31, Amzbook p=.28, LSAT p=.87), and swapping in expert-written explanations did not help either (Beer p=.19, Amzbook p=.43).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The counterweight finding.** Splitting results by AI correctness (Figure 4B): explanations improved team accuracy when the AI was right but *reduced* it when the AI was wrong — net change minimal. Explanations increased the chance humans accepted the recommendation regardless of correctness. This echoes psychology findings that explanations cause agreement even when wrong (Koehler 1991) and that explanations can seduce data scientists into over-trusting models (Kaur et al. 2020) — but the authors state theirs are, to their knowledge, the first large-scale end-to-end studies to quantify the cost of this over-reliance on objective team performance.

- **Adaptive explanations showed suggestive promise in agreement rates** (Figure 5): they reduced blind agreement when the AI was uncertain, and preserved agreement when it was confident. But this did not translate into higher final accuracy, likely because (1) in high-confidence regions users would need to spot **unknown unknowns** — confidently wrong predictions — which is hard; and (2) in low-confidence regions users had to not only disagree but also solve the task correctly themselves.
- **Expertise overlap matters** (Figure 6): Beer and LSAT had many examples the AI got right but humans found hard (room for AI to add value); Amzbook did not — human and AI errors correlated — so AI assistance helped least there, and participants learned to ignore the AI after seeing obvious mistakes. One curious reversal: on Amzbook, Explain-Top-1 *improved* accuracy by 5% over confidence when the AI was incorrect (it decreased it on Beer and LSAT), plausibly because most AI mistakes there were obvious to humans, who learned to discount the AI despite the convincing effect of explanations.

### 5.2 Survey Responses on Likert Scale Questions

(Post-hoc analysis, no significance claims.)

- **AI usefulness:** majorities found the AI helpful across conditions; expert Adaptive explanations rated highest (Beer 70% vs 57% for Conf; Amzbook 65% vs 49%; LSAT 63% vs 45%).
- **Explanation usefulness:** rated lower than overall AI usefulness; expert Adaptive beat AI-generated (Beer 53% vs 38%, Amzbook 50% vs 40%).

> [!WARNING]
> Higher perceived helpfulness did not translate into better team performance — consistent with Buçinca et al.'s finding that people may *prefer* one explanation style yet *decide better* with another. Subjective ratings are an unreliable guide to what actually helps.

### 5.3 Qualitative Analysis on Collaboration

Coded 409 free-text responses to "Describe how you used the information Marvin provided" (two annotators, mean Cohen's κ = 0.71, 93% raw agreement). The codebook (Table 3):

| Collaboration approach | Share of participants |
|---|---|
| Mostly Follow AI | 6% |
| AI as Prior Guide (looked at prediction first) | 47% |
| AI as Post Check (decided, then verified) | 25% |
| Mostly Ignore AI | 22% |

- Over 70% partially incorporated the AI rather than blindly following or ignoring it. "Prior guide" dominated in sentiment tasks; "post check" in LSAT — matching the LSAT interface that placed AI information apart.
- **Ignoring scaled with error overlap:** only 11% ignored the AI on LSAT vs 23% (Beer) and 30% (Amzbook) — where the AI's mistakes were visible to humans, they learned to discount it.
- **Mental models of confidence:** of the 90 participants who used confidence scores, 27% reported an explicit trust threshold (mean 83 ± 8 Beer, 89 ± 7 Amzbook, 90 ± 0 LSAT; as low as 65). The same proportion used confidence whether or not explanations were shown.
- **Deferring under self-doubt:** 13% said they went with the AI when on the fence — behaviour the authors link to **Truth-Default Theory** (Levine 2014): people default to assuming a communicator is truthful unless suspicion triggers fire.
- **Explanations as accelerators, rarely validators:** 27% (Beer) and 32% (Amzbook) of explanation-users used highlights to read faster; only 17% used explanations to validate the AI's reasoning in sentiment, and just 2% in LSAT — verifying explanations on hard tasks is costly.

## 6 Discussion & Future Directions

### 6.1 Limitations

- Only one performance dimension (decision accuracy); complementarity might be defined multi-dimensionally (speed, effort, trust, engagement).
- Crowdsourceable tasks only — results may not generalise to high-stakes expert domains such as medical diagnosis; user expertise unexplored.
- Only two explanation presentations tested (keyword highlights, natural-language arguments).

### 6.2 Explaining AI for Appropriate Reliance

- The concern: explanations increased **blind trust rather than appropriate reliance**. In domains where humans are kept in the loop for moral or legal reasons, explanations that merely soothe experts into compliance are "deeply unsatisfactory and ethically fraught."
- **Explanations should inform, not just convince.** Expert explanations helping no more than AI ones suggests that optimising for maximally convincing explanations — a common objective in explanation-algorithm design — is the wrong goal for complementary performance. The ideal is explanations that let the user gauge when the AI's reasoning is sound and when it should raise suspicion (positive example: GAM global explanations that let medical experts spot a model's spurious "asthma lowers pneumonia risk" correlation, Caruana et al. 2015).
- Beyond confidence: strategies could adapt to human-AI agreement frequency — e.g. the AI playing **devil's advocate**, explaining its doubts even when it agrees, possibly as an interactive drill-down dialogue rather than static justification.

### 6.3 Rethinking AI's Role in Human-AI Teams

- **Comparable accuracy does not guarantee complementary partners** — the ideal team has minimally overlapping error sets (Amzbook's correlated errors produced the smallest gains). Training could directly optimise for complementarity by modelling the human.
- **Divide labour by talent:** e.g. AI gathers statistics and retrieves documents; humans do evidence-combination and logical reasoning (Feng & Boyd-Graber's Quiz Bowl design).
- **Timing matters:** showing the AI's answer up front risks the **anchoring effect** — people over-weight the first information presented — reflected in the dominance of "AI as Prior Guide" in sentiment tasks. Alternatives: slower AIs, showing the AI's view only after the human's initial answer, explaining only on disagreement, or evidence-only agents that never give hard predictions. But delaying the AI sacrifices speed — a three-way tension between **speed, accuracy, and independence**.

## 7 Conclusions

- Prior explanation-benefit results came only from settings where the solo AI beat every team. With AI and human accuracy matched across three tasks and 1,500+ participants, **all human-AI teams showed complementarity, but no explanation condition significantly beat the confidence-only baseline**.
- Explanations raised accuracy when the AI was correct and lowered it when the AI was wrong — minimal net gain.
- A "call to action" for HCI and AI: characterise when collaboration genuinely helps, develop explanation and coordination strategies that beat the confidence baseline, and communicate explanations to increase *understanding* rather than to persuade.
