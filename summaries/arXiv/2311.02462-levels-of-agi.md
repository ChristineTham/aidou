# Position: Levels of AGI for Operationalizing Progress on the Path to AGI

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2311.02462-levels-of-agi.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2311.02462-levels-of-agi.pdf) — Meredith Ringel Morris, Jascha Sohl-Dickstein, Noah Fiedel, Tris Warkentin, Allan Dafoe, Aleksandra Faust, Clement Farabet & Shane Legg (Google DeepMind), *Position: Levels of AGI for Operationalizing Progress on the Path to AGI*, Proceedings of the 41st International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR 235, 2024 (arXiv:2311.02462; first posted Nov 2023, cited version v5, 24 Sep 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02462
> This is a **frontier-lab position/definitional paper from Google DeepMind** — a proposed framework and taxonomy, not an empirical study. It advances the authors' preferred ontology of AGI; the level assignments of example systems are explicitly described as "approximate" and the paper deliberately proposes no benchmark. Read its claims as a considered definitional argument from a leading industry lab, not as measured results.

## Abstract

The paper proposes a framework for classifying the capabilities of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) systems and their precursors, aiming to give the field "a common language to compare models, assess risks, and measure progress along the path to AGI." Its central move is a **two-dimensional, matrixed taxonomy** — *performance* (depth: how well the system does a task versus humans) crossed with *generality* (breadth: the range of tasks it can do) — yielding six performance levels (0 to 5) across a Narrow and a General column. The authors first analyse nine prior definitions of AGI, distil **six principles** a good definition should satisfy (e.g. focus on capabilities not processes; require both generality *and* performance), and then argue AGI should be understood as a *path* with levels rather than a single endpoint. The single most citable claim is their placement of current systems: as of writing (September 2023), frontier language models such as ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2 and Gemini are classed as **Level 1 General AI ("Emerging AGI")** — while "Competent AGI" (Level 2 General) "has not been achieved by any public systems at the time of writing." Separately, the paper introduces **six Levels of Autonomy** (from "No AI" to "AI as an Agent") that are *unlocked but not determined* by AGI capability, and ties each to specific risks — insisting that "AGI is not necessarily synonymous with autonomy." It matters for a chapter on AI's future because it supplies the field's most widely cited vocabulary for saying *where we are* on the road to AGI and for separating what a system *can* do from how autonomously we *choose* to deploy it.

## Why AGI needs operationalising (Introduction)

AGI has moved "from a subject of philosophical debate, to one which also has near-term practical relevance." The authors note the spread of expert positions — some claim "sparks" of AGI already exist (Bubeck et al., 2023), some predict human-surpassing AI within about a decade (Bengio et al., 2023), and some assert current LLMs already *are* AGIs (Agüera y Arcas & Norvig, 2023). AGI matters because it maps onto three things: **goals** (human-level intelligence as a north-star, from the 1956 Dartmouth conference to mission statements like OpenAI's "ensure that artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity"); **predictions** (progress toward greater generality, plus "emergent" properties, with likely economic implications up to widespread labour substitution and geo-political/military ones); and **risks** (some treat AGI as the point of "extreme risks," speculating about systems that could deceive, accumulate resources, act agentically, displace humans, or recursively self-improve). The stated purpose is that shared, operationalisable definitions of performance, generality and autonomy will support model comparison, risk assessment, clear criteria for policymakers, and communicating "where we are along the path to AGI."

## Nine case-study definitions of AGI (Section 2)

The authors review nine prior formulations and their limits:

1. **The Turing Test** (Turing, 1950) — the imitation game. Modern LLMs pass some framings, so it is "insufficient." Whether a machine can *think* is "orthogonal to the question of what the machine can do"; the latter is easier to measure and matters more for impact. Motivates defining AGI by *capabilities rather than processes*.
2. **Strong AI / consciousness** (Searle, 1980) — the computer "really is a mind." Process-oriented; there is no scientific consensus on detecting consciousness (Butlin et al., 2023), making it "impractical."
3. **Analogies to the human brain** (Gubrud, 1997 — the term's original 1997 use, in a military-technology article) — AI that rivals/surpasses the brain "in complexity and speed." The success of transformers (not reliant on human-like learning) suggests brain-based processes are "not inherently necessary."
4. **Human-level performance on cognitive tasks** (Legg, 2008; Goertzel, 2014) — doing the cognitive tasks people typically do; notably focuses on *non-physical* tasks. Leaves ambiguity ("what tasks?", "which people?").
5. **Ability to learn tasks** (Shanahan, 2015) — not specialised, "can learn to perform as broad a range of tasks as a human." Introduces *metacognition* (learning) as a requirement.
6. **Economically valuable work** (OpenAI charter, 2018) — "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work." Strong on capabilities-not-processes and offers a yardstick, but may miss non-economic intelligence (artistic creativity, emotional intelligence) and implies *deployment* to realise value.
7. **Flexible and general — the "Coffee Test"** (Marcus, 2022) — intelligence "flexible and general, with resourcefulness and reliability comparable to (or beyond) human." Captures both generality and performance; proposes five gauge tasks (understand a movie, understand a novel, cook in an arbitrary kitchen, write a bug-free 10,000-line program, convert NL maths proofs to symbolic form). The cooking task (a variant of Wozniak's "Coffee Test") requires robotic embodiment.
8. **Artificial Capable Intelligence (ACI)** (Suleyman & Bhaskar, 2023) — enough performance/generality for "complex, multi-step tasks in the open world." His "Modern Turing Test": turn $100,000 of capital into $1,000,000 over several months. Narrower than OpenAI's definition and risks pure-profit misalignment, but valuably emphasises complex real-world tasks with more *ecological validity*.
9. **SOTA LLMs as generalists** (Agüera y Arcas & Norvig, 2023) — mid-2023 GPT-4, Bard, Llama 2 and Claude "already are AGIs" because they are highly general. The authors agree generality is crucial but insist it must be *paired with performance* ("if an LLM can write code or perform math, but is not reliably correct, then its generality is not yet sufficiently performant").

## The six principles a good AGI definition should satisfy (Section 3)

1. **Focus on Capabilities, not Processes** — what an AGI *can accomplish*, not the mechanism. AGI systems need not think/understand in a human-like way, nor possess *consciousness* or *sentience* (both process-focused). (Footnote: advances in mechanistic interpretability may later enable process-oriented metrics.)
2. **Focus on Generality *and* Performance** — both are key; some prior definitions emphasise generality but omit performance.
3. **Focus on Cognitive and Metacognitive, but not Physical, Tasks** — robotic embodiment increases generality but is *not* a necessary prerequisite (physical capabilities lag non-physical ones). By contrast, **metacognitive capabilities** — learning new tasks, knowing when to ask for clarification/help — are "key prerequisites" for generality.
4. **Focus on Potential, not Deployment** — being *capable* of a task set at a given performance level suffices to declare AGI; open-world deployment should not be part of the definition (deployment adds legal, social, ethical and safety hurdles).
5. **Focus on Ecological Validity** — benchmark on tasks that align with real-world tasks people value (economic, social, artistic), possibly "eschewing traditional AI metrics that are easy to automate or quantify."
6. **Focus on the Path to AGI, not a Single Endpoint** — by analogy with the SAE Levels of Driving Automation, define *Levels of AGI*, each tied to metrics, identified risks, and Human-AI Interaction paradigms. This lets many prior definitions coexist: Agüera y Arcas & Norvig ≈ "Emerging AGI"; OpenAI's labour-replacement threshold ≈ "Exceptional AGI"; "Competent AGI" is "the best catch-all" for the Legg, Shanahan and Suleyman formulations.

## The Levels of AGI taxonomy: performance × generality (Section 4, Table 1)

Two dimensions:

- **Performance** = *depth* — how the system compares to human-level performance on a task. Above "Emerging," percentiles refer to "a sample of adults who possess the relevant skill."
- **Generality** = *breadth* — the range of tasks for which the system reaches a target performance threshold.

The matrix crosses six performance rows (Levels 0–5) with two generality columns — **Narrow** (a clearly scoped task or task set) and **General** (a wide range of non-physical tasks, including metacognitive tasks like learning new skills):

| Level | Performance definition | Narrow AI (examples) | General AI (examples) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0: No AI** | — | Narrow Non-AI: calculator software; compiler | General Non-AI: human-in-the-loop computing, e.g. Amazon Mechanical Turk |
| **1: Emerging** | equal to or somewhat better than an unskilled human | Emerging Narrow AI: GOFAI; simple rule-based systems, e.g. SHRDLU | **Emerging AGI**: ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2, Gemini |
| **2: Competent** | at least 50th percentile of skilled adults | Competent Narrow AI: toxicity detectors (Jigsaw); smart speakers (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant); VQA (PaLI); Watson; SOTA LLMs *for a subset of tasks* | **Competent AGI**: not yet achieved |
| **3: Expert** | at least 90th percentile of skilled adults | Expert Narrow AI: Grammarly; image models Imagen, DALL·E 2 | **Expert AGI**: not yet achieved |
| **4: Exceptional** | at least 99th percentile of skilled adults | Exceptional Narrow AI: Deep Blue, AlphaGo | **Exceptional AGI**: not yet achieved |
| **5: Superhuman** | outperforms 100% of humans | Superhuman Narrow AI: AlphaFold, AlphaZero, Stockfish | **Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)**: not yet achieved |

The taxonomy specifies the *minimum* performance over *most* tasks needed for a rating — e.g. a Competent AGI must be ≥50th percentile of skilled adults on most cognitive tasks, but may reach Expert, Exceptional or even Superhuman on a subset. A general system rated at level *N* may still perform a narrow subset of tasks at higher levels.

*(Terminology note: Level 4 General was originally named "Virtuoso AGI"; the cited version renames it "Exceptional AGI" (Shah et al., 2025), said to better capture the level.)*

**Placement of current systems.** As of September 2023, frontier LLMs (ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2) show "Competent" performance on *some* tasks (short-essay writing, simple coding) but "Emerging" performance on *most* (mathematical ability, factuality). They are therefore **Level 1 General AI ("Emerging AGI")** overall, and would only reach "Competent AGI" once performance rises across a broader task set. The authors recommend model cards document this uneven mixture of performance levels. "Competent AGI" — not achieved by any public system at the time of writing — "best corresponds to many prior conceptions of AGI, and may precipitate rapid societal change once achieved."

**Caveats they raise.** The order of skill acquisition may matter for safety (e.g. strong chemical-engineering knowledge before strong ethical reasoning "may be a dangerous combination"); progression between levels may be nonlinear, and acquiring the ability to learn new skills may accelerate it. *Capable* performance may not match *deployed* performance: DALL·E 2 is estimated as Level 3 Narrow ("Expert") on output quality, but in practice may be only "Competent" because prompting interfaces are too complex for most users. ASI (Level 5 General) implies systems doing a wide range of tasks better than any human, and possibly *non-human* skills (decoding thoughts from brain signals, oracular prediction, communicating with animals) that "by definition outperform all humans."

## Testing for AGI (Section 5)

The paper deliberately proposes **no benchmark**, instead clarifying what one should measure and what properties it needs. Principles 2 and 6 shaped the matrixed ontology; Principles 1, 3, 4 and 5 bear on measurement. The performance dimension gives percentile ranges, but generality leaves open questions: which set of tasks counts, what proportion must be mastered, and whether some tasks (e.g. metacognitive) are mandatory. An AGI benchmark should span cognitive and metacognitive tasks — linguistic intelligence, mathematical/logical reasoning, spatial reasoning, interpersonal and intra-personal social intelligence, ability to learn new skills, and creativity. The authors stress three metacognitive tasks in particular: **(1) the ability to learn new skills; (2) the ability to know when to ask for help** (including awareness of one's own limits, i.e. *model calibration*); **(3) social metacognition** such as theory of mind. The benchmark should favour open-ended, interactive tasks (higher ecological validity than traditional metrics) and be a **"living benchmark"** with a framework for adding new tasks. Declaring a system *not* an AGI at a level only requires finding tasks people can do that it cannot; the authors decline to fix a pass threshold (e.g. "90% of tasks"), expecting a high but not 100% bar, since "broad but imperfect generality is impactful" (humans too lack consistent performance across all tasks).

## Risk, Autonomy, and Interaction (Section 6, Table 2)

**Levels of AGI as a risk framework.** As capability rises toward ASI, new risks appear — misuse, alignment, and structural (Zwetsloot & Dafoe, 2019). "Expert AGI" is likely to bring structural risks of economic disruption and job displacement as more industries cross the substitution threshold, while also *reducing* some earlier risks (e.g. incorrect task execution). "Exceptional AGI" and "ASI" are where existential-risk concerns are most likely to emerge (e.g. a system outperforming its operators could deceive them to achieve a mis-specified goal). Below "Expert AGI," risks "stem more from human actions" (accidental, incidental or malicious misuse). The authors lean toward *including* dual-use dangerous capabilities in benchmarks but de-risking via Principle 4 (test potential in sandboxes, not deployment). They note Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (v1.0), a levels-based approach inspired by biosafety levels, which then classed SOTA generative AI as ASL-2 risk.

**Capabilities vs. Autonomy.** The key separation: increasing capabilities "unlock new interaction paradigms, but *do not determine them*." The authors propose **six Levels of Autonomy**, framed (unlike prior computer-centric automation taxonomies) through the *style of human-AI interaction*:

| Autonomy Level | Description | Example systems | Unlocking AGI level(s) | Example risks introduced |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0: No AI** | human does everything | analogue/non-AI digital workflows (pencil, text editor) | No AI | status-quo risks |
| **1: AI as a Tool** | human fully controls the task, AI automates mundane sub-tasks | search engine; grammar checker; machine-translation app | Possible: Emerging Narrow AI; Likely: Competent Narrow AI | de-skilling (over-reliance); disruption of established industries |
| **2: AI as a Consultant** | AI takes a substantive role, but only when invoked | LLM document summarisation; code generation; recommender systems | Possible: Competent Narrow AI; Likely: Expert Narrow AI, Emerging AGI | over-trust; radicalisation; targeted manipulation |
| **3: AI as a Collaborator** | co-equal human-AI collaboration; interactive coordination of goals and tasks | training via a chess AI; entertainment with AI-generated personalities | Possible: Emerging AGI; Likely: Expert Narrow AI, Competent AGI | anthropomorphisation (parasocial relationships); rapid societal change |
| **4: AI as an Expert** | AI drives interaction; human provides guidance/feedback or does sub-tasks | AI advancing scientific discovery (e.g. protein-folding) | Possible: Exceptional Narrow AI; Likely: Expert AGI | societal-scale ennui; mass labour displacement; decline of human exceptionalism |
| **5: AI as an Agent** | fully autonomous AI | autonomous AI personal assistants (not yet unlocked) | Likely: Exceptional AGI, ASI | misalignment; concentration of power |

Autonomy levels correlate with — and are "unlocked" by — AGI levels, but the appropriate autonomy for a task "need not be the maximum achievable." Lower autonomy may be preferred even at high capability, for education, enjoyment, assessment or safety (the paper's analogy: keeping Level-0 manual driving available even when Level-5 self-driving exists). Because General systems develop unevenly, they may unlock higher autonomy on their specific strengths, and paradigms tend to require *higher* performance for Narrow than for General systems. Autonomy Levels 3–5 depend on strong *metacognitive* abilities (knowing when to ask for help, theory of mind, social-emotional skills); Level 5 implies an AI that can act in an aligned way "without continuous human oversight, but knows when to consult humans." The authors frame model-building research as pushing capability along the path to AGI, and human-AI interaction research as ensuring systems are *usable* and *useful* — i.e. "intelligence augmentation."

## Conclusion

The paper analyses nine AGI definitions, distils six principles (capabilities not processes; generality *and* performance; cognitive and metacognitive not physical; potential not deployment; ecological validity; path not endpoint), and from them builds the Levels of AGI ontology — generality (Narrow or General) crossed with five performance levels (Emerging, Competent, Expert, Exceptional, Superhuman). It argues for a living, ecologically valid benchmark, and stresses that **AGI is not synonymous with autonomy**: the separate Levels of Autonomy are unlocked but not determined by AGI progression, so jointly considering AGI Level and Autonomy Level gives more nuanced risk assessment. The authors intend the framework to be adaptable — it may change with advances such as interpretability, and its interaction paradigms and risks may evolve as society adapts.

## Limitations the authors state

- It is a **position paper proposing an ontology**, not an empirical evaluation; assignments of example systems to cells are "approximate," and "unambiguous classification" will require a standardised benchmark they do not provide.
- They decline to specify the number/percentage of benchmark tasks a system must pass to earn a level ("Determining what portion of benchmarking tasks at a given level demonstrate generality remains an open research question").
- The generality dimension leaves unresolved which tasks count and whether some (e.g. metacognitive) are mandatory.
- Deployed performance can diverge from capability because of interface limits (the DALL·E 2 example).
- The capabilities-not-processes stance is provisional: mechanistic interpretability may later justify process-oriented metrics and reshape the definition.
