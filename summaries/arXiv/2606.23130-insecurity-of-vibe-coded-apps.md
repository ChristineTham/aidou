# Understanding the (In)Security of Vibe-Coded Applications

> Source: arXiv:2606.23130 (2026-06-22). Deng, Fan & Meng. PDF in `../../sources/arXiv/2606.23130-insecurity-of-vibe-coded-apps.pdf`.

## Thesis
This is the first large-scale empirical study of the security of real-world "vibe-coded" applications — apps built almost entirely by AI coding agents (e.g. Claude Code, Lovable) from natural-language prompts, with minimal human implementation or review. The authors mine GitHub for agent "fingerprints", build a corpus of 10,517 vibe-coded apps (VIBEAPPS), and audit a random sample of 200 deployed web apps using a multi-agent auditing pipeline plus human validation, yielding 1,471 confirmed vulnerabilities (VIBEVULNS). They find such apps are pervasively and severely insecure, and that the failures stem from systematic weaknesses in the vibe-coding workflow — not just one-off code-generation slips — so stronger models and better prompts reduce but do not eliminate the risk.

## Key findings
- 90% of the 200 audited deployed apps contained at least one vulnerability; median 7 per vulnerable repo, mean 8.1 (p6, RQ2).
- Of the 1,471 vulnerabilities, 20% were Critical and 56.7% High — 76.7% Critical-or-High (p6-7). Just three OWASP categories (Broken Access Control, Cryptographic Failures, Injection) account for 74.4% of them.
- Vibe-coded apps show substantially higher vulnerability rates than the OWASP Top-10 (2025) human-led baseline "across nearly every category, with differences up to 20x" (p7-8).
- Root causes cluster into three workflow "defect" classes: knowledge defects 734 vulns (49.9%), objective defects 450 (30.6%), memory defects 287 (19.5%) — spanning specification, implementation, iteration, and deployment (p8, Fig 3).
- In a 360-run controlled replay, the baseline agent reintroduced the original vulnerability 40% of the time. Mitigations helped modestly: a "production-ready" prompt cut the trigger rate 27%, "self-check" 22%, a hardened security skill 18%; a bigger model (Opus) only 7%, and a smaller model (Haiku) 0% (p11-12, Table 4). A detailed "professional" prompt made it *worse* (+15.6%).
- Awareness–action gap: in 32% of runs that shipped a vulnerability, the agent had explicitly recognised the security risk (often noting it in a comment) yet still produced insecure code (p12-13).
- Scale/speed profile: median app is 8,351 LoC over 101 files built in a median 9.8 days; 94.5% are web apps; ~12% are publicly deployed — implying little human hardening before release (p5).

## Relevance to the book
This is the empirical, peer-review-style counterweight Chapter 3 needs to balance Ahuja's opinion-driven vibe-vs-SDD framing. It gives hard numbers for why unstructured vibe coding is risky at deployment scale, and — crucially — its diagnosis is not "AI writes bad code" but "the *workflow* lacks the requirements, review, and release gates that human-led development uses." That directly supports the case for spec-driven / intent-driven development as risk mitigation, while its finding that prompts and even Opus-class models only partially close the gap complicates any "better models will fix it" optimism. The awareness–action gap and the three-defect taxonomy (memory/objective/knowledge) are ready-made structure for discussing where AI-native engineering discipline must intervene.

## Stance
empirical-vibe / vibe-critical — an evidence-based, measurement-first study (not an opinion piece) whose data lands critically on unsupervised vibe coding: it documents pervasive, severe, systematic insecurity and argues the fix is workflow-level discipline rather than model scaling.
