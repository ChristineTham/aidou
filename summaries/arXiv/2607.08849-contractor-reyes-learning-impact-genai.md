## Abstract

A proctored, in-person randomized controlled trial (211 undergraduates at Middlebury College, Spring 2025) testing whether access to off-the-shelf generative AI (ChatGPT/GPT-4o) helps or harms learning, measured on *unaided* assessments taken immediately and again about one week later. AI access raised immediate knowledge-test scores by 0.27 SD, and roughly three-quarters of that gain persisted a week later — but the effect on higher-order skills (essay quality) depended sharply on *how* students used AI.

**Key points**

- **Immediate gain:** AI access raised immediate knowledge-test scores by **6.7 pp** (control mean 56.3%), a **0.27 SD** intent-to-treat effect (p = 0.034); treatment-on-the-treated **10.0 pp / 0.40 SD**. Gains concentrated in the *middle* of the score distribution.
- **Persistence:** One week later, unaided, AI-allowed students still scored **5.1 pp** higher (p = 0.027), **≈76%** of the immediate effect (also ≈0.27 SD). Knowledge gains are durable though partly decaying.
- **Augmentation vs automation (the central nuance):** Of AI users, **49% were "augmentation" users** (AI works *with* the student — mainly explaining concepts) and **32% "automation" users** (AI does the work). In Session One, automation users' *essay-quality* advantage was large (**0.54 SD**, p = 0.029) vs augmentation's near-zero (0.05 SD). Once AI was removed (Session Two), automation's essay-quality gain **faded to ≈0** (0.02 SD) and its test-score effect attenuated to an insignificant 0.19 SD, while augmentation users **retained** essay-quality (0.22 SD) and test-score gains (**0.29 SD**, p = 0.061).
- **Essay quality overall** was largely unchanged while students had AI, but *improved* when they later wrote unaided: writing style & clarity **+0.30 SD** (p = 0.016) and relevance to prompt **+0.26 SD** (p = 0.041) in Session Two.
- **Mechanisms:** total learning time unchanged, but time shifted **away from writing (−5.3 pp)** and **toward reading/searching (+4.4 pp)**; enjoyment rose **13%** (+0.66 on 0–10, p = 0.031). AI access also raised test-rule violations (combined +12.6 pp), but these account for at most ~a third of the score gain.

**Takeaways**

- This is careful causal evidence that off-the-shelf AI *can* build durable human capital, not just boost immediate output — nuancing the "AI harms learning" narrative. The decisive moderator is augment-vs-automate: scaffolding cognitive effort produces lasting gains; outsourcing it produces gains that vanish once the AI is gone.
- The authors explicitly do *not* claim widespread AI adoption will raise learning overall — their design holds time-on-task fixed. Whether AI helps in the wild depends on how students reallocate saved time and on the incentives (grade inflation, signalling) that push them toward automation.

## Introduction

Generative AI is now pervasive in higher education (ChatGPT: 900M+ weekly users; college-age users send nearly half its messages; >25% of messages relate to learning), yet evidence on whether it *helps students learn* is scarce — most evidence comes from workplace settings measuring job performance, not human-capital accumulation. AI could help (on-demand tutoring, personalized feedback) or hurt (offloading the cognitive effort learning requires). The paper studies a common academic task: a time-bounded assignment where students must learn unfamiliar material and demonstrate understanding through writing and tests.

**Design in brief:** 211 undergraduates, two in-person sessions ~1 week apart. Session One randomly assigns students to *AI-allowed* (may use any generative AI plus traditional resources) or *AI-forbidden* (traditional resources only); compliance monitored via proctors, ChatGPT logs, and self-reports. Session Two: all students take a test and write an essay **without AI or any resources**, measuring retention.

**Two learning dimensions:** knowledge tests (factual + conceptual recall) and open-ended essays (higher-order analysis, graded by humans and an LLM), plus objective linguistic features (length, readability, lexical diversity, textual similarity, and fraction flagged as AI-generated).

**Headline findings previewed:** ~68% of AI-allowed students used AI; 88% found it helpful; most common use was explaining concepts (57% in logs), then drafting (31%) and summarizing (17%). AI access raised immediate test scores 0.27 SD, persisting a week later; essay-quality gains surfaced only after AI was removed and were driven by augmentation users.

**Contribution vs prior literature.** Two problems with existing evidence: (1) many studies test *scaffolded/restricted* AI (teacher-designed hints, metacognitive prompts, tutoring-only modes) rather than the raw chatbot students actually use; (2) some compare AI against an active alternative rather than a business-as-usual counterfactual. This study uses off-the-shelf AI against a no-AI control. It also adds: measuring knowledge *persistence*; distinguishing augmentation from automation; tracing how AI shifts learning *inputs*; assessing multiple learning dimensions; and comparing students' *beliefs* to estimated effects. Related literatures: digital tech in education (laptops, online courses, internet — generally mixed/limited effects; computer-assisted learning does better); AI productivity (large task-performance gains, but few studies speak to skill accumulation — Brynjolfsson et al. 2025 find AI aids learning for support agents, while Budzyń et al. 2025 and Shen & Tamkin 2026 find deskilling).

## Experimental Design

Two in-person sessions ~1 week apart, administered through Qualtrics (tracks progress, enforces time limits, records time-on-component).

### Session One (60-minute fixed structure)

Between-subjects design; eight time slots, two parallel labs each, one lab per arm, students randomized to a specific computer with privacy dividers.

| Component | Detail |
|---|---|
| Welcome & instructions | Condition-specific script; phones away; comprehension checks (mean 4.77/5 correct) |
| Topic assignment + baseline test | One of three topics — **blockchain, carbon capture, CRISPR** — chosen for rich factual content and low prior knowledge; 5-question baseline MCQ (no aids) |
| Learning phase | Up to **35 minutes**; learn the topic and write a ~500-word analytical essay; students told to use their normal approach; given an intro reading (1,253–1,399 words) |
| Post-learning survey | Self-assessed knowledge (0–10), time allocation, enjoyment/effectiveness, attention check |
| Post-learning test | 5 MCQs (differ from baseline), **no aids regardless of condition** |

**Treatment:** AI-allowed students got explicit oral + written permission and a pre-logged-in **ChatGPT (GPT-4o)** account, plus Wikipedia and library tabs. AI-forbidden students were explicitly prohibited and given Google, Wikipedia, library tabs.

**Compliance monitoring:** two proctors per lab with seating charts; exit-survey self-reports of which tools used; periodic capture of writing interfaces to detect pasted text blocks.

**Incentives:** $5 for Session One alone, **$50 for completing both**; performance-based lottery tickets (1 per correct answer, 1 per essay quality point); 30 tickets drawn at $100 each.

### Session Two (~45 minutes, mean gap 6.99 days)

All students work **without AI or resources**. Two assessments in randomized order: a **10-item** MCQ test (distinct questions) and a 20-minute analytical essay (the complementary prompt). Concludes with a questionnaire on: beliefs about AI's impact on own/others' scores; self-reported AI skills and usage history; retrospective Session One behaviour (which tools, for what purpose); and an open-ended question on AI's effect on learning. Students were *not* told in advance about Session Two, and there is no evidence of between-session studying (only 5% looked anything up).

## Data and Research Design

- **Sample:** Middlebury College, Spring 2025, where AI adoption already exceeded 80%. 256 signed up, **211 (82.4%) attended Session One**, 204 (79.7%) attended both. Study framed as "a study on learning" (AI never mentioned in recruitment) to limit selection.
- **Typical student:** 20.1 years, 35.1% male, 49.8% white, 26.5% international; mean GPA 3.68, mean SAT 1386. Low baseline knowledge (1.5/10 self-assessed; 30.3% baseline MCQ correct).
- **Balance:** balanced on observables (only age significant at 10%; joint F-test p = 0.173); GPA and SAT near-identical across arms. No differential attendance/attrition. Residual imbalance handled with double-lasso control selection.
- **Model:** OLS of outcome on an AI-allowed indicator plus randomization-strata fixed effects and lasso-selected controls; heteroskedasticity-robust SEs. β is the **intent-to-treat (ITT)** effect of being *allowed* AI (policy-relevant, since institutions can permit but not force use). A **treatment-on-the-treated (TOT)** 2SLS analysis instruments actual use with assignment (LATE for compliers).
- **Outcomes:** AI usage (first stage); test-based (self-assessed knowledge 0–10; fraction correct; standardized scores; threshold indicators); essay-based (graded 0–10 by 311 Prolific graders holding a master's/PhD, 3–4 blind graders per essay, plus an LLM grader; a quality index averaging five dimensions); objective linguistic features; textual similarity (cosine, following Brynjolfsson et al. 2025); and **Pangram** AI-content detection and plagiarism flags.

## Immediate Effects of AI Access on Learning (Session One)

### First stage — AI adoption and usage patterns

- Assignment to AI-allowed raised AI use by **67.3 pp** (activity in the provided ChatGPT account, p < 0.001) from a near-zero control baseline; self-reported any-AI use +59.9 pp. Adoption was almost entirely ChatGPT (+60.2 pp) vs other models (+1.9 pp, ns).
- **88% of users** found AI somewhat/very helpful.
- Usage purposes (self-report / logs): **explaining concepts most common (46% / 57%)**, then drafting responses and summarizing readings. Logs show more drafting/editing than self-reports — students **underreport text-generation uses**.

### Effects on test scores

- **No effect on self-assessed knowledge** (ITT 0.02, ns): both arms' self-rating jumped from ~1.56 to ~4.79.
- **Test performance responds:** control fraction-correct rose 30.6% → 56.3%; AI access adds **6.7 pp** (a 12% improvement), i.e. **0.27 SD** (p = 0.034). TOT = **10.0 pp / 0.40 SD** (p = 0.036). Robust across the three topics and to dropping attention/comprehension failures.
- **Where gains land:** concentrated in the middle of the distribution (8.0 pp more likely to reach ≥40% correct, 9.6 pp for ≥60%; little at 80%/perfect). **Which students gain:** larger in upper GPA/SAT quartiles than the bottom — AI access **may widen learning gaps**.

**Benchmarking:** 0.27 SD is more than double Kraft's (2020) median educational-RCT effect of 0.10 SD (across 747 RCTs); comparable to the 0.29 SD pooled gain from structured human tutoring (Nickow et al. 2024), but not supply-constrained. Roughly the effect of a one-SD GPA increase, or ~$8,600/student of school spending over four years. A meta-analysis of 22 estimates from 13 RCTs gives a random-effects grand mean of **0.18 SD**; losses cluster where AI can do the practice for the learner (e.g. base GPT-4, −0.19 SD in Bastani et al. 2025), gains where AI acts as a coach.

### Effects on essay-based outcomes (Session One)

Session One essays blend three channels (direct AI output, changed approach, real learning), so they are ambiguous — the learning channel is isolated in Session Two.

- **AI detection:** fraction of text flagged AI-generated rose **12.3 pp** (p = 0.019), a 96% increase over the 12.8% control mean; TOT 18.3 pp. No plagiarism effect.
- **Linguistic features:** essays trend longer (+0.13 SD length, ~18 more tokens) and easier to read (+0.06 SD; Flesch Reading Ease +4.2, sentence length −4.7 words), small lexical-diversity change — all statistically insignificant.
- **No homogenization:** within-group cosine similarity essentially unchanged (contrary to Brynjolfsson et al. 2025's convergence among support agents), likely because essay prompts are open-ended.
- **Quality largely unchanged** in Session One: all five dimensions positive but imprecise (organization +0.17 SD, accuracy +0.17 SD, style +0.15 SD; overall +0.17 SD, p = 0.245).

### Mechanisms — how AI transforms the "production function" of learning

| Mechanism | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Total time** | No change (control ~32.6 min Qualtrics / 34.3 min self-report; treatment ≈0) — contrast with workplace time savings (Noy & Zhang 2023: −0.80 SD) |
| **Time allocation** | Shift *away from writing* (−5.3 pp of time, from 53.4%, p = 0.028) and *toward research* (reading/searching +4.4 pp, from 44.5%, p = 0.050) — effort moves from execution to stewardship |
| **Learning experience** | Enjoyment +0.66 on 0–10 (**+13%**, p = 0.031); above-median enjoyment +14.1 pp; perceived effectiveness small |
| **Academic integrity** | Rule violations rise: proctor-observed +5.7 pp, self-reported +8.6 pp, **combined +12.6 pp** (p = 0.005, from 4.9%). Back-of-envelope: cheating explains at most ~a third (~2.2 pp) of the score gain |

## Learning Retention and Heterogeneity (Session Two)

### Persistence of test-score effects

Both arms decline in Session Two (skill depreciation), but treated stay ahead: control fraction-correct 56.3% → 49.5%; **ITT +5.1 pp** (p = 0.027), **≈76%** of the Session One effect (≈0.27 SD). Gains again sit in the middle of the distribution (+12.2 pp for ≥60% correct). Durable, partly decaying, knowledge gains. (Consistent with the two other retention studies: Lira et al. 2025 at 0.41 SD; Kazemitabaar et al. 2023 at 0.41 SD.)

### Persistence of essay effects

- **AI traces collapse:** AI-detection effect falls from 12.3 pp to ~0.0 pp (p = 0.991) — the Session One style differences were AI text entering essays, not durable changes.
- **Quality *improves* unaided:** writing style & clarity **+0.30 SD** (p = 0.016) and relevance to prompt **+0.26 SD** (p = 0.041) both significant; overall +0.20 SD (imprecise). AI access improved higher-order learning, not just fact recall.

### Automation versus Augmentation (the key heterogeneity)

Framework (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell 2017; Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019): **augmentation** = AI works *with* the student (raises the productivity of effort); **automation** = AI does the work (reduces cognitive effort). An LLM labelled each ChatGPT log: **49% pure augmentation, 32% pure automation, 8% mixed, 11% other.**

Validation: automation users prompt more for drafting/editing; augmentation users for explaining. Automation users spend ~17% less time on research and ~8% less overall. Pangram flags **53.6%** of automation users' text as AI-generated vs **20.9%** for augmentation users.

| Outcome | Automation users | Augmentation users |
|---|---|---|
| Session One essay quality | **+0.54 SD** (p = 0.029) — AI writing, not learning | +0.05 SD (near zero) |
| Session One test score | +0.33 SD | +0.44 SD |
| **Session Two essay quality (unaided)** | **≈0** (0.02 SD overall; 0.04 SD index) — **fades entirely** | +0.22 SD (p = 0.216) — **retained** |
| **Session Two test score (unaided)** | 0.19 SD (p = 0.330, ns) — attenuates | **0.29 SD** (p = 0.061) — persists |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Automation's Session One advantage reflects AI-produced output and does not survive AI's removal; augmentation's gains persist unaided, consistent with real skill accumulation. The short-term-output-vs-learning tradeoff seen elsewhere (Bastani et al. 2025; Strömberg et al. 2026; Shen & Tamkin 2026) is **not inevitable** — it depends on whether students scaffold or substitute their cognitive effort.

### Other heterogeneity

Gains larger in upper GPA/SAT quartiles (bottom quartile ~0.05–0.06 SD; SAT peak 0.40 SD in Q3) — may widen gaps vs the lowest-ability students. Neither prior AI experience nor most demographics moderate the effect; a suggestive gender difference (women +0.46 SD more than men on Session One tests, p = 0.079).

## Beliefs About AI's Impact on Learning

- **Magnitude beliefs:** Both arms think AI helps, but only *treated* students (who used it) gauge the size correctly. Actual ITT ≈ 5.1 pp. Control students predict a **25.2 pp** own-gain (~5× actual); treated predict **3.8 pp** (close to estimate). Beliefs track actual gains across subgroups.
- **Mental models:** Coding open-ended responses as causal graphs (following Andre et al. 2026): **69%** of students naming a channel name *both* a helping and a harming one; **54%** say the effect depends on *how* AI is used ("a double-edged sword"); the two most-named mechanisms are AI **explaining/tutoring** (augmentation, 40%) and AI **shortcutting the work** (automation, 42%). Students grasp the mechanisms from the outset but only judge magnitudes accurately after using AI. Higher self-assessed proficiency predicts framing AI as augmentation.

## Conclusion

Causal, proof-of-concept evidence that off-the-shelf AI *can* improve learning, with gains that on average persist a week, concentrate in the middle of the distribution, shift effort from drafting to reading/searching, and raise enjoyment — but whose durability depends on augment-vs-automate use. The authors caution this does **not** imply widespread adoption will raise learning overall: their estimates hold time-on-task fixed (the lab offered few competing uses of time), and in real academic work students use AI to *save* time; whether learning rises depends on how saved time is reallocated. Implications for incentive design: grade inflation and signalling motives push students toward automation; aligning incentives toward learning is a promising research avenue.

## Relation to the book

This is a high-value source for **§6.5 (AI and learning)** and it directly *nuances* the "AI harms learning" storyline the section otherwise risks over-stating. It is a rare **proctored, randomized** study with a clean no-AI counterfactual and an unaided one-week retention test, so it can carry real evidentiary weight: off-the-shelf AI raised immediate knowledge-test scores by 0.27 SD and roughly three-quarters persisted a week later. The decisive, book-worthy distinction is **augmentation vs automation** — using AI to *explain concepts* built durable gains, whereas using it to *generate text* produced a Session One essay-quality bump (0.54 SD) that vanished once AI was removed. That maps cleanly onto the book's broader augment-vs-automate through-line and lets §6.5 say the harm-to-learning finding is conditional on *how* the tool is used, not intrinsic. Pair it with the deskilling-side evidence (e.g. the "productive struggle" erosion in Yu & Moon 2026 for §6.4) as the contrasting case: this paper shows the same technology can build or erode human capital depending on use and incentives. Note for citing: preprint (arXiv July 2026), so flag it as a not-yet-peer-reviewed working paper; the authors themselves caution against extrapolating to aggregate learning because time-on-task was held fixed.
