# Human Oversight and Overload: Two Hidden and Costly Burdens of AI-Assisted Software Engineering — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2606.05770-oversight-and-overload.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2606.05770-oversight-and-overload.pdf) · Vahid Garousi (rendered "Vəhid Gəruslu" on the title page; Queen's University Belfast, UK, and Azerbaijan Technical University), 2026 · arXiv preprint, <https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05770>. Written in the style of a practitioner-facing magazine column; no venue is printed on the paper.
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording and figures.

## Abstract

An 8-page structured synthesis — deliberately positioned between an opinion piece and a research article — arguing that AI-assisted software engineering carries two hidden, costly human burdens: the **oversight burden** (the cumulative effort to review, validate, repair, and integrate AI-generated artifacts) and **cognitive overload** (the mental strain of evaluating, filtering, and iteratively refining floods of AI suggestions). Either burden can erode or cancel the productivity gains teams hope for.

**Key points**

- **Evidence base:** a "rapid review" of **eight sources identified in summer 2025** — seven practitioner grey-literature pieces (blogs, LinkedIn posts, a Stack Overflow blog post) and one academic paper (a Brain Sciences neurophysiological study). The author calls these "experience-based opinions" and explicitly disclaims statistical generalisability.
- **Oversight burden** was the most frequently discussed hidden cost across the sources. Quoted practitioners: "the bottleneck has shifted from writing code to reviewing AI-generated code" and "every AI suggestion must be read with suspicion until proven correct". Benchmarks like SWE-Bench+ show even top LLMs rarely produce production-ready artifacts without repair; several sources note oversight "may sometimes consume more effort than the effort saved by using AI".
- **Oversight scales with tool type and artifact complexity:** inline completions (Copilot-style) are quickly reviewed or discarded; chat-based tools produce long, complex outputs (whole classes, test suites) demanding deeper inspection and carrying higher risk if accepted unchecked. Oversight cost "grows non-linearly with scope ambiguity and artifact size".
- **Cognitive overload** manifests as increased decision density (more micro-decisions per task), frequent context switching between prompting and reviewing, and sustained evaluation of superficially-correct alternatives — effort shifts from construction to continuous assessment, which "can extend task completion time even when initial code generation appears fast". The cited neurophysiological study found repeated cycles of evaluating and adapting AI outputs "increase mental fatigue and reduce creative capacity" even when suggestions are technically sound. Grey literature calls the phenomenon **AI fatigue**.
- **The core structural tension:** AI accelerates artifact *generation*, but *validation* remains bounded by human review capacity. When the marginal effort to review and integrate AI output exceeds the marginal time saved by generation, net productivity turns negative. Figures 1–2 illustrate this conceptually (explicitly not empirical measurements).
- **Mitigations:** eight pragmatic heuristics from three ongoing academia–industry projects (UK, Türkiye, Azerbaijan) — e.g. scope-constrained prompting, explicit review budgeting, AI-code tagging, senior review for architecture/security-critical output, capped prompt iterations, separating drafting from reviewing, controlling suggestion volume, rotating AI-intensive tasks.

**Takeaways**

- The paper offers **no direct quantitative measurement** of oversight cost — its evidence is converging practitioner testimony, one benchmark citation (SWE-Bench+), one field study, and one lab study; it proposes future proxies (prompt-iteration counts, review time per artifact, discard frequency) precisely because these are not yet instrumented.
- Agent-assisted engineering consumes attention through a *generation–validation* loop: usage patterns, not adoption alone, determine whether AI yields net gains. Well-scoped prompts keep the loop manageable; broad, vague prompts amplify both burdens.
- Organisationally, engineers shift from primary creators to **validators and curators**: juniors become prompt operators and first-pass validators, seniors spend proportionally more time on architectural review and systemic validation — work is redistributed upward, not eliminated. Global coherence of large codebases remains a human responsibility even as AI accelerates local code production.

## 1 Introduction

- Positions itself as neither pure opinion nor conventional research: a **structured synthesis** blending grey-literature practitioner evidence with the author's own recent AI-in-SE projects, aimed at "practical sense-making".
- Two burdens in scope: (1) the **constant, non-optional need for human oversight** of AI-generated artifacts (review, validate, rework), and (2) **cognitive overload** from the "barrage" of completions, refactorings, and alternatives — fatigue "not easily captured by productivity metrics".
- Other hidden costs (onboarding effort, energy use, security, licensing, organisational change) are acknowledged but explicitly out of scope.
- The contribution is not to "discover" the burdens but to clarify their operational meaning, articulate how they **interact as structural forces** (not isolated frustrations), and frame them within a productivity trade-off and production-shift perspective.
- Caveat the author states up front: the topic is formative; rigorous longitudinal or large-scale empirical studies remain limited, so the column highlights patterns and conceptual risks warranting further investigation rather than statistically generalisable claims.

## 3 The Oversight Burden

> [!NOTE]
> **Definition — oversight burden:** "the cumulative effort required to review, validate, repair, and integrate AI-generated artifacts into production-quality systems" — including inspection time, debugging of subtle errors, architectural alignment, and the cognitive effort of verifying correctness.

- The **most frequently discussed** hidden cost across the reviewed sources.
- Practitioner quotes: "the bottleneck has shifted from writing code to reviewing AI-generated code" [2]; "every AI suggestion must be read with suspicion until proven correct" [1].
- **Benchmark evidence:** SWE-Bench+ (Aleithan et al.) demonstrates that even top-performing LLMs rarely produce production-ready SE artifacts without repair.
- Multiple practitioner reports [1, 2, 5–7] note oversight **may sometimes consume more effort than the effort saved** by using AI; plausible-looking but flawed code can create **false confidence**, increasing later testing and debugging costs [3, 6].
- Tooling (e.g. Sonar AI Code Assurance) can assist inspection but **supplements, not replaces, human judgement**.
- **Two scaling factors:**
  - *Type of AI support* — inline completion (Copilot) yields short, quickly-reviewed suggestions; chat-based tools (ChatGPT) produce long, complex outputs (entire classes, test suites) demanding deeper inspection with higher risk if accepted unchecked [7].
  - *Artifact complexity* — boilerplate needs minimal verification; code/tests for complex business logic can require multiple review iterations and rework (citing the author's own field study, Garousi & Jafarov 2024, arXiv:2507.17930).
- **Figure 1** (conceptual, not a workflow model): a green arrow shows AI as a potential accelerator across SDLC phases; two red arrows show the hidden burdens arising *after* generation — (1) oversight/inspection/correction effort and (2) cognitive overload — contrasting acceleration with validation effort.

## 4 Information and Cognitive Overload

> [!NOTE]
> **Definition — cognitive overload:** "the mental strain created by evaluating, filtering, and iteratively refining AI-generated suggestions". Oversight burden concerns *quality assurance* of AI artifacts; cognitive overload concerns the *mental cost of processing* them.

- **Field-study evidence** (the author's own [12]): engineers prompting for complex artifacts often received overly general or inconsistent results, requiring **iterative re-prompting** to reach a "good-enough" solution — each iteration adding context switching and mental fatigue. Echoed by practitioners [7, 8].
- **Concrete example** from [12]: requesting a data-validation layer with multiple edge cases returned long outputs with alternative implementations, optional configurations, and extra helper methods. The cognitive effort lay "not in writing code from scratch, but in evaluating and pruning multiple plausible options" against architectural constraints, naming conventions, and performance requirements.
- **Experimental evidence** [8]: overload can trigger **measurable frustration and reduced creative output even when AI suggestions are technically sound**; repeated evaluate-and-adapt cycles "increase mental fatigue and reduce creative capacity".
- **"AI fatigue"** in grey literature: Khare — early gains give way to "workload creep, cognitive fatigue, burnout, and weakened decision-making", where "the productivity surge enjoyed at the beginning can give way to lower quality work"; similar concerns in Business Insider and Harvard Business Review (AI may *intensify* work rather than reduce it).
- **Concrete manifestations:** increased **decision density** (more micro-decisions per task), frequent **context switching** between prompting and reviewing, and sustained evaluation of alternatives that all appear superficially correct. Unlike traditional programming (effort in construction), AI-assisted workflows shift effort to continuous assessment and comparison — which "can extend task completion time even when initial code generation appears fast".
- **Team restructuring implication:** engineers shift from primary creators to **validators and curators**; juniors increasingly act as prompt operators and first-pass validators; seniors spend proportionally more time on architectural review, integration consistency, and systemic validation. "This shift does not eliminate work; it redistributes it upward toward higher-level reasoning and cross-module coherence."
- Grounded in established cognitive science on working-memory limits and decision fatigue (Sarkar & Parnin 2017): more alternatives and micro-decisions can **amplify** rather than reduce cognitive load.
- **Proposed measurement proxies** (not yet instrumented): number of prompt iterations, review time per AI-generated artifact, and frequency of discarded suggestions.

## 5 Balancing Efficiency Gains Against Hidden Burdens

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The paper's central claim: net productivity impact hinges on the balance between efficiency gains and the two hidden costs. AI primarily accelerates *generation*, while oversight and cognition remain bounded by human review capacity — "when the marginal effort required to review and integrate AI outputs exceeds the marginal time saved by generation, net productivity can decline". AI thus introduces "a structural tension between generation speed and validation capacity".

- When hidden costs are low — careful task selection, mature use (proper prompt engineering), disciplined integration — AI yields measurable gains; as oversight and mental load grow, net gains shrink and may turn negative.
- **Figure 2** (explicitly a *conceptual illustration*, not temporal progression or empirical measurement): X-axis = hidden cost (oversight/inspection/fixing effort + overload losses); Y-axis = productivity gain (positive or negative); "Low–Medium–High" labels are illustrative categories. Two hypothetical curves: **effective usage** (hidden costs stay low, gains stay positive) vs **ineffective usage** (hidden costs rise and erode or reverse benefits). Identical AI assistance can produce sustained gains or long-term degradation — **usage patterns, not adoption alone, shape overall impact**.
- Reframed as a **shifting automation–validation boundary**: AI expands the automation frontier, but validation is constrained by human cognitive limits and architectural reasoning. Validation effort "does not disappear; it relocates and often scales with artifact complexity". The key analytical question is whether the combined **generation–validation cycle** produces net value.
- **Delegation limits:** well-scoped components and boilerplate may be largely automated, but complex system integration, architectural decisions, and cross-cutting concerns (security, compliance) still need sustained human judgement. In million-line codebases, "consistency cannot be assumed merely because code compiles; it must be deliberately governed" — AI accelerates local production, but **global coherence remains a human responsibility**. The boundary is not fixed: future models with better contextual reasoning and long-horizon planning may assist system-level thinking, yet governance, accountability, and responsibility for system integrity "are likely to remain human-led, particularly in safety-critical or high-stakes domains".

## 6 Pragmatic Heuristics to Manage Oversight and Overload

Drawn from ongoing industry collaborations and multiple AI-assisted development projects — "experience-informed patterns", not a formal framework (Table 2):

| Managing human oversight effort | Managing cognitive overload |
|---|---|
| **Scope-constrained prompting** — limit AI requests to well-bounded tasks, not entire modules/subsystems | **Cap prompt iterations** — limit refinement cycles before switching to manual adjustments |
| **Explicit review budgeting** — fixed time windows for reviewing AI output, preventing hidden review creep | **Separate drafting from reviewing** — generate first, review in a dedicated pass, don't mix |
| **AI-generated code tagging** — mark AI artifacts for traceability and structured review | **Control suggestion volume** — reduce/disable continuous suggestions during complex design or architectural work |
| **Senior review** — required for AI-generated code affecting architecture or security-critical components | **Rotate AI-intensive tasks** — alternate with non-AI tasks to reduce mental fatigue |

## 7 Concrete Prompt Examples: Low-Burden vs. High-Burden AI Use

- **Low-burden prompts** (clear, scoped, easily verifiable outputs): e.g. "Generate a JUnit test for the method processPayment(amount, currency)… ensuring it throws an exception if the currency is unsupported"; a SQL query against a named table with named fields; a JUnit test for calculateDiscount with enumerated customer types.
- **High-burden prompts** (broad, vague, underspecified): "Write a complete backend for a shopping cart system in Java"; "Generate all the tests we need for a large-scale banking application"; "Optimize this system for performance, scalability, and security".
- Key pattern: **"oversight cost grows non-linearly with scope ambiguity and artifact size."** Well-scoped prompts constrain output variability and review effort, keeping the generation–validation loop manageable; large ambiguous prompts shift oversight from spot-checking to architectural reasoning and raise decision density across intertwined design choices.

## 8 Toward a Pragmatic Lightweight Framework for Effective and Sustainable AI Use in SE

- The heuristics are being applied and refined in **three joint academia–industry projects (UK, Türkiye, Azerbaijan)**, converging toward a lightweight framework at two levels:
  - **Individual level:** scope-constrained prompting, explicit review budgeting, capped iteration cycles; separating generation from evaluation reduced context switching; limiting suggestion density during architecture-intensive tasks lowered cognitive strain.
  - **Team level:** AI-artifact tagging for traceability, senior-review thresholds for architecture-sensitive components, sprint retrospectives that include reflection on AI-related review effort, and experiments with architectural consistency checks so AI-generated modules align with design principles and cross-cutting constraints.
- Described as "burden-aware AI integration" — not yet formalised, but a structured way to maximise efficiency gains while containing oversight and overload.

## 9 Conclusions and Implications

- Either burden "can erode, or even cancel out, the productivity gains many teams hope to achieve with AI".
- **Forward-looking organisational shifts:** as AI-assisted development scales, teams may invest proportionally more in oversight, architectural governance, and validation; quality, cross-codebase consistency, and trust in AI-generated components become central concerns. Ecosystem-level risks named: **architectural monoculture, shared model biases, and systemic vulnerability propagation** as AI-generated artifacts accumulate across organisations.
- The convergence of eight independently authored sources across organisations and contexts suggests the burdens are "emerging systemic concerns", not isolated anecdotes.
- Scope reminders: Figures 1–2 are analytical visualisations, not empirically calibrated models; onboarding, prompt-framing skill, and defining "right" use are acknowledged adjacent human factors outside scope; the focus is not efficiency per se but the two burdens that determine whether efficiency gains are realised or lost.
- **Practitioner message:** (1) track the time and energy spent reviewing and repairing AI outputs; (2) pay attention to the mental strain of processing constant AI suggestions; (3) share positive *and* negative experiences openly. Empirical validation at scale remains the community's next step, but the burdens "deserve proactive attention before they become entrenched organizational risks".
- Closing line: "Handled thoughtfully, AI can boost productivity. Handled without awareness of these hidden costs, it risks becoming a liability instead of a help."

> [!WARNING]
> **Limitations the paper itself states:** the evidence base is eight sources (seven grey-literature opinion pieces plus one lab study), selected via rapid review in summer 2025; the paper presents no original quantitative measurements of oversight cost, and both figures are explicitly conceptual. Treat its claims as converging practitioner signal and structured interpretation, not measured effect sizes.
