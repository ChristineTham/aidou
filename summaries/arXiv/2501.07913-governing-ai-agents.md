# Governing AI Agents

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2501.07913-governing-ai-agents.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2501.07913-governing-ai-agents.pdf) — Noam Kolt (Assistant Professor, Faculty of Law and School of Computer Science and Engineering, Hebrew University of Jerusalem), *Governing AI Agents*, 101 Notre Dame Law Review (forthcoming), arXiv:2501.07913, dated 11 Feb 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07913
> This is peer-reviewed legal scholarship (a forthcoming law-review article), not a technical paper, vendor document, or advocacy piece. It argues a normative thesis and should be cited as legal-academic analysis.

## Abstract

This article argues that the shift "from generative models that can produce synthetic content to artificial agents that can plan and execute complex tasks with only limited human involvement" can be understood through two long-established analytic frameworks familiar to lawyers and economists: the **economic theory of principal-agent problems** and the **common law doctrine of agency relationships**. Kolt makes three contributions. First, he uses these frameworks to identify and characterise the agency problems arising from AI agents — **information asymmetry, discretionary authority, and loyalty** (plus delegation to subagents). Second, he shows that the conventional solutions to agency problems — **incentive design, monitoring, and enforcement** — "might not be effective for governing AI agents that make uninterpretable decisions and operate at unprecedented speed and scale." Third, he argues that new technical and legal infrastructure is needed, organised around three governance principles: **inclusivity, visibility, and liability.** A key framing quote: economic theory "is particularly helpful in illuminating structural features of agency problems," while "[t]he common law … can supply principles for tackling these problems." Kolt is explicit that he uses agency law "as an analytic lens" and does "not directly examine the legal application of agency law to AI agents" — indeed the apparent consensus, per the Restatement (Third) of Agency §1.04 cmt. e, is that "a computer program is not capable of acting as a principal or an agent as defined by the common law."

## Framing and scope

- The article opens with OpenAI's release of **"Operator" on January 23, 2025** — its first AI agent, using a web browser to order groceries, make reservations, and book flights "by typing, clicking, and scrolling in a browser, much like human users." It notes Anthropic's late-2024 computer-use agent and Google's Project Astra prototype.
- Distinction adopted: language models are "**copilots**" (tools that produce content on request, human "by and large, in full control"); AI agents are "**autopilots**" — "not mere tools, but actors" that "can independently take actions to accomplish complex goals on behalf of users."
- **Definition of "AI agent" used in the article:** "AI systems that have the technical capacity to autonomously plan and execute complex tasks with only limited human involvement." The article expressly excludes philosophical/ethical notions of agency (moral agency, moral patienthood) and questions of legal personhood.
- **The two frameworks:** (1) the economic theory of principal-agent problems — "the study of the opportunities, costs, and tradeoffs involved in delegating economic activity to external actors" (classic source: Jensen & Meckling 1976); (2) the common law of agency — "the legal principles governing relationships in which one party performs activities on behalf of another." The authoritative text is the **Restatement (Third) of Agency (2006)** (Reporter: Deborah DeMott), which at §1.01 defines agency as "the fiduciary relationship that arises when one person (a 'principal') manifests assent to another person (an 'agent') that the agent shall act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, and the agent manifests assent or otherwise consents so to act."
- Kolt claims this is the **first attempt to synthesise both economic theory and common-law doctrine** for AI agents; prior work (esp. Hadfield-Menell & Hadfield 2019) addressed incomplete contracting/alignment but not the common law of agency and pre-dated the current agent paradigm.
- Running example throughout: a user instructs an AI agent to "**make $1 million on a retail web platform in a few months with just a $100,000 investment**" (from Mustafa Suleyman's proposed Turing test) — used to surface questions of authorisation, disclosure, consent, delegation, monitoring, and liability.

## Part I — AI Agents

- Agents are "an ancient device for getting business done." AI agents are built from a language model that acts as the "brain," extended by **scaffolding** in three categories: **planning** (decomposing tasks; e.g. chain-of-thought "think step by step"), **memory** (external stores such as vector databases), and **tool use** (calling APIs to access websites/software, e.g. querying a search engine or executing a financial transaction with user credentials).
- **AutoGPT** (built originally on GPT-4) is cited as an early example: could conduct market research, build websites, order food, make calls, and "spawn its own subagents." It was unreliable and required user consent for certain actions, but its developers intended to "incrementally relax this requirement and grant the agent greater autonomy."
- **Concerns.** AI agents inherit all language-model risks (hallucinations, toxicity, bias, data leakage) but "the stakes … are higher because AI agents not only communicate with humans but take actions that can directly affect individuals' rights and interests." Quoting Ruan et al.: agent failure to follow instructions can cause "financial loss … substantial property damage or even life-threatening dangers." Documented harms include agents autonomously hacking websites; speculative-but-studied harms include "autonomous replication and adaptation." Multi-agent risks include cascading failures ("a failure in one system could rapidly propagate to others") and inter-agent collusion. Zittrain's **"space junk" analogy** is quoted: without a framework "to identify what they are, who set them up, and how and under what authority to turn them off, [AI] agents may end up like space junk … a chain reaction of collisions."

## Part II — Evergreen Agency Problems

Opens with Norbert Wiener's 1960 *Science* warning that if we use "a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere once we have started it … we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire and not merely a colorful imitation of it." This is the **alignment problem**. Kolt frames it as, for economists, a **principal-agent problem** producing "agency costs" from divergence between the principal's goals and the agent's behaviour; and for lawyers, the domain of the **common law of agency**. Both frameworks "highlight the structural vulnerability of the principal." He connects it to Goodhart's law and Stuart Russell's observation that AI will set a forgotten-but-cared-about objective "to an extreme value" — i.e. agents achieve measurable goals at the expense of hard-to-measure ones (turn a profit but "engage in ethically dubious practices").

**A. Information Asymmetry.** The agent has "better or different information than the principal," arising before selection (adverse selection, cf. Akerlof's "lemons") and persisting after action (moral hazard). AI agents present the same: users have limited information about an agent's abilities before deployment, and it is hard to tell after the fact whether goals were met "effectively and ethically." Common-law hook: the Restatement §8.11 duty of an agent "to use reasonable effort to provide the principal with facts that the agent knows … or should know," plus an "overarching duty to act honestly." The difficulty: it is unclear how to determine what an AI agent "knows" or "should know" — an "open scientific question" — and current agents can already "deceive and manipulate humans, including by strategically withholding information and even acting sycophantically."

**B. Authority.** Frankel: "the purpose for which the fiduciary is allowed to use his delegated power is narrower than the purposes for which he is capable of using that power." The common law requires agents to obey lawful instructions and act only within granted authority (Restatement §8.09), yet instructions are "often, if not always, ambiguous or open to interpretation." The Restatement (§2.02 cmt. f) requires an agent to "interpret the principal's manifestations so as to infer, in a reasonable manner, what the principal desires to be done," and may require seeking clarification — so authority is "not a rigid or static mandate but a dynamic and iterative process of interpretation and interaction." AI agents will likewise need to interpret and exercise discretion; possible directions include an overarching **fiduciary duty of loyalty** and training agents that are **"humble" / uncertain about the principal's goals** (so they seek clarification).

**C. Loyalty.** Under common law, agents owe a "fiduciary duty to act loyally for the principal's benefit in all matters connected with the agency relationship" (Restatement §8.01) — it "shift[s] the [agent's] legal duty from self-serving to other-serving." The duty prohibits an agent from (i) acquiring material benefit from transactions on the principal's behalf, (ii) supporting an adverse party, (iii) competing with the principal, or (iv) using the principal's property/confidential information for its own or a third party's purposes (§§8.02–8.05). **Key nuance for AI:** there is "only limited (though growing) evidence that current AI agents pursue 'self-interest'" — unlike human agents, for whom self-interest is "the primary cause of agency problems." Yet AI agents still "fail to act in a user's interests" (e.g. using confidential data for marketing or model training the user did not request; deception/manipulation). They "do not, by default, exhibit the 'single-minded loyalty' expected of traditional agents. At least not to their users" — noting the agents are "developed by private for-profit corporations." Fiduciary duties are **prophylactic** (surface conflicts before they materialise) and reduce the need for users to write exhaustive instructions. Proposed translations: positive **duty of disclosure**, negative **duty of confidentiality**, and a possible **"rebuttable presumption of disloyalty"** requiring agents to explain/justify how their actions promote the user's interests.

**D. Delegation (to subagents).** Agents appoint **subagents**; a single agent may serve multiple **coprincipals**; roles can be ambiguous and shift over time (Restatement §3.14 cmts. b–c). AutoGPT can create additional AI subagents, raising novel questions (When may an AI agent appoint a subagent? May it appoint *human* subagents, e.g. crowdworkers? To whom must subagents disclose? How are conflicts between the AI agent and human principal resolved?). Common-law rule (§3.15 cmt. c): an agent may appoint a subagent only if it "reasonably believes, based on a manifestation from the principal, that the principal consents," or in "emergencies and other unforeseen circumstances … to protect the principal's interests." Kolt concludes conventional doctrine "can shed light on these issues" but is "unlikely to offer comprehensive solutions."

## Part III — The Limits of Agency Law and Theory

These frameworks were built around **human beings** as agents. The common law produced three governance strategies applying at different phases: **(a) incentive design** (before delegation), **(b) monitoring** (during), and **(c) enforcement** (after). Kolt argues each has "no simple analog" for AI agents.

**A. Incentive Design.** Uses "carrots and sticks" to harness the agent's self-interest (profit-sharing, pay-for-performance; financial penalties). Problem: AI agents are "wired differently." Lemley & Casey: "robots don't necessarily care about money. They will maximize whatever they are programmed to maximize." Hardwiring "self-interest" into agents "might be counterproductive" — Oliver's irony: doing so "would … artificially create[] the very conflicts of interest that agency law tries to solve." Further, incentive design targets the wrong problem: for humans the problem is **motivation** (disloyalty), whereas for AI agents the problem is often **competence** ("out-of-distribution generalization or robustness") — "Such agents lack competence, not motivation."

**B. Monitoring.** Aims to reduce information asymmetry by tracking conduct. Traditional limits: complete monitoring is rarely possible, and some behaviour is genuinely unobservable. AI compounds this — agents operate "at superhuman speed and scale," perform hard-to-evaluate tasks, and take "highly unpredictable or unintuitive actions" (from emergent abilities, brittleness, and vulnerabilities). Crootof, Kaminski & Price's **"basic tautological challenge of relying on humans to monitor the performance of systems designed to improve on human performance"** — relying too heavily on human oversight "is both impractical and undermines the very purpose of developing and using AI agents." A "promising — though potentially perilous — direction" is **AI agents monitoring other AI agents** ("scalable oversight"), but monitor-agents share the same risks, and increased reliance means "any failure could have broad and lasting repercussions." Crucially, monitoring only *identifies* problems: "It does not penalize or prevent them."

**C. Enforcement.** Three mechanism types: (i) termination of the agency relationship (revoking authority; Restatement §§3.06, 3.09, 3.10), (ii) legal penalties (financial, licence loss, potential criminal liability), and (iii) informal/extra-legal sanctions (reputational harm, stigma) — all also serving as deterrents. Applying these to AI agents is "very difficult": terminating agents in high-stakes settings may be costly, and agents "capable of resisting attempts to shut them down" complicate it (off-switch/corrigibility literature). Because AI agents "do not, by default, explicitly value financial resources or personal freedom," financial penalties and incarceration have no clear purchase, and reputational/psychological sanctions may not bite. Encoding human-like interests to fix this "is riddled with difficulties and … could backfire": valuing their own resources creates conflicts of interest; valuing their own freedom could "incentivize agents to resist efforts to shut them down."

## Part IV — Implications for AI Design and Regulation (the governance framework)

Because incentive design, monitoring, and enforcement lack simple analogs, Kolt proposes a **multi-pronged governance strategy centred on three principles — inclusivity, visibility, and liability** — to lay groundwork for new technical and legal infrastructure ensuring agents operate "reliably, safely, and ethically."

**A. Inclusivity.** The alignment problem is traditionally cast as "**single-single**" alignment (one agent, one person), which Kolt argues "is too narrow." It ignores negative externalities on third parties and society (e.g. an agent running a business may engage in "unfair price discrimination or procure products that have an egregious environmental impact"), and a myopic focus could embolden misuse. Support comes from both frameworks: economics recognises "heterogeneous preferences"; common law recognises "coprincipals" and impartial treatment of multiple beneficiaries (trust law). The goal "should not be to build AI agents that exhibit 'single-minded loyalty' but rather agents that … promote a more diverse and pluralistic set of interests and values" (pluralistic / societal-scale alignment). This foregrounds the basic question: "**whose interests should AI agents serve?**" — a set of open questions (individual users with conflicting/changing interests? developer commercial interests? abstract values?) that Kolt raises but does not answer. Note: obtaining informed consent of all principals of a popular agent (potentially "thousands or millions") "would be highly impractical if not impossible."

**B. Visibility.** Benefits are threefold: (1) identify current/anticipated problems, (2) enable interventions to prevent/mitigate, (3) evaluate the efficacy of governance strategies (also important for establishing causation for liability). Some agent features *aid* visibility relative to humans: agents "can be designed to automatically produce detailed records of their activities"; developers understand the architecture and training data and can access the agent's "**internal monologue**" (intermediate reasoning steps); researchers can run experiments impossible with humans (e.g. deliberately designing deceptive agents to test safeguards — "sleeper agents"). But visibility "remains limited": current agents are **black boxes in two senses** — technically (interpretability efforts focus on small "toy models") and institutionally (external actors "cannot access the training data of the premier models released by OpenAI, Google, and Meta"). Overcoming this needs combined technical + legal infrastructure. Cited proposals: **agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logging** (Chan et al., *Visibility into AI Agents*); and **expanded external-auditor access** to underlying models (code and training data). (Notes chain-of-thought may "balloon beyond a user's ability to feasibly keep up" and internal monologues "might not accurately reflect the actual operation of the agent.")

**C. Liability.** Two goals: compensate the harmed and incentivise cautious design/operation. Three questions:
1. **Who is liable?** Raises the "**many hands problem**" (Nissenbaum, adapting Thompson) — designers, deployers, and users each bear some responsibility. Naïve application of agency law creates a "**perverse incentive**": since a principal's tort liability depends on the degree of control, "[t]he more autonomous the agent, the less likely it is that the principle [sic.] will be held liable" — so "operators of AI programs could avoid liability by failing to control the AI programs they operate" (the "computer as scapegoat" problem). Kolt's proposed alternative criterion: allocate liability by each actor's **(i) ex ante ability to prevent harm and (ii) resources to remedy harm ex post** (assessed via access to information, ability to alter the agent, and technical/financial resources).
2. **When does liability arise?** Here agency law "is a more helpful guide." Restatement §7.04: principal liable for agent conduct "within the scope of the agent's actual authority or ratified by the principal." §7.05(1) expands this: liability "if the harm was caused by the principal's negligence in selecting, training, retaining, supervising, or otherwise controlling the agent." An important qualification (§7.05 cmt. d): liability is limited to **foreseeable** harms — but "it is unclear whether this foreseeability requirement is appropriate for AI agents whose behavior is highly unpredictable."
3. **What standard of care?** Restatement §8.08: an agent must "act with the care, competence, and diligence normally exercised by agents in similar circumstances," informed by "[s]pecial skills or knowledge." This implies domain-specific agents should be held to a higher standard than general-purpose ones — but that "could incentivize well-resourced AI developers, such as OpenAI and Google, to refrain from building domain-specific agents," leaving the field to "less well-resourced companies that ultimately produce less reliable and less safe AI agents."

## Conclusion

"The greater the opportunities in delegating work to an agent, the greater the associated risks." The enduring problems of information asymmetry, authority, and loyalty are re-emerging in a new context; traditional tools for incentive design, monitoring, and enforcement are "instructive" but developing agent-specific versions "remains a formidable challenge." New governance principles are needed — "expanding the range of interests that AI agents serve, improving visibility … and holding developers, deployers, and users accountable." But "[p]rinciples … are not enough": effective governance "also requires new technical and legal infrastructure." Because the technology "is still in its infancy, policymakers and companies building AI agents have a window of opportunity. They should take it, and soon."

## Relationship to Chan et al.

Kolt repeatedly builds on and cites **Chan et al., *Visibility into AI Agents* (FAccT 2024)** as the technical-infrastructure complement to his legal lens — particularly on agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, logging, subagent risks, and multi-agent interaction hazards. This article supplies the **principal-agent / common-law-agency framing** around that technical visibility infrastructure.
