## Abstract

Across five experiments (N = 3,132; four preregistered, one direct replication), merely having access to AI advice — engineered to be almost always **wrong** — nearly eliminated participants' willingness to say "I don't know" when answering hard trivia questions they were free to decline. People answered more, were correct about a third as often, yet were nearly twice as confident. Rewarding accuracy and penalising error reduced reliance on AI and improved accuracy somewhat, but never restored abstention to the AI-free level.

**Key points**

- **N = 3,132 across five experiments** (Studies 1a, 1b, 2, 3, 4; four preregistered, one direct replication). Participants answered six deliberately obscure movie-detail questions and could always decline ("judgment suspension").
- **The advice was engineered to be wrong** (LLM: StepFun's Step 3.5 Flash), deliberately separating AI *use* from AI *accuracy* — so any drop in abstention is deference, not sensible delegation.
- **Suspension collapsed under AI access:** 0.36 → 0.06 (Study 1a) and 0.44 → 0.03 (Study 1b) — baseline vs. AI condition, both p < .001.
- **Accuracy fell to about a third** while **confidence nearly doubled:** pooled correctness 27.5% (no AI) vs. 9.2% (AI); mean confidence 29.6 vs. 75.9 (no-stakes, Study 2).
- **Incentives did not fix it:** rewarding correct answers ($0.10) and penalising errors (−$0.10) reduced AI use and lifted accuracy, but abstention stayed far below the AI-free level. The preregistered AI × stakes interaction on suspension was non-significant in all three incentivised studies (p = .274, .506, .784).
- The effect held whether advice was **requested** (Studies 1–3) or **displayed automatically/unsolicited** (Study 4).

**Takeaways**

- AI does not just change *what* people answer; it lowers the **metacognitive threshold** at which they decide they know enough to answer at all — eroding the willingness to abstain under uncertainty.
- What AI changes is **willingness, not capacity**: incentives partly re-engage judgment, so abstention is a contextual response, not a fixed limit. But the pull to defer is strong enough to survive modest incentives.

## Introduction

Human judgment involves not only choosing an answer but deciding whether one knows enough to answer at all — a metacognitive act. The capacity to suspend judgment ("I don't know") protects against acting on false beliefs and is a precondition for calibrated reasoning. LLMs answer almost anything fluently yet are reluctant to admit uncertainty. The paper's question: does the mere *availability* of an AI answer change whether people answer under uncertainty?

The nearest prior work is Shaw and Nave's (2026) "cognitive surrender" — participants who consulted an erring AI became less accurate yet more confident, and neither incentives nor time pressure removed the pattern. But that work measured correctness, not *whether people withheld judgment at all*. This paper studies precisely the willingness to suspend judgment.

**Removing the rationality confound by design.** Following AI advice is usually rational when the advice is good, making any decline in independent judgment hard to interpret. The authors remove this by using questions about fine visual details in films (e.g. the colour of the team's uniform in *Bend It Like Beckham*; the vehicle Monica drives in *Like a Cat on a Highway*) — details minor enough to be largely absent from online text and therefore likely to induce hallucinations. The LLM used (Step 3.5 Flash) answered almost all incorrectly; state-of-the-art models (GPT-5.5, Claude 4.6 Sonnet, Gemini 3.5 Flash) all failed the hardest question. Because the advice was usually wrong, reduced suspension reveals deference rather than delegation.

## Results

### Studies 1a–1b: mere access nearly eliminates suspension

| Study | N | Baseline suspension | AI-condition suspension | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1a (live LLM) | 314 | 0.36 | 0.06 | t = 8.295, p < .001 |
| 1b (pre-generated wrong answers) | 310 | 0.44 | 0.03 | t = 11.772, p < .001 |

Study 1b pre-generated three incorrect responses per question (drawn from Study 1a outputs) to rule out live-query failures (~10% of requests failed in 1a). The replication confirmed the effect. Mere possibility of consulting AI nearly eliminated willingness to suspend judgment — before any incentives or unsolicited advice.

### Study 2 (N = 812): crossing AI access with monetary stakes

A 2 (AI: absent/present) × 2 (stakes: absent/present) design. In stake conditions: +$0.10 correct, −$0.10 incorrect, $0 for suspending. Confidence also measured.

- **Suspension:** AI access nearly eliminated it both without stakes (0.17 → 0.01) and with stakes (0.21 → 0.02), both p < .001. Stakes did not significantly raise suspension. The preregistered negative **AI × stakes interaction was not significant** (b = −0.034, p = .312).
- **Confidence:** AI advice **nearly doubled** reported confidence (29.6 → 75.9 without stakes; 37.7 → 73.5 with stakes). A significant stakes × AI interaction (p = .006): stakes raised confidence when AI was absent, but not when AI was available.
- **Correctness:** AI access lowered correctness; stakes raised it both with AI (0.10 → 0.17) and without (0.28 → 0.33).
- **Behaviour:** participants sought AI advice less when stakes were present (4.93 vs. 5.44 requests out of six; p = .002) — a plausible route by which incentives improved accuracy without restoring suspension.

### Study 3 (N = 843): salient, per-question incentives

Addressed two limits of Study 2 (dropped confidence elicitation; reminded participants of stakes before every question).

- AI access again reduced suspension: 0.32 → 0.02 (no stakes) and 0.41 → 0.08 (stakes), both p < .001.
- Unlike Study 2, stakes here **did** significantly raise suspension (both with and without AI), but the **AI × stakes interaction remained non-significant** (p = .506) — stakes and AI acted independently.
- Correctness: significant stakes × AI interaction; incentives improved correctness specifically when AI was available (0.11 → 0.16).
- Fewer AI requests under stakes (4.53 vs. 5.27; p < .001).

### Study 4 (N = 853): unsolicited AI advice

Identical to Study 3 except AI advice appeared **automatically** rather than on request (mirroring search summaries, writing assistants, autocomplete).

- AI access still dramatically reduced suspension: 0.35 → 0.01 (no stakes), 0.39 → 0.07 (stakes), both p < .001.
- **AI × stakes interaction again non-significant** (p = .784).
- Correctness: significant interaction; stakes raised correctness only when AI was available (0.07 → 0.14).

Across Studies 2–4 the preregistered negative interaction was never supported (p = .274, .506, .784). AI sharply reduced suspension regardless of stakes; stakes modestly raised suspension regardless of AI. The one place they interacted was **accuracy**, where incentives helped specifically when AI was present.

## Discussion

- **Headline:** mere access to (usually wrong) AI advice nearly eliminated suspension. Participants abandoned "I don't know" for answers that were more often wrong while reporting higher confidence. Without incentives, pooled correctness was 27.5% (no AI) vs. 9.2% (AI) and confidence 29.6 vs. 75.9 — "far more assured and far less accurate."
- **Willingness, not capacity:** incentives partly correct the collapse, so people *can* recruit judgment when motivated; but a large residual effect shows the pull to defer survives small incentives.
- **Epistemia:** the tendency to accept AI outputs for surface plausibility and fluency rather than verification. An LLM must always produce an output and never suspends judgment under ignorance; users who delegate judgment may inherit its lack of suspension, with fluency substituting for epistemic evaluation.
- **Against normal advice-taking:** people usually *underweight* others' advice (egocentric discounting, shifting only ~1/3 toward an advisor). Here participants did the opposite — a divergent, usually-wrong cue that should have *lowered* confidence instead raised it, as if the fluent answer resolved deliberation prematurely.
- **Links to automation bias/complacency:** rewarding accuracy reduced over-reliance and made participants override the AI more — paralleling accountability effects in automation research.
- **Confidence from a wrong partner:** extends "search inflates self-assessed knowledge" (Fisher, Goddu & Keil, 2015) to generative advice that is factually wrong; the risk is sharper than search because a model returns one decisive answer rather than conflicting sources.

**Limitations.** Single stimulus class (film details, chosen to induce hallucinations); untested whether suspension collapses as much when AI is *usually correct*; verification behaviour (consulting other sources) not recorded; incentives modest.

## Methods (essentials)

- **Six common questions** about films, designed to be hard for humans and hallucination-inducing for the LLM. LLM: StepFun's **Step 3.5 Flash** (chosen for easy Qualtrics embedding, not accuracy; name never shown to participants).
- **Outcome:** judgment suspension = proportion of the six responses withholding judgment ("I don't know", "I'm not sure", or blank), hand-coded by two authors with near-total agreement. GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 were tried for coding validation but proved unreliable on the task.
- Recruitment via Prolific (US-based); a priori power analyses; preregistrations on AsPredicted/OSF.

## Relation to the book

This paper is direct empirical support for **§4.1.5 *Independence of Mind***: it shows that AI erodes epistemic humility — specifically the willingness to abstain, to say "I don't know" — and does so even when the advice is wrong and even when accuracy is financially incentivised. The book can cite the striking numbers (suspension collapsing from ~0.36–0.44 to ~0.03–0.06; correctness falling to about a third while confidence nearly doubles; the preregistered incentive fix failing in all three studies) to argue that preserving independent judgment is a *discipline* that AI actively works against, not a default that survives contact with fluent machine advice. The authors' framing — that AI lowers "the metacognitive threshold at which people decide whether they know enough to answer" — maps closely to the book's case that verification and the readiness to withhold are human habits that must be deliberately maintained.
