## Abstract

Introduces a **national AI exposure metric** — the employment-share-weighted average of occupation-level AI exposure across a country's labour force — computed for **141 countries** by combining ILO occupational-employment data with ILO/Gmyrek occupational exposure scores. The picture is "jagged": exposure varies 2.6× across nations, high-income economies are far more exposed than low-income ones, women are more exposed than men in most countries, and a novel *indirect* channel (cross-border remittances) means even low-exposure countries inherit exposure from the economies their migrant workers depend on.

**Key points**

- **Coverage:** national AI exposure for **141 countries**, from ILO 2-digit ISCO-08 employment data (40 occupational groups) × occupational exposure estimates from Gmyrek et al.
- **Income gap:** high-income countries are substantially more exposed; **Europe & Central Asia is ~50% more exposed than Sub-Saharan Africa**; the most-exposed nation (Luxembourg, n = 0.37) is **2.6×** the least (Burundi, n = 0.14). White-collar employment share alone explains **R² = 0.91** of the cross-country variation (log GNI explains 77%).
- **Gender gap:** women are more exposed than men in **91% of countries** (126 of 138 with reliable sex-disaggregated data; median gap 13.7% of national exposure), driven by their concentration in white-collar and sales work. Exceptions: countries where women's work is concentrated in agriculture/household enterprises (e.g. India, Pakistan).
- **Validity:** the metric predicts observed national AI-adoption statistics from Anthropic, Microsoft, and OpenAI (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.80–0.90); a 0.10 rise in exposure ≈ **12× more per-capita Claude usage** and ~19–20 pp higher generative-AI adoption.
- **Indirect exposure (novel mechanism):** through remittances. **Tajikistan's** direct exposure is below average (n = 0.222, 21st percentile) but remittances equal **47.9% of GDP** and **79% come from Russia** (a highly exposed economy), so its remittance-accounted exposure rises to n = 0.300 (~75th percentile). (≈37% of Tajikistan's GDP is *Russian* remittance specifically — 47.9% × 79.0%, matching the abstract's "37%".)

**Takeaways**

- AI's labour-market effects are extremely unevenly distributed across nations, so **policy calibrated to US/European markets will not generalize** to the rest of the world — the paper's core normative claim.
- Exposure is *not* inherently good or bad (it flags productivity opportunity as much as displacement risk), but the groups that are more exposed (richer countries, women) are also the ones polling *least* optimistically about AI — a possible explanation for their pessimism.

## 1. Introduction

How frontier AI reshapes national economies matters to workers, firms, and governments. Publics already expect disruption (2025 Stanford AI Index; 2025 Ipsos Monitor finds more people expect AI to *worsen* than improve their local job market), and both governments (UK, Canada, EU) and AI firms frame national AI as strategic; the UN projects a **$4.8 trillion** global AI market by 2033.

Effects will be uneven because frontier AI capabilities are **jagged across tasks** (Dell'Acqua et al. 2026) and nations allocate labour very differently. Example: generative AI raised call-centre issues-resolved-per-hour by 15% (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025a) — a shock that would hit India or the Philippines far harder than economies with little call-centre work. The same technology could complement workers in one nation, reorganize entry-level labour in another, and barely touch a third.

The paper introduces a **national AI exposure metric** combining occupational exposure estimates with internationally comparable labour-allocation statistics. It is explicitly **not a forecast** of adoption, wages, or job losses — it is a comparable measure of how strongly a nation's labour composition aligns with what frontier AI can already accelerate. Exposure "is not necessarily positive or negative": it can mean productivity gains and growth, and may either reduce or increase the comparative advantage of human labour via augmentation.

## 2. Data

Combines two ingredients: national occupational employment statistics (ILO) and occupation-level AI exposure scores (Gmyrek et al.).

### 2.1 Occupational employment statistics

- ILO employment data on the **ISCO-08** classification (a four-level hierarchy spanning 436 occupational categories). Uses the most recent annual **2-digit** (sub-major group) counts.
- After excluding armed-forces, aggregate, and residual rows, ends with **40 occupational categories across 141 countries** — strong world coverage; most missing nations are small with weak labour-data infrastructure (notably **no data for China, Canada, Saudi Arabia** at the 2-digit level).
- **Labour composition varies enormously** (Figure 2): office/knowledge work (ISCO 1–4) is **61% of employment in the US** but **14% in India** and **5% in Mozambique**; agriculture (ISCO 6) is **73% in Mozambique, 35% in India, 0.4% in the US**. Wages differ too: average monthly white-collar wage PPP **$7,778 (US)** vs **$1,740 (India)**; white-collar work earns 74.2% of the US wage bill vs 30.4% in India.

### 2.2 Occupational exposure estimates

- **Exposure** = the share of time a worker spends on tasks that current AI could save (bridging technological capability with task-based economic models — Autor, Acemoglu, Restrepo).
- Eloundou et al. (2024) popularized AI exposure ("80% of the US workforce could have ≥10% of tasks affected by LLMs; ~19% could see ≥50% affected"), using human + GPT-4 annotations.
- Because the study is global, it uses **ISCO-08 exposure estimates from ILO researchers (Gmyrek et al. 2025)**, built by mapping ISCO occupations to ~29,753 Polish-data tasks, gathering human judgments on a sample of 2,861 tasks from 1,640 workers, expert-adjusting, and training a model to predict task-level exposure aggregated to 4-digit ISCO occupations.
- **Critiques acknowledged:** task→occupation averaging may assume linear production (chains/bottlenecks matter); wage/employment impact may hinge on residual-task expertise; exposure is *technical feasibility*, not observed productivity (large gaps between theoretical exposure and real usage exist).

## 3. National AI Exposure Estimates

National AI exposure = employment-share-weighted average of occupational exposure: `nᵢ = Σⱼ cᵢⱼeⱼ / Σⱼ cᵢⱼ` (cᵢⱼ = employment of occupation j in nation i; eⱼ = its exposure).

- **Variation:** most-exposed **Luxembourg (n = 0.37)** is **2.6×** least-exposed **Burundi (n = 0.14)**. Luxembourg's finance sector drives it (business/admin professionals = 38.7% of total exposure); Singapore is similarly high (n = 0.37, 73.8% white-collar). Burundi's low score reflects agrarian structure (3.1% white-collar; 69.3% of its exposure from subsistence farmers/fishers). Most-exposed regions are North America and Europe & Central Asia, **≥50% more exposed than Sub-Saharan Africa**.
- **Gender gap:** women more exposed than men in **126 of 138** countries studied (**≈91%**); median gap **13.7%** of national exposure. Male/female white-collar share differences explain ~half (50.3%) of gap variation; adding sales raises this to **87.8%** — women's concentration in white-collar + sales work is the dominant mechanism. Concrete cases: women more exposed in the Philippines (0.296 vs 0.221) and Jamaica (0.317 vs 0.228); **less** exposed in Pakistan (0.197 vs 0.234) and India (0.200 vs 0.224), where women's work is more agricultural/household. This compounds pre-existing disadvantages (labour-force barriers, wage gaps, lower AI adoption, less positive views of AI).

### 3.1 Explaining national AI exposure

- **White-collar share** is highly predictive: **R² = 0.91**. Log GNI explains 77%; together R² = 0.93 (white-collar share is the more explanatory of the two). Reclassifying sales (ISCO 52) as white-collar raises this to R² = 0.96.
- Correlates with public opinion: advanced-economy respondents report *less* trust and perceived benefit; Anthropic's interviews with 81k Claude users found workers in more-exposed occupations more worried about displacement.

## 4. Predicting National AI Adoption

Exposure measures feasibility, not adoption — so the authors test whether the metric predicts *observed* national adoption using usage data from **Anthropic (Economic Index / Claude), Microsoft (AI Diffusion), and OpenAI (Signals ChatGPT)**.

- **Strong monotonic relationships** across all three (Spearman ρ = 0.90 Anthropic, 0.90 OpenAI, 0.80 Microsoft).
- A **0.10 increase in national exposure** ≈ **12× more Claude usage per 100k working-age people**; OpenAI top-exposure quintile sits at the 86.5th usage percentile vs 14.9th for the bottom; a 0.10 exposure rise ≈ **+203 Microsoft-measured adopters per 1,000** working-age people (≈ +19–20 pp).
- Exposure alone predicts adoption well (R² = 0.77 Anthropic, 0.81 OpenAI, 0.61 Microsoft), but adds little *beyond* white-collar share + log GNI — it is best read as a structural summary consistent with those national fundamentals.

## 5. Indirect Channels for National AI Exposure

National outcomes depend on other nations (trade, outsourcing, migration), so the authors add an indirect channel: **remittances**. If a country's income comes partly via remittances from a highly exposed foreign economy, it inherits *indirect* exposure. Using the **World Bank KNOMAD** bilateral remittance matrix:

- **18 of the 19** countries with remittances >10% of GDP move to higher (remittance-accounted) exposure.
- **Tajikistan** is the clearest case: **$6.80B remittances = 47.9% of GDP** in 2024; in the 2021 bilateral matrix **Russia = 79.0%** of its remittance inflows. Its direct exposure is low (n = 0.222, 21st percentile) but Russia (n = 0.298, 75th pct) and Germany (n = 0.333, 96th pct) pull its remittance-accounted exposure up to **n = 0.300** (~75th percentile).
- Central America: **~25% of GDP** is remittance in Honduras, Guatemala, El Salvador, and **>80%** of each is remitted from the highly-exposed **United States** — so accounting for the indirect effect substantially raises their exposure despite below-average direct exposure.
- Lesson: even countries not directly exposed (or where the digital divide blocks adoption) should prepare for **ripple effects** of AI adoption elsewhere; remittance-dependent policymakers may not currently account for this second-order channel.

## 6. Discussion

Frontier AI *development* is concentrated in a few nations (US, China) but its *impacts* are globally diffuse. The metric encodes two heterogeneities (occupations differ in exposure; nations differ in occupational mix) and surfaces a third — **data availability** varies hugely. Usage data (from AI providers) is valuable and centralized, and several firms committed at the 2026 India AI Impact Summit (New Delhi Frontier AI Commitments) to publish usage reports; but traditional labour-economics data depends on government infrastructure and is scarcer in developing economies — exactly where impacts will differ most from advanced economies.

**Limitations:** (1) each occupation is treated as identically exposed in every country (ignores cross-country intra-occupation variation); (2) the coarse 2-digit ISCO level collapses distinct occupations (e.g. lawyers with musicians); (3) ISCO-08 dates from 2008 and misses newer/emerging occupations (e.g. prompt engineers); (4) incomplete national coverage (no China, Canada, Saudi Arabia), inheriting ILO/World Bank handling of contested regions.

## Appendices (selected robustness)

- **Country coverage** (App. A.1): 141 countries in the baseline sample (medium/high reliability) out of ~167 nominally covered; white-collar = ISCO major groups 1–4.
- **Robustness to exposure source** (App. B.1): results are stable using alternative exposure measures — occupation-level Pearson r = 0.954 (Eloundou) and 0.948 (Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger); national-ranking Spearman ρ = 0.993 and 0.990.
- **Full country table** (App. B.2, Table 4): e.g. US 0.323, UK 0.329, Japan 0.316, India 0.217, Nigeria n/a; ten least-exposed (≥1M workers) are all Sub-Saharan (Burundi 0.14 … Zimbabwe 0.19); ten most-exposed are Singapore/Western-European.
- **Predictors** (App. B.4): a cognitive-minus-physical (CMP) task-composition score from O*NET predicts exposure even better than the white-collar label (R² up to 0.975+), indicating the underlying cognitive-vs-physical organization of work is the real driver.

## Relation to the book

A strong quantitative anchor for **§6.4/§6.5** on the cross-national distribution of AI's effects — the emerging "global AI divide." Where much of the book's labour-market evidence comes from the US, this paper is explicitly built to show that such evidence **does not generalize**: it measures AI exposure for 141 countries and finds it "jagged," with high-income economies far more exposed than low-income ones (Europe & Central Asia ~50% above Sub-Saharan Africa; Luxembourg 2.6× Burundi), because exposure tracks white-collar employment share (R² = 0.91). Two findings are especially citable. First, a **gender gap**: women are more exposed than men in 91% of countries because they cluster in white-collar and sales work — useful for the book's equity/distribution thread, with the honest caveat that in agrarian economies (India, Pakistan) the gap reverses. Second, a **novel indirect-exposure mechanism** — remittances — that extends the divide argument: low-exposure economies like Tajikistan (below-average directly) inherit exposure from the highly exposed economies their migrant workers depend on (37% of GDP is Russian remittance), so AI shocks abroad transmit into national incomes at home. The central policy claim — responses calibrated to US/European labour markets will not transfer — is a clean line for the book.
