# Frontier AI Auditing: Toward Rigorous Third-Party Assessment of Safety and Security Practices at Leading AI Companies

## Abstract

The paper argues that frontier AI systems face far less rigorous third-party scrutiny than comparably consequential systems (consumer products, corporate financial statements, food supply chains), and that public transparency alone — system/model cards, self-published safety frameworks — cannot support well-calibrated trust because many safety- and security-relevant details are legitimately confidential, require expert interpretation, and come from companies "checking their own homework." It defines **frontier AI auditing** as "rigorous third-party verification of frontier AI developers' safety and security claims, and evaluation of their systems and practices against relevant standards, based on deep, secure access to non-public information." Crucially, audits should not stop at publicly deployed products but should cover the full range of **organisation-level** risks — internal deployment of AI systems, information security practices, and safety decision-making processes. The paper proposes four **AI Assurance Levels (AALs)** to calibrate and communicate confidence in audit findings, recommending **AAL-1 as a baseline floor for frontier AI generally, and AAL-2 as a near-term goal for the most advanced developers** (AAL-3 and AAL-4 are not yet technically feasible). It closes with eight design principles and eight concrete recommendations across four challenges (quality standards, growing the auditor ecosystem, accelerating adoption, and achieving technical readiness).

## What "frontier AI auditing" is — and how it differs from self-reporting

The formal definition (Section 2): frontier AI auditing is (1) rigorous **third-party evaluation** of developers' systems and practices against relevant standards, and (2) rigorous **third-party verification** of developers' safety and security claims, **both based on deep, secure access to non-public information**. In the paper's usage, *verification* = confirming a specific claim/commitment/property is true (e.g., a training-compute figure); *evaluation* = measuring/characterising properties of models, systems, or the organisations operating them; *assessment* is the umbrella covering either or both.

The distinction from existing practice is the paper's central motivation:
- "Transparency alone cannot enable well-calibrated trust in the most capable ('frontier') AI systems... many safety- and security-relevant details are legitimately confidential and require expert interpretation, and third parties are right to be skeptical of companies 'checking their own homework'."
- Today most third-party assessment relies on **public information and publicly accessible products** (black-box API access). This is "valuable but insufficient" — public disclosures rarely reveal the exact conditions under which an evaluation was run, whether its conclusions generalise, or whether negative findings were softened or withheld.
- The alternative to companies "grading their own homework" (an approach "with a checkered track record in many industries") is an ecosystem of independent auditors — both for-profit and non-profit — analogous to financial auditors, penetration testers, and product-testing laboratories in other industries.
- Their survey of current practice (Section 4.2) finds: public reporting is sparse and inconsistent; most evaluators get **only black-box API access** (gray-box/white-box remain rare); assessments are one-off "snapshots" not continuous; scope is heavily skewed toward technical systems (often just models) rather than organisational practices; and evaluators depend on company goodwill for access and funding, creating conflicts of interest. They cite METR's review of Anthropic's sabotage-risk report as "among the first AAL-1 audits."

**Frontier AI** is defined by capability: general-purpose AI models/systems "whose performance is no more than a year behind the state-of-the-art on a broad suite of general capability benchmarks." The frontier is a moving threshold (state-of-the-art capability one year prior). The paper focuses on **closed-weight** models and on **developers** (entities that train models from scratch or significantly extend capabilities), using "developer" and "company" interchangeably.

## The four AI Assurance Levels (AALs)

AALs calibrate how much confidence stakeholders can place in an audit's conclusion. At lower levels auditors rely more on company-provided information and can speak mainly to a particular system; at higher levels they take fewer assumptions for granted, assess the whole organisation, and (at the top) can rule out materially significant deception. Access is cumulative — each level adds to the one below.

| Level | Assurance | Duration | Access (cumulative) | Methods / what it targets | Readiness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AAL-1** | Limited | Time-bounded, ~a few weeks to a small number of months | **Black-box** API access to multiple checkpoints/variants, chain-of-thought and logits, ability to toggle safety classifiers, overview of mitigations; limited non-public info scoped to the audited system. Org: written representations, org chart, key-staff list, attestations. | Run private dangerous-capability eval suites (cyber, bio, manipulation), limited red-teaming; review docs; interview key staff. Relies heavily on company representations. | Achievable now; early pilots already conducted. |
| **AAL-2** | Moderate | Months (at least) | **Gray-box** access to multiple key systems; training/testing log samples; compute-allocation records; ability to remove/examine all mitigations. Org: safety/security documentation (e.g., unredacted safety cases), governance decision records, cross-function staff interviews (safety, security, policy, product), subsets of training data, prior-incident info. | Reproduce a sample of internal evaluations; extensive targeted red-teaming; investigate sandbagging using model internals; develop statistical "model signatures" to confirm audited = deployed model; some continuous monitoring. | Near-term goal; feasible with investment. |
| **AAL-3** | High | Ongoing, multiyear engagement (lead auditor + many subcontractors) | **White-box** access; all company activities presumptively in scope and fully available; extensive continuous monitoring; authority to examine any area of concern. | Directly verify all material aspects of safety cases and infosec protocols; continuous monitoring over years; compute accounting to detect undisclosed training runs. Remaining gaps: sophisticated deception, industry-wide vulnerabilities. | (At least) very difficult today. |
| **AAL-4** | Very high | Continuous | Full understanding of the company's systems, computing hardware, and governance | Continuous verification **designed to detect active deception attempts**; hardware attestation and cryptographic logs; provides "treaty-grade" confirmation of the company's risk profile, robust even to highly-resourced actors trying to cheat. | Appears infeasible today; possibly late 2027 if major investment begins soon. |

Framing details worth citing:
- **Errors vs. fraud.** "While audits at these levels [AAL-1/2] may be able to detect errors (i.e., unintentional misstatements or mistakes), they are less likely to be able to detect fraud (i.e., intentional deception)." AAL-3/4 are the levels that address deliberate deception.
- **AAL-4 lineage.** "Very high assurance" is borrowed from the literature on verifying international arms-control agreements; the authors envisage AAL-4 for very high-stakes purposes such as verifying US–China cooperation on baseline AI safety standards. AAL-4 would likely require government involvement, multi-jurisdictional representation, and security clearances.
- **Cost (very rough estimates).** AAL-1 ≈ US$300,000–600,000 (multi-week to few-month); AAL-2 ≈ US$1,000,000+ (multi-month); AAL-3/AAL-4 ≈ several million dollars annually.
- **The recommendation.** "We recommend AAL-1 as a baseline for frontier AI generally, and AAL-2 as a near-term goal for the most advanced subset of frontier AI developers." AAL-1 is described as "the peak of current practices in AI." The paper deliberately declines to be more prescriptive about which AAL applies to which developers.

## The organisational-level argument: beyond deployed products

The paper's second core claim is that audits must take an **organisation-level** perspective, not just examine individual models or deployed products — because risk "emerges from the interaction of three overarching components: digital systems, computing hardware, and governance practices, and harm can arise even when a model is never deployed in external-facing systems."

The rationale is the danger of **abstraction error**: "forming the wrong conclusion by treating a partial or simplified unit of analysis... as if it were sufficient to assess overall system and organizational risk." An audit "can be right about the specific artifact or process it examined while still being wrong in the way that matters about the company's overall risk posture" — missing the forest for the trees. Four ways abstraction errors arise:

1. **Portfolio blindness** — auditing the most visible or best-behaved system while missing a materially riskier surface. Developers maintain portfolios (checkpoints, post-training variants, internal research models, partner preview builds, customer fine-tunes, internal tools with broader permissions), so a clean finding on the flagship deployment can be misleading if treated as representative.
2. **Configuration drift** — real-world risk depends on system-level configuration (system prompts, filters, routing, retrieval sources, tool access, memory, rate limits, monitoring thresholds, personalisation). Modest changes (enabling a tool, relaxing a filter, swapping a classifier) can materially change risk; an audit of a staging config may not match production. This motivates **continuous monitoring** ("living assessments, not stale PDFs") — findings should carry explicit validity conditions and auto-deprecate when key assumptions no longer hold.
3. **Non-compositional safety/security** — safe components can produce an unsafe assembly (e.g., a model that refuses harmful chat requests may enable harm inside an agentic scaffold chaining tool calls over long horizons). The risk-relevant question is "what can the organisation's integrated systems do, under realistic conditions, given the actual controls?"
4. **Boundary mismatch in security** — strong product security but weak protection of trade secrets/model weights. "At least two frontier AI companies have had AI research-related intellectual property stolen from them"; stolen weights can be used without the company's public-facing mitigations, so a system-level audit of the external interface can badly underestimate infosec risk.

Hence the **three lenses** auditors must integrate: (a) **models and systems** (models plus connected features like classifiers/system prompts, and infosec safeguards like authentication); (b) **computing hardware** (its quantity, security, and allocation across development and deployment — gaps in compute accounting can indicate undisclosed **internal deployments** or attempts to mislead auditors); and (c) **governance** (development/deployment decision-making, infosec systems and protocols, incident response, safety culture, and clarity of responsibility allocation).

## Risk scope: four categories

Audits should cover four risk categories, for each verifying company claims and evaluating practices against the company's own policies, applicable regulations, and industry best practice: (1) **intentional misuse** (e.g., cyberattacks); (2) **unintended system behaviour** (errors harming users, property, or third parties from wrong goals or unreliable performance); (3) **information security** (theft of a model or user data); and (4) **emergent social phenomena** (e.g., AI addiction, facilitation of self-harm).

## Eight design principles (Section 5)

1. **Scope of risks** — comprehensive coverage of the four risk categories.
2. **Organisational perspective** — audit the company as a whole, not just individual models/systems (the abstraction-error argument above).
3. **Levels of assurance** — the AAL framework for calibrating and communicating confidence.
4. **Access** — "deep enough to assure auditors and other stakeholders, secure enough to reassure auditees." Access spans model internals, training processes, compute allocation, governance records, and staff interviews, protected via mechanisms from other domains (on-site review under restrictive NDAs) and new techniques (AI-powered summarisation/analysis of information too sensitive to share directly).
5. **Continuous monitoring** — "living assessments, not stale PDFs"; combine periodic deep assessments of slow-moving elements (governance, culture) with event-triggered reviews (releases, incidents) and continuous automated monitoring of fast-changing surfaces (API behaviour, config drift).
6. **Independent experts** — genuine independence via mandatory disclosure of financial relationships, standardised terms of engagement to prevent "auditor shopping," cooling-off periods in both directions between industry and audit roles, alternative payment models to reduce dependence on auditees, and consortia/subcontracting for breadth.
7. **Rigor** — standardised but adaptive process; auditors define their own metrics rather than validating company-preselected ones; automated, transparent, reproducible where feasible; guard construct and ecological validity and protect criteria against gaming; procedural fairness (companies can correct factual errors without unduly influencing conclusions).
8. **Clarity** — standardised audit reports (scope, assurance level, conclusions, reasoning, recommendations); redacted/summarised versions for external stakeholders and full unredacted reports for boards, executives, and, in some cases, regulators.

## Challenges and eight recommendations (Section 6)

Four challenges: (1) **ensuring high quality standards** so auditing doesn't devolve into a checkbox exercise or lag the industry; (2) **growing the ecosystem** of auditors rapidly without compromising quality; (3) **accelerating adoption** by clarifying and strengthening incentives; and (4) **achieving technical readiness** for high AALs.

The eight recommended next steps:
1. AI companies, philanthropists, investors, insurers should fund public analysis of the quantity and quality of audits/auditors.
2. Policymakers should create a **PCAOB-style non-profit "auditor of auditors,"** with government-approved standards and authority to revoke accreditation.
3. The AI evaluation ecosystem should establish a **Frontier AI Auditor Accreditation Program** with tiered certifications, specialty endorsements, and accountability mechanisms.
4. Policymakers and developers should implement **targeted safe harbours** protecting good-faith safety research and auditing, conditional on compliance with best practices.
5. Governments should clarify **insurance coverage** for AI risks, and require explicit AI-risk coverage in government procurement.
6. Policymakers should embed frontier AI auditing in **public procurement**, with strong requirements for high-stakes domains (health, defence).
7. Philanthropists, governments, and companies should invest in an **"Auditability R&D and Pilots" portfolio** to make AAL-3/AAL-4 feasible and cost-effective.
8. State-of-the-art developers should run **early pilots of AAL-3 (and later AAL-4)** auditing.

The overarching message: auditing complements — does not replace — internal safety practices and external regulation; it needs meaningful standards to audit against and strong incentives to comply; and companies already routinely share sensitive information with financial auditors, acquirers, penetration testers, and product-testing labs under controlled terms, so similar practices for AI safety are "achievable and urgently needed."
