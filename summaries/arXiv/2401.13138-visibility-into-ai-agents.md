# Visibility into AI Agents

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2401.13138-visibility-into-ai-agents.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2401.13138-visibility-into-ai-agents.pdf) — Alan Chan, Carson Ezell, Max Kaufmann, Kevin Wei, Lewis Hammond, Herbie Bradley, Emma Bluemke, Nitarshan Rajkumar, David Krueger, Noam Kolt, Lennart Heim, and Markus Anderljung, *Visibility into AI Agents*, in Proceedings of the 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT '24), Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, June 3–6, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1145/3630106.3658948 (arXiv:2401.13138v6, 17 May 2024).
> Peer-reviewed conference paper (ACM FAccT). Lead author Alan Chan is affiliated with the Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI) and Mila; several co-authors are also from GovAI. The paper is descriptive/analytical policy scholarship, not an audited empirical study: it proposes and assesses measures rather than reporting deployed systems, and explicitly declines to advocate for immediate implementation.

## Abstract

This paper argues that governing increasingly capable AI agents — "systems capable of pursuing complex goals with limited supervision" — requires **visibility**, which the authors define as "information about where, why, how, and by whom certain AI agents are used." It assesses three complementary categories of measures to increase visibility: **agent identifiers**, **real-time monitoring**, and **activity logging (activity logs)**. For each, it outlines implementations that "vary in intrusiveness of data collection and informativeness of the data," analyses how they apply across a spectrum from centralised to decentralised deployment (including supply-chain actors such as hardware and software service providers), and weighs the implications for **privacy** and **concentration of power**. The authors deliberately do not advocate immediate implementation; they argue visibility is a *necessary but not sufficient* foundation for agent governance — even with full visibility, "those harmed by them may not have the power to intervene and reduce risks." The paper uses "visibility" rather than "transparency" as more common in a regulatory context, and distinguishes both from "explainability."

## Framing: why agents, why visibility

The authors define **agency** as "the degree to which an AI system acts directly in the world to achieve long-horizon goals, with little human intervention or specification of how to do so"; an **(AI) agent** is a system with a relatively high degree of agency (used interchangeably with "agentic system"). Systems that mainly predict without acting (e.g. image classifiers) have low agency; existing foundation models are *not* themselves considered agents. The definition compresses four axes from Chan et al. [35]: how specified the behaviour is, how goal-directed it is, how directly it impacts the world, and over how long a time-horizon it can achieve goals.

The paper focuses on **deployed** agents (agents in use — public, select-customer, or internal), not pre-deployment testing, because users and deployers can exacerbate risks after release through fine-tuning, connecting tools/services, or **scaffolding** ("any method that structures the calls to an AI system so as to facilitate the pursuit of goals," e.g. AutoGPT).

**Five agent-specific risks** motivate visibility (§1.1): (1) **Malicious use** — agents as an "impact multiplier," automating end-to-end pipelines (e.g. autonomous science enabling biological/chemical harm) and lowering the expertise barrier because no human need be in the loop; (2) **Overreliance and disempowerment** — collective dependence on agents for essential functions, with malfunctions that may not be immediately apparent and may be hard to stop; (3) **Delayed and diffuse impacts** — harms (e.g. entrenched algorithmic hiring bias) that require long-term, aggregate tracking to notice; (4) **Multi-agent risks** — destabilising feedback loops (e.g. the 2010 flash crash), common vulnerabilities from shared foundation models, collusion; (5) **Sub-agents** — an agent instantiating further agents (via API calls or by training/fine-tuning others), adding points of failure and making shutdown harder because "we lack methods for determining when an agent has created a sub-agent."

Visibility supports the standard purposes of governance regimes for delegated authority — reducing information asymmetries, shaping incentives, and triggering enforcement — analogous to workplace supervision, corporate audits/shareholder oversight, and freedom-of-information regimes for government.

## Supply-chain actors (Definitions, §2)

The measures are analysed across distinct roles: **developers** (build the system, including scaffolding, not just the underlying model); **users** (interact with and instruct the agent); **deployers** (operate the system and serve it to users — may differ from developers, e.g. Microsoft deploys OpenAI's GPT-4 which it did not build; deployers can serve either a foundation model that users make agentic, or an agent directly); **compute providers** (supply/maintain hardware infrastructure); and **tool/service providers** (external systems the agent interacts with, often via dedicated APIs). Crucially, **because deployers run agents, the inputs and outputs of agents are by default visible to the deployer**, which is what makes the three measures feasible for the centralised case. Tool/service providers see only outputs relevant to their service.

## Measure 1 — Agent identifiers (§3.1)

An **agent identifier** "indicates whether and which AI agents are involved in interactions." Identifiers are attached to select outputs, visible to certain actors, and may carry additional information. Uses: regulators requiring agents to disclose they are non-human (like bot-disclosure laws); the public knowing they interacted with an agent; summary statistics on agent use in high-risk settings; flagging significant actions (e.g. transfer of sensitive information); service providers rejecting requests that lack certain guarantees; and — via **unique** identifiers — linking an action to a specific agent and its user, developer(s), and deployer for accountability.

**Three design decisions** (§3.1.1):
- *Which outputs contain the identifier?* Depends on **format** (images → watermarks; API requests → a simple header like an HTTP header) and **content** (significant outputs, e.g. purchases on the user's behalf, or actions above a threshold such as large compute requests). Identifiers generalise watermarks because they cover *all* outputs including tool/service use, not just text/image/audio.
- *Which actors can see it?* Different actors may need visibility (e.g. bank, counterparty, API provider); detecting multi-agent risks may require identifiers visible to *other agents*.
- *How specific is it?* From "some agent was involved" up to a unique per-instance identifier; unique instance identifiers may require **cryptographic methods such as software attestation** to assure provenance.

**Agent cards** (§3.1.2) — additional documented information attached to an identifier, inspired by model cards/datasheets. Three important categories: (a) *the underlying system* (evaluation results, prior incidents, dependencies, training methods/data); (b) *the specific instance* (how deployed, accessible tools/services, scaffolding, intended scope/permissions/goals, intended sector); (c) *the actors involved* (user, compute provider, developers, other humans interacted with — supporting accountability and user authentication). Appendix A gives a fuller list, including evaluations of the system's degree of agency and generality, red flags, agent permissions (e.g. sudo access), persistence/lifetime, number of people impacted and severity, and the degree/ease of human oversight. **Tradeoff:** a more comprehensive agent card is more useful but more intrusive to collect and costlier to analyse; "comprehensiveness of information should likely scale with the risk of the activity."

## Measure 2 — Real-time monitoring (§3.2)

**Real-time monitoring** is "oversight of agent activity in real-time, with the goal of flagging and potentially filtering problematic behaviour." It is likely to be automated to cope with the speed/scale of agents (cf. Naihin et al. for a preliminary implementation). **Key distinction and privacy advantage:** monitoring "does not require the collection or storage of activity logs" — analogous to moderation filters versus chat logs. This matters for privacy-sensitive users (e.g. business customers protecting corporate IP); some cloud providers already offer **no-logging provisions** subject to real-time abuse monitoring (the paper notes Google's no-logging provision still keeps logs for 72 hours for feasibility).

Once behaviour is flagged, the deployer can intervene and inform authorities; users could be offered human review of any action impeding their agent. Because it demands an immediate decision, monitoring is **most suitable for clear-rule violations** — restrictions on allowed activities, limits on tools/permissions, leakage of sensitive information, usage caps — and for **anomalies**. It can also combine information across agents (e.g. an indicator of algorithmic collusion could be correlation in price movements; many agents doing the same thing might overload a system), and can flag when an agent exerts **significant influence** (large transactions, heavy compute, many sub-agents, many physical systems), with a possible response of pausing until the deployer verifies explicit user approval — as banks suspend accounts on suspected fraud.

## Measure 3 — Activity logs (§3.3)

**Activity logs** are "records of certain inputs and outputs of an agent," from users or tools/services, and can also record state changes such as weight updates. They serve **post-incident attribution and forensics**, audits, incident investigation, research, and can surface novel behaviours to improve real-time monitoring. Categories can include tool use, internal reasoning, self-critique, and memory management. Logs may need to persist well beyond an agent's lifetime (impacts can be delayed; a user can re-run an agent with the same inputs/memory), and logs across agents may need combining to trace one agent as a continuation of another or to model malfunction propagation across a network.

**Level of detail** (§3.3.2) is a key design decision: from high-level summaries or samples up to a regulator requiring a deployer to record *all* of an agent's behaviour in high-risk environments. "More detailed logging is more useful, but may impose more significant costs on the deployer, require more resources and expertise for analysis, and pose more significant privacy concerns."

## Privacy and access tradeoffs (§3.4)

Privacy "may conflict with obtaining detailed information about agent activity." Deployers increasingly offer confidentiality assurances: no-logging APIs (with the ability to turn off safety filters/moderation classifiers), guarantees that customer data won't train any AI system and stays in the customer's cloud instance, and the ability to delete logs after a period. Existing law (**GDPR**) further restricts: agent cards and logs may constitute personal data (e.g. when an agent accesses a filesystem with PII). A central worry: "if agents substitute for humans in a wide variety of activities, information about those agents might be tantamount to information about the users of those agents," and agent activity may be *easier* to monitor than human activity because deployers are a central intermediary — creating surveillance risk from governments or deployers.

Mitigations: limit collection to the agent's risk level; **decentralised data-custody schemes or data trusts**; and **modulating access** along two dimensions — *granularity* (aggregated / de-identified / identifiable; differentially private summary statistics) and *quantity* (full access / query- or filter-based / upon-request for pre-specified records with a compelling reason). The principle: access "should be the minimum necessary for the accessing party to achieve its (legitimate) objectives," with identifiable information available on a showing of compelling need and/or third-party-adjudicator approval. Precedent cited: the CFTC collects identifiable personal information for traders transacting above a specified threshold (Large Trader Reporting).

## Decentralised deployments (§4)

Some deployments bypass deployers: users run downloadable (open-release) agents on their own or cloud hardware, or combine systems from different deployers — and a malicious actor could deliberately do this to avoid detection. Two leverage points extend visibility:

- **Compute-provider oversight (§4.1):** compute providers can spot large-scale deployments (concerning because they multiply impact and consume noticeable resources) and ask users for proof they have implemented visibility measures — connected to a "know-your-customer" scheme for compute (Egan & Heim).
- **Tool/service providers as distributed enforcement (§4.2):** by conditioning tool/service access on visibility measures (e.g. requiring agent identifiers from trusted deployers, or permissions confirmations), providers incentivise adherence. Limitation: agents may bypass APIs by mimicking human behaviour, motivating CAPTCHA-like detection of disguised AI activity or proof-of-human-identity (know-your-customer) for high-risk actions — though these must be robust to improving agent capabilities and raise their own privacy questions. APIs' convenience may keep most interaction visible anyway.

**Risks of extending visibility (§4.3):** serious implications for privacy and concentration of power. Because "a handful of compute providers dominates the market," monitoring their users "would [be] equivalent to monitoring much of society"; providers may also have lax security. Conditioning tools on certified deployers could pressure users toward a concentrated set of deployers who may be captured, invasive (e.g. excessive logging), or ill-suited to some users (e.g. low-resource languages), exacerbating systemic risk. Proposed mitigations: **voluntary standards** (allowing experimentation before codification); **open-source agent-identifier frameworks** with independent certification authorities — explicitly analogised to **Let's Encrypt** (the non-profit CA that promoted HTTPS); **limiting** which tools require identifiers (prioritise high-risk tools, e.g. platforms handling pathogens/chemicals, and business over personal use); and using **liability incentives** rather than hard mandates — accounting for compliance when determining a user's legal liability, analogous to HIPAA de-identification safe harbours and the 2023 U.S. National Cybersecurity Strategy's proposal to shield firms following cybersecurity best practices. Also floated: decentralised data-custody for compute-provider logs and transparency into government data requests.

## Conclusion (§5)

The three mechanisms — agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logs — form a foundation for governing agentic systems, extendable to decentralised deployments via compute and tool/service providers, and must be balanced against privacy and power-concentration concerns. The authors again decline to advocate immediate implementation, calling for more understanding of the measures and their negative impacts. Their closing caveat is the paper's most important qualifier for a governance chapter: **"Visibility informs actions to manage risks... but is not by itself sufficient. Even with a comprehensive understanding of agent activities, those harmed by them may not have the power to intervene and reduce risks."**
