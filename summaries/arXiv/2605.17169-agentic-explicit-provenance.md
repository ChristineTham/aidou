# Responsible Agentic AI Requires Explicit Provenance

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2605.17169-agentic-explicit-provenance.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2605.17169-agentic-explicit-provenance.pdf) — Jinwei Hu, Xinmiao Huang, Qisong He, Youcheng Sun, Yi Dong, Xiaowei Huang, *Responsible Agentic AI Requires Explicit Provenance*, arXiv:2605.17169v1 [cs.AI], 16 May 2026. <https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17169>
> **Preprint** (not peer-reviewed), authored by academics at the University of Liverpool and Mohamed bin Zayed University of AI. It is a *position paper* — its central argument is normative and its experiments are self-described as "preliminary" and "not intended as a standalone system contribution." Treat its claims accordingly.

## Abstract

The paper argues that in agentic AI — where LLM-based agents plan, act, use tools, hold persistent memory, and delegate to one another — responsibility "remains a subjective and unenforced concept" because no current framework produces the "quantifiable, traceable, and interventionable provenance needed to assign it when harm emerges from compositions no single party designed." The authors' position: what is missing is not better benchmark evaluation but **explicit provenance across the full agentic lifecycle**, which they call "the only viable basis for making responsibility computable and actionable." They advance this along four axes: *why* provenance is a structural necessity (socio-technical responsibility gaps); *what* it must encode (a causal-attribution function κ and a responsibility tensor R); *how* it is made computable (four lifecycle layers, plus a neuro-symbolic experiment showing causal signal is estimable online *before* irreversible harm accumulates); and *who* bears responsibility (applying the tensor to a concrete incident). The single most citable claim: explicit provenance is "not a discretionary refinement but the necessary condition for responsible agentic AI." This grounds the multi-agent accountability problem — that when one agent delegates to another, harm can arise from the *composition* that no single party built or audited.

## The core problem: composition dissolves responsibility

The motivating case (Introduction, §1): "individually audited skills each pass safety review, yet their composition within an LLM-based agent produces regulatory violations that no per-skill audit could have detected: no party can be held accountable" — because "no responsibility envelope was specified before deployment to bound each party's obligations, and no record exists of how the harmful trajectory was jointly produced." Harm "does not reside in any single component but emerges from system-level trajectories, compositions, and distributed causal chains that no individual audit can inspect." When harm occurs, "each party points to another and responsibility dissolves before it can be assigned."

The authors frame three structural limitations of the older per-component "trustworthy AI" paradigm (§2):
- **Risk is compositional**: properties verified for individual components "need not survive their interaction."
- **Evaluation is fragile**: task completion "can diverge from safety, policy compliance, and harmfulness control."
- **Accountability is diffuse**: harmful outcomes "arise from multi-step trajectories distributed across developers, platforms, and deployers that no per-component audit can reconstruct."

Adoption context (self-reported, from cited industry surveys): McKinsey reports 23% of respondents *scaling* agentic AI and 39% *experimenting*; PwC reports 79% of senior executives say AI agents are already adopted; Deloitte characterises agentic AI as "scaling faster than its guardrails." "Agentic AI is therefore not merely a capability frontier but a responsibility frontier."

## Why explicit provenance is a structural requirement (§3)

**Proposition 3.1 (Structural Requirement).** A responsible agentic system requires explicit provenance satisfying three properties:
1. **Quantifiability** — it "produces measurable causal attribution for each component and corresponding party."
2. **Traceability** — it "grounds responsibility assignments in causally verified execution records and epistemic position."
3. **Interventionability** — it "is produced continuously so that agentic systems remain open to interception and recovery across the lifecycle."

The three are "jointly necessary rather than independently sufficient": quantifiability without traceability leaves assignments contested; traceability without interventionability confines attribution to post-hoc reconstruction (a mere "forensic instrument"); interventionability without quantifiability gives monitoring with no basis to assign what was detected.

Each property is mapped to socio-technical gaps. Notably (Quantifiability): "the EU AI Liability Directive's presumption of fault cannot attach when harm is a property of component interaction rather than any individual component," and "no party clears the culpability threshold and victims have no one to hold accountable." Some cited failure rates (Traceability, self-reported from other papers): indirect prompt injection succeeds "with attack rates reaching 47% against GPT-4 across 17 tool-integrated systems"; "over 68% of sandbox scenarios exhibit potential real-world agent failures"; "over 90% of high-popularity skills in ClawHub fail security review."

## What provenance must encode: the formal machinery (§4)

The paper's distinctive contribution is a formalisation that makes responsibility "computable."

**Causal contribution (Def 4.1).** For a harm event ω and trajectory τ, party p's causal contribution is the counterfactual difference

  κ(p, ω, τ) = Pr[ω | τ] − Pr[ω | τ₋ₚ],

where τ₋ₚ is a counterfactual trajectory with p's relevant decisions removed/neutralised. κ > 0 iff p has nonzero causal contribution. The authors are careful (Remark 4.1) that "κ is a theoretical anchor rather than a directly observed quantity," approximable "from execution traces, tool-activation records, permission boundaries, counterfactual replay, and controlled ablations."

**Epistemic position (Def 4.2).** Responsibility also depends on "what [a party] knew, or should reasonably have known, before the harm occurred" — assessed against "the objective standard of what a reasonably informed actor in p's role should have anticipated," explicitly so parties "cannot evade responsibility by manufacturing ignorance."

**Computable responsibility (Def 4.3).** A responsibility-assignment function ρ : P × Ω → [0,1] satisfying:
- **Proportionality** — ρ scales with causal contribution under reasonable foreseeability;
- **Completeness** — Σ ρ(p, ω) + ρ_inst(ω) = 1, i.e. every share is accounted for, with the remainder pushed to an *institutional* layer;
- **Non-evasion** — real and foreseeable contribution "cannot be erased by system complexity."

**Residual institutional responsibility (Def 4.4).** The unassigned portion "is borne by the institutional layer when that layer had the authority and capacity to close or absorb the accountability gap through standards, certification, monitoring, incident response, or compensation mechanisms, but failed to do so."

**Responsibility tensor (Def 4.5).** R ∈ [0,1]^(|P|×|Ω|×|D|) gives each party p's responsibility for each harm ω along each socio-technical dimension d. The scalar ρ(p, ω) is recovered by weighting dimensions (weights wₖ summing to 1). Key design claim: the tensor "remains computable from P even without full provenance by reducing to party identification alone" — so responsible parties "can always be identified"; with explicit provenance, each entry becomes "causally grounded, making responsibility attribution actionable."

## How provenance is made computable: four lifecycle layers (§5)

Four "sequentially dependent" layers, each with an open research agenda:
- **L1 Design** — the causal structure (a multilevel dependency graph over parties, models, skills) "must be designed into the system, not merely relying on reconstructed post-hoc logs." Current practice fails because components "are composed without documenting causal dependencies."
- **L2 Engineering** — execution records must capture the evidence to evaluate κ and epistemic position, covering both *retrospective* (traceability) and *prospective* (interventionability) needs, including online risk estimation and *compositional verification* whenever a new component enters (test Δκ before co-activation).
- **L3 Deployment** — populating the tensor "requires human judgment that technical systems cannot produce on their own." Introduces a **Deployment Readiness Condition (Def 5.1)** requiring, among other things, that "all parties have established a responsibility envelope documenting their obligations and intervention boundaries," incident-response plans exercised with humans, and validated informed-consent mechanisms.
- **L4 Experience** — addresses *gradual* harms (autonomy erosion, compositional bias amplification) that "build up gradually across interactions without any single event crossing a detectable threshold," requiring population-scale counterfactual monitoring.

**Neuro-symbolic experiment (§5.5, Appendix A).** A plug-in monitor (NeSyGuard) attaches to agents without modifying internals, converts raw traces to a canonical step-level representation, learns failure-aligned event abstractions, and scores prefixes with neural (GRU, Transformer) and symbolic (soft-FSM, DFA) variants. Across four benchmarks (WebArena, TAU2Bench, SkillsBench, TerminalBench), learned monitors substantially beat both a random baseline and a zero-shot LLM judge on AUPRC (e.g. WebArena GRU 0.90 vs random 0.41; zero-shot LLM 0.41). Three claimed findings: causal signal Pr[ω | eₜ, αₜ:T] "is estimable online" (quantifiability tractable at runtime); finite-state variants compress warnings into human-legible symbolic states (traceability); and high-risk prefixes are flagged "early enough to support intervention before irreversible consequences accumulate" (interventionability). This is the paper's evidence for **online intervention before irreversible damage** — provenance is not merely forensic.

## Who bears responsibility: worked incident (§6)

Using a WebArena DFA monitor, warning states surface *before* a task concludes (e.g. a "misaligned search query" state at 25% through the trajectory, an "external-search redirect" at 40%). The paper walks these back through the deployment-chain dictionary P to distribute obligations across a *skill developer* (failure to specify retrieval boundaries), a *platform operator* (failure to enforce navigation constraints), and an *agent developer* (deploying without compositional verification), with the end user's share "negligible absent prior authorization or risk disclosure" and the remainder falling to ρ_inst. The point: this "transform[s] the question of who bears responsibility from an unanswerable subjective concept into a[n] actionable and computable allocation."

## Alternative views the authors pre-empt (§7)

- *Provenance overhead slows deployment.* Rebuttal: "the constraint on agentic AI deployment is not the absence of provenance infrastructure but the absence of trust"; provenance "does not cap what agentic systems can do; it builds the institutional trust that makes large-scale delegation of authority viable."
- *Distributed responsibility produces irresolvable value conflict.* Rebuttal: the tensor's dimension weights wₖ "are not fixed by the framework but determined by domain-specific governance, making value disagreements legible and contestable rather than hidden."

## Connection to Cheng's three-pillar accountability (already cited in Ch5)

The book already cites Cheng et al. (2026), *Toward safe and responsible AI agents: A three-pillar model for transparency, accountability, and trustworthiness* — transparency (record of *what* the agent did), accountability (record of *why*, via a decision journal, and *who* is answerable), trustworthiness (calibrated confidence gating human review). Hu et al. sit directly downstream and one level deeper: where Cheng prescribes *that* agents keep a record of why and who, Hu et al. ask what such a record must formally *encode* to survive **multi-agent composition and delegation** — the case where Cheng's "who is answerable" fractures because harm belongs to no single agent. Their answer (causal-attribution function + responsibility tensor + institutional residual) is a mechanism for making Cheng's "accountability" pillar computable across a delegation chain, and their interventionability requirement extends Cheng's "trustworthiness" from a review-gating signal into a real-time circuit-breaker. Useful as the multi-agent accountability point in Ch5: individually well-behaved agents can compose into harm no one owns, and closing that gap needs explicit provenance that traces each agent's contribution.
