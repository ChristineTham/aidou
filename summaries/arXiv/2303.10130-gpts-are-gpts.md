# GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2303.10130-gpts-are-gpts.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2303.10130-gpts-are-gpts.pdf) — Tyna Eloundou, Sam Manning, Pamela Mishkin & Daniel Rock, *GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models*, arXiv:2303.10130v5 [econ.GN], 22 Aug 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130
> A later version was published in *Science* 384:6702 (2024), DOI [10.1126/science.adj0998](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj0998).
> This is an economics working paper (three of four authors were at OpenAI/OpenResearch at the time; Daniel Rock is at the University of Pennsylvania). It is a peer-reviewed empirical study, but note the mixed vendor affiliation. The title is a deliberate pun: Generative Pre-trained Transformers are General-Purpose Technologies.

## Abstract

The paper estimates how much of the US labour market is *exposed* to large language models (LLMs), where exposure is defined as an LLM (or LLM-powered software) being able to cut in half the time needed to complete a work task at equivalent quality. Applying a new exposure rubric — via both human annotators and GPT-4 itself — to O*NET occupational task data, the authors find that around 80% of the US workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by LLMs, and roughly 19% of workers could see at least 50% of their tasks exposed. Exposure rises (not falls) with wages and educational attainment, contrary to earlier automation waves that hit routine and manual work hardest. The single most citable sentence: "around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by the introduction of LLMs, while approximately 19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted." The authors are emphatic that this is *potential* exposure, not a prediction of realised impact, adoption timing, or job loss, and conclude that LLMs exhibit the hallmarks of a general-purpose technology.

## What "exposure" means (the rubric)

Exposure is defined as whether access to an LLM or LLM-powered system "would reduce the time required for a human to perform a specific DWA or complete a task by at least 50 percent" while maintaining equivalent quality. "Equivalent quality" means a third party (typically the recipient of the output) would not notice or care that an LLM helped. The 50% time-reduction threshold is acknowledged as "somewhat arbitrary" but was chosen for ease of interpretation and deliberately set high, because the authors expected real-world time savings to fall short of the labelled potential.

Three exposure levels:
- **E0 — No exposure:** the LLM gives no/minimal time saving, or degrades quality.
- **E1 — Direct exposure:** an LLM accessed via ChatGPT or the OpenAI Playground can cut task time by ≥50%.
- **E2 — LLM+ exposed:** the bare LLM cannot halve the time, but *additional software built on top of it* could. Image-generation capability was labelled separately as E3 in annotation, then combined with E2 for analysis.

From these, three aggregate measures per occupation:
- **α** = share of tasks at E1 (direct-only; the lower bound).
- **β** = E1 + 0.5·E2 (the paper's primary measure; the 0.5 weight discounts E2 tasks for the extra investment complementary tooling requires).
- **ζ** = E1 + E2 (upper bound; maximal exposure including software/tooling).

Unless stated otherwise, results refer to β.

## Method and data

- **Tasks:** O*NET 27.2 database, covering 1,016 occupations — 19,265 tasks and 2,087 Detailed Work Activities (DWAs). DWA labels are aggregated to the task level, then to the occupation level.
- **Wages/employment:** 2020–2021 BLS Occupational Employment series, plus BLS education and on-the-job-training requirements and CPS-derived demographics. Data mostly cover documented, non-self-employed workers in the formal economy.
- **Human ratings:** the authors personally labelled tasks/DWAs requiring physicality or manual dexterity; experienced annotators (who had reviewed GPT-3/3.5/4 outputs for OpenAI's alignment work) labelled the rest. This is the paper's *primary* results source.
- **GPT-4 ratings:** an early version of GPT-4 rated all task/occupation pairs, using a rubric-as-prompt slightly tuned for agreement with a small set of author labels. The models available to annotators at labelling time were GPT-3.5-family, not GPT-4.
- **Agreement:** for the primary GPT-4 Rubric 1 vs. Human comparison, agreement was 80.8% on E1 (Pearson 0.223) and 82.1% on E1+E2 (Pearson 0.654). Human and GPT-4 occupation-level exposure ratings correlate strongly (Figure 2). No single annotation source is treated as definitive ground truth.
- **Core vs. supplemental:** O*NET "core" tasks receive twice the weight of "supplemental" tasks in occupation-level aggregation (weights sum to one), except where the paper notes equal weighting.

## Headline results

- **Task shares within occupations (β measure, Table 3):** mean occupation-level α ≈ 0.14–0.15 (about 15% of tasks *directly* exposed), rising to ~0.30 (human) / 0.34 (GPT-4) for β, and 0.46 (human) / 0.55 (GPT-4) for ζ.
- **Worker coverage (β):** **~80% of workers are in an occupation with at least 10% of tasks exposed; ~19% are in an occupation with over half of tasks exposed.**
- **Human vs. GPT-4 on the 50%-exposed cut:** human annotators judged 3% of US workers to have over half their tasks exposed under the *direct-only* (E1/α) definition; accounting for complementary tools and other generative models, human estimates rise to as many as 49% of workers with half-or-more tasks exposed. On equal-weight occupation counts, humans found 2.3% of occupations α50-exposed, 21.6% β50-exposed, and 47.3% ζ50-exposed.
- **The software multiplier:** across all tasks, the mean gap between α and ζ is 0.42 (GPT-4) / 0.32 (human). The authors read this as complementary software potentially more than doubling the task exposure of LLMs used bare — so tooling built on top of LLMs, not the raw models, is where the broad economic impact comes from. Their abstract puts it as ~15% of tasks completable much faster with an LLM alone, rising to 47–56% once LLM-powered software/tooling is included.

## Who is most exposed

- **Wages:** higher-wage occupations tend to be *more* exposed, "a result contrary to similar evaluations of overall exposure to machine learning" — a reversal of prior automation patterns, though many low-wage occupations are also highly exposed.
- **Employment level:** essentially uncorrelated with exposure.
- **Skills (regression on O*NET Basic Skills, Table 5):** **science and critical-thinking** skills are strongly *negatively* associated with exposure; **programming and writing** skills are strongly *positively* associated (programming coefficient ≈ 0.64 on α, writing ≈ 0.37).
- **Barriers to entry / Job Zones (Table 6):** exposure rises from Job Zone 1 (least preparation) through Job Zone 4, then flattens or dips at Job Zone 5. Median income rises monotonically across zones ($30,230 in Zone 1 to $80,980–$81,980 in Zone 5). Share of workers with >50% β exposure by zone: 0.00% (Z1), 6.11% (Z2), 10.57% (Z3), 34.5% (Z4), 26.45% (Z5).
- **Education (Table 7):** workers with Bachelor's, Master's and professional degrees — and, notably, those with "some college, no degree" — are more exposed than those without formal credentials. Jobs requiring the most on-the-job training tend to be *least* exposed.
- **Most-exposed occupations (Table 4):** examples include interpreters/translators, survey researchers, writers/authors, public relations specialists, mathematicians, tax preparers, financial quantitative analysts, accountants/auditors, and journalists. Humans labelled 15 occupations "fully exposed"; the model labelled 86. This does *not* mean these jobs can be fully automated — only that a large share of their tasks could be done much faster.
- **Industries (Appendix C):** information-processing industries are highly exposed; manufacturing, agriculture and mining are less so. Manual/physical work is largely unexposed to LLMs even with tooling.

## Validation against prior automation measures

Regressing the new LLM-exposure scores on earlier measures (Webb 2020 software/robot/AI patent scores; Brynjolfsson et al.'s Suitability for Machine Learning, SML; Felten et al.'s AI Occupational Exposure; Frey & Osborne automation; Acemoglu–Autor routine cognitive/manual), plus a wage control, yields R² between 60.7% and 72.8% — meaning **28–40% of the variance in LLM exposure is not explained by prior technology-exposure measures**, i.e. LLMs pick out something genuinely new. Software, SML and routine-cognitive scores correlate positively and significantly; Webb's *robot* scores, routine-*manual* content, and Frey & Osborne's overall automation metric correlate *negatively* — reflecting that physical/manual work is not exposed to LLMs.

## General-purpose technology framing (the pun)

A general-purpose technology (GPT), per Lipsey et al. (2005) and Bresnahan & Trajtenberg (1995), must meet three criteria: (1) improvement over time, (2) pervasiveness across the economy, and (3) the ability to spawn complementary innovations. The AI literature already establishes (1); this paper's contribution is evidence for (2) and (3) — pervasive task-level exposure across occupations, and a large exposure boost from complementary software. Hence "GPTs are GPTs." Like electricity or the steam engine, realising the potential requires costly, slow *co-invention* (redesigned business processes and tooling), so diffusion may lag capability. The authors argue the economic effect would "persist and increase even if we halt the development of new capabilities today," and that this pervasiveness makes the trajectory hard for policymakers to predict and regulate.

## Author-stated limitations and caveats

The authors repeatedly stress that **exposure is potential, not realised impact**: it "reflects an estimate of the technical capacity to make human labor more efficient; however, social, economic, regulatory, and other determinants imply that technical feasibility does not guarantee labor productivity or automation outcomes." They make **no predictions about development or adoption timelines**, and note the exposure/augmentation-vs-displacement question is left open. Specific limitations:
- **Subjective labelling** by annotators who are familiar with LLMs but not occupationally diverse — a key validity gap.
- **GPT-4 as classifier** is sensitive to rubric wording, prompt order, and examples; no annotation source is definitive ground truth.
- **Validity of the task-based framework** — if occupations cannot be cleanly decomposed into tasks (e.g. tacit skills, task dependencies), the analysis "would largely be invalidated."
- Human and model tended to get "stuck" on tasks requiring collective habit change (meetings, negotiations), regulated or judgment/empathy-laden tasks (decisions, counselling), and tasks already automatable by other means (reservations).
- **Forward-looking and US-only** — findings may not generalise to other countries; vision/multimodal capability was excluded from direct (α) exposure ratings; theoretical and practical performance may diverge on complex, open-ended, domain-specific tasks.

The paper closes with two playful alliterative "LLM conclusions" (a GPT-4 version and an author-augmented version), underscoring the general-purpose-technology pun.
