# Exposing Limitations of Language Model Agents in Sequential-Task Compositions on the Web — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2311.18751-compositional-limits-web-agents.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2311.18751-compositional-limits-web-agents.pdf) · Hiroki Furuta, Yutaka Matsuo, Aleksandra Faust, Izzeddin Gur (Google DeepMind & The University of Tokyo), 2024 · Transactions on Machine Learning Research (12/2024); arXiv:2311.18751v3 · [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=Y9kAsYIjYc).
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording and figures.

## Abstract

The paper introduces **CompWoB**, a benchmark of 50 compositional web-automation tasks built by stitching together MiniWoB base tasks the agents can already solve, and uses it to show that language model agents (LMAs) which look near-perfect on single tasks collapse when those tasks are chained. It is a deliberate counterweight to the optimistic composability narrative: solving each sub-task well does not imply solving their composition.

**Key points**

- **The headline drop:** prompted LMAs (RCI, AdaPlanner, Synapse on `gpt-3.5-turbo`/`gpt-4`) average **94.0% on base tasks but 24.9% on composed tasks** — a ~69-point collapse, even though the composed tasks are built by chaining base tasks the agents already solve.
- **The counterintuitive reversal:** the *stronger* base-task agents generalise *worse*. Prompted LMAs beat finetuned ones on base tasks yet lose the most on composition; **transferred (finetuned-only) LMAs drop far less, 85.4% → 54.8%**. Base-task skill is anti-correlated with compositional robustness.
- **Order fragility:** merely reversing instruction order ("solve B and C, after A" instead of "A, then B, then C") degrades every agent — transferred 54.8% → 31.0% (−23.8), prompted 24.9% → 18.0% (−6.9).
- **Bigger models help but don't fix it:** `gpt-4` lifts RCI from 28.7% to 56.0% on composition, still ~35 points below its 90.6% base-task score; reverse-order still only 43.5%.
- **What makes composition hard:** compositional difficulty tracks the *product* of base success rates (R=0.691), long instructions (R=−0.579), and deep HTML subtrees (R=−0.433) — not HTML length or element count.

**Takeaways**

- Composition is a distinct capability, not an emergent by-product of per-task competence. Benchmarks that report only single-task success rates systematically overstate how deployable an agent is.
- The failure is largely a *planning/instruction-parsing* failure: agents skip intermediate steps, hallucinate actions/XPaths, and can't re-order sub-instructions — because their exemplars encode left-to-right, single-task assumptions that don't transfer.
- Prompt-engineering and self-improvement loops "over-fit" to the base tasks; robustness to composition needs either much better base LLMs or agent-specialised training with balanced data coverage.

## 1. Introduction

LMAs have emerged as a strong paradigm for multi-step decision-making — robotics, information retrieval, tool use — and in **web automation** (MiniWoB, Shi et al. 2017) prompted LMAs now *outperform humans* and imitation/RL agents. But real-world tasks are open-ended combinations of sub-tasks, and it is impractical to pre-stage prompts and exemplars for every unseen combination. So the realistic deployment assumption is **zero-shot evaluation of off-the-shelf agents**: (1) you cannot cover all possible user requests in advance, and (2) iterative trial-and-error to tune system prompts hurts user experience.

The authors study transferability to sequential task compositions and report the central finding: prompted LMAs drop from **94.0% (base) to 24.9% (compositional)**; transferred LMAs (finetuned only on base tasks) generalise better at **54.8%**. A rebalanced-data model, **HTML-T5++**, reaches human-level MiniWoB (95.2%) and the best zero-shot CompWoB (61.5%). All agents further degrade under **reverse-order instructions** (prompted 6.9% drop vs transferred 23.8% drop). Instruction length and observation (HTML) complexity are useful predictors of compositional difficulty.

**Key contributions:**

- Empirically show (1) prompted LMAs — even with `gpt-4` — generalise to compositional tasks *worse* than transferred LMAs, and (2) LMAs are highly sensitive to instruction order.
- Release **CompWoB**, simulated web environments to measure generalisation to realistic compositionality and complex instructions.
- A new data-mixture heuristic: add demonstrations for data-starved tasks, then gradually cut easy-task ratios to focus on hard tasks. **HTML-T5++** hits 95.2% on MiniWoB and 61.5% zero-shot on CompWoB.
- More capable models (e.g. `gpt-4`) improve results but still struggle with compositionality and complex instructions.

## 2. Related Works

- **Web automation:** early finetuned LMAs (≤3B params) amortise training cost with web priors but are sub-optimal from insufficient data coverage. Recent prompted LMAs with self-refinement (RCI), program synthesis (AdaPlanner), structured instruction-state translation (Synapse), or hierarchical prompts beat finetuned LMAs and humans. This work instead probes their *transferability and robustness* in zero-shot compositional settings, and fixes finetuned-LMA sub-optimality via data-rebalancing.
- **Beyond MiniWoB**, real-world benchmarks exist (WebShop, Mind2Web, WebArena, WorkArena) but their miscellaneous obstacles — complex HTML, domain gaps, ambiguous instructions — make error attribution hard. CompWoB keeps difficulty and ambiguity *controllable*.
- **LMAs generally** act as autonomous agents using context-as-state and sequential tool/actuator manipulation; open question is out-of-domain generalisation, which this paper measures.
- **LLMs for compositional tasks:** prior work on semantic parsing, logic puzzles, math, programming, planning shows dynamic exemplar selection or model scaling can help — but on *static* tasks. This paper targets compositionality in *interactive decision-making*, where web tasks have more explicitly decomposable structure than NL tasks.

## 3. Preliminaries

Web automation is a **deterministic sequential decision problem**: state space *S*, action space *A*, deterministic transition *T: S×A→S*, instruction set *G*, contextual prompts *C*, and episodic (0/1) success reward *r: S×G×A→{0,1}*. At each step the agent π infers an action from prompt, instruction, current state and prior actions. State *s* is raw HTML; the action space is programmatic `function(selector, text)` where `function` ∈ {`click`, `move`, `type`}, `selector` is an integer index or XPath, and `text` is input for `type`.

> [!NOTE]
> **Task compositionality:** a task ψ is characterised by an HTML subtree (Sψ ⊂ S) and instructions (Gψ ⊂ G). Tasks can be dependently combined so long as the composition is executable — e.g. (1) click checkboxes, (2) fill a password form, (3) close a dialog.

## 4. Language Model Agents for Web Automation

Three representative prompted LMAs are reviewed, plus finetuned/transferred LMAs and the authors' data-rebalancing.

| Agent | Core mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **RCI** (Kim et al. 2023) | Recursive Criticism & Improvement: open-loop plan → **ERCI** (explicit self-criticism of the plan) → **IRCI** (implicit grounding/formatting refinement per step, no criticism). Recommended 1 ERCI + 3 IRCI. | First to beat imitation/RL agents on MiniWoB with prompting. Optimal loop counts vary per task. |
| **AdaPlanner** (Sun et al. 2023) | **Program synthesis**: generates an open-loop plan as a Python function (each snippet = an action), then adaptively re-generates remaining steps on environmental feedback (assertion/functional errors, "ask" action). | Code-capable LLMs do better (`text-davinci-003` > `gpt-3.5-turbo`). |
| **Synapse** (Zheng et al. 2023) | **Structured prompts** without self-improvement or code: *state filtering* (raw HTML → Pythonic list/dict) + *task reformulation* (decompose instructions/HTML into stepwise queries, map nouns→XPath). Uses 7 reformulation types, specialised per task. | Strongest on MiniWoB but prompts are task-specialised. |

### 4.4 Finetuned and Transferred LMAs

LMAs finetuned on base tasks are built on pre-trained LMs (T5, Flan-T5, HTML-T5, Pix2Struct); they take HTML/screenshots + prior actions and predict text actions closed-loop. Their web priors let small (≤3B-param) models rival RL agents, enabling cheaper on-premise deployment — but they need *hundreds of thousands* of demonstrations (vs hundreds of episodes for prompted LMAs) and are historically sub-optimal. Evaluated here as **transferred LMAs**: finetuned only on base tasks, then tested zero-shot on unseen compositions.

### 4.5 Data-Rebalancing Improves Finetuned LMAs (HTML-T5++)

The insight: agent-collected demonstrations (10k per task, filtered by success) leave *hard* tasks data-starved because the collector fails them. Fixing this imbalance yields super-human MiniWoB performance. Method:

1. Run Synapse on MiniWoB to collect **77K additional demos across 16 tasks** on top of Furuta et al.'s 347K.
2. Estimate "brute-force" task difficulty by averaging success rates across agents; classify 65 base tasks as **easy (0.8–1.0), medium (0.6–0.8), hard (0.0–0.6)**.
3. Progressively reduce easy-task episode ratios to concentrate on hard tasks.

**Data-mix results on MiniWoB (Table 1):** the best strategy — 424K then removing 50% of easy-task episodes → **282K episodes → 95.2%** — beats the naive 347K (85.6%) and 424K (94.1%) mixes.

| Dataset size | MiniWoB success |
|---|---|
| 347K (original, Furuta et al.) | 85.6% |
| 424K (+77K) | 94.1% |
| 351K (+77K −73K) | 94.6% |
| 322K (+77K −102K) | 94.8% |
| **282K (+77K −142K, remove 50% easy)** | **95.2%** |
| 241K (+77K −183K) | 95.0% |

For reference, comparable-agent MiniWoB scores: RCI 90.6%, AdaPlanner 92.9%, Human 93.5%, CC-Net 93.5%, RCI (gpt-4) 94.0%, Synapse 98.5%. HTML-T5++ is finetuned on HTML-T5-XL.

## 5. Designing CompWoB

CompWoB ("compositional MiniWoB") combines **2–8 base MiniWoB tasks** into a single sequential task (e.g. `click-checkboxes-transfer + enter-password + click-dialog → click-checkboxes-transfer_enter-password_click-dialog`), instructions stitched with "and then". Because the base tasks are already solvable, CompWoB isolates *compositional* difficulty from *base-task* difficulty, and serves as a hold-out **test** environment with MiniWoB as the **train** environment. Tasks are visually simple but reflect real structures (e.g. a login form with agreement checkboxes and a pop-up, like internet banking), including page transitions.

**Design principles:** solvability, feasibility, reality. Rather than auto-generate all combinations (104 base tasks would yield 185,460 two-/three-way combinations, and MiniWoB's per-environment locality makes auto-modularisation infeasible), the authors *manually curate* 50 compositional tasks (+50 reverse-order variants).

**Task categories (50 total):**

| Category | Count | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Two-way | 20 | combine 2 easy base tasks |
| Three-way | 10 | combine 3 |
| n-way | 5 | combine 4–8 sequentially |
| Transition | 5 | explicit page transition (e.g. login form → email browser) |
| Easy-medium two-way | 10 | one easy + one medium base task |

**Reverse-order instructions:** the same task order semantically, but instructions given "upside down" (e.g. "solve B and C, after solving A") — a minimal, controllable stress test of instruction parsing.

**Sub-task dependency:** real sites have dependencies (email needs successful login first). CompWoB encodes these in the reward via logical AND — e.g. a "write email" sub-task done without a successful login yields **zero reward** even if the email step itself succeeds. Dependencies are signalled by connectors ("solve B after A").

**Complexity vs other benchmarks (Table 2):** CompWoB raises complexity above MiniWoB to roughly match Mind2Web (a real-website benchmark).

| Benchmark | Avg episode steps | Max steps | Avg instruction tokens | Max tokens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MiniWoB | 3.6 | 7.0 | 8.5 | 23.2 |
| Mind2Web | 7.7 | 37.0 | 19.2 | 84.0 |
| **CompWoB (ours)** | 7.2 | 17.8 | 37.3 | 100.0 |

CompWoB also uses **randomised** instructions (template-generated) and element-randomised HTML, versus the deterministic instructions of many real-website benchmarks. Real-website benchmarks are noted to yield weak agents anyway (Mind2Web ~5–10% episode success at most; WebArena ~15% at most), making error attribution hard — CompWoB's controllability is the point.

## 6. Results

**Evaluation methodology:** zero-shot — *no* compositional-task demonstrations in the training corpus or exemplars. 50 tasks × 100 episodes each; backbone `gpt-3.5-turbo` unless stated; optimal exemplar retriever assumed; hyper-parameters/prompts borrowed from each source paper with minimal change. Prompting strategies tested: RCI — zero-shot, first-task, second-task, combination (comb) exemplars; Synapse — first-task, second-task, best; AdaPlanner — original-code exemplars where base tasks are covered, else zero-shot. (For >2 base tasks, RCI always uses the first two tasks due to context limits.)

### 6.1 LMAs Struggle to Handle Task Compositionality

All LMAs degrade on CompWoB. The reversal from base-task rankings is the key result: **transferred LMAs average 54.8% vs prompted LMAs 24.9%** — the opposite of MiniWoB, where prompted agents lead.

Per-agent success (MiniWoB → CompWoB), from Figure 2:

| Agent | Type | MiniWoB | CompWoB |
|---|---|---|---|
| WebGUM | transferred | 75.5 | 46.3 |
| HTML-T5 | transferred | 85.6 | 56.6 |
| **HTML-T5++** | transferred | **95.2** | **61.5** |
| RCI (zero) | prompted | 90.6 | 17.9 |
| RCI (1st) | prompted | 90.6 | 22.5 |
| RCI (2nd) | prompted | 90.6 | 25.8 |
| RCI (comb) | prompted | 90.6 | 28.7 |
| AdaPlanner | prompted | 92.9 | 20.6 |
| Synapse (1st) | prompted | 98.5 | 17.8 |
| Synapse (2nd) | prompted | 98.5 | 15.4 |
| Synapse (best) | prompted | 98.5 | 25.4 |
| **Transferred (Ave.)** | | **85.4** | **54.8** |
| **Prompted (Ave.)** | | **94.0** | **24.9** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Even the *best* prompted configuration — RCI with combined-task exemplars — reaches only **28.7%** on CompWoB, down from **90.6%** on base MiniWoB. Synapse, the strongest base-task agent at 98.5%, falls to as low as 15.4%. Per-task competence does not compose.

Among prompted LMAs, RCI > AdaPlanner > Synapse on composition, suggesting **iterative self-criticism is more robust to out-of-domain decision-making** than program synthesis with feedback or structured prompting with state translation.

**Failure modes (Table 3):** LMAs commonly (1) **skip necessary intermediate steps** (instructions get long, so steps get dropped), (2) predict **wrong action types** (Synapse mixes up `click` and `type`), and (3) **hallucinate XPaths** (RCI). Without oracle exemplars they struggle to parse compositional instructions and understand complex HTML.

### 6.2 Reverse-Order Instructions Degrade LMAs

Simply reversing instruction order degrades everything. From Figure 3 (CompWoB → reverse-order):

| Agent | CompWoB | Reverse-order | Drop |
|---|---|---|---|
| WebGUM | 46.3 | 29.1 | −17.2 |
| HTML-T5 | 56.6 | 29.7 | −26.9 |
| HTML-T5++ | 61.5 | 34.3 | −27.2 |
| RCI (zero) | 17.9 | 15.7 | −2.2 |
| RCI (1st) | 22.5 | 17.0 | −5.5 |
| RCI (2nd) | 25.8 | 18.1 | −7.7 |
| RCI (comb) | 28.7 | 19.2 | −9.5 |
| AdaPlanner | 20.6 | 18.9 | −1.7 |
| Synapse (1st) | 17.8 | 9.8 | −8.0 |
| Synapse (2nd) | 15.4 | 12.1 | −3.3 |
| Synapse (best) | 25.4 | 16.0 | −9.4 |
| **Transferred (Ave.)** | **54.8** | **31.0** | **−23.8** |
| **Prompted (Ave.)** | **24.9** | **18.0** | **−6.9** |

Transferred LMAs suffer the larger *absolute and relative* hit (54.8 → 31.0), implying they **don't generalise beyond the training distribution's instruction phrasing**; prompted LMAs are already so low that they have less to lose, and their inter-method differences become marginal.

**Why (Table 4):** base-task exemplars encode **left-to-right** instructions, strongly conditioning the agent to process instructions linearly — but compositional/reverse-order instructions imply *non-linear* processing. The prompt–task mismatch causes agents to mis-order plans, hallucinate unnecessary `type` actions (RCI), or pick wrong action types/XPaths (AdaPlanner, Synapse).

### 6.3 Do Advanced LLMs Solve Compositional Tasks?

Swapping in more capable backbones (`gpt-4` for RCI/Synapse; `text-davinci-003`, a stronger coder, for AdaPlanner) helps but does not close the gap. From Figure 4 (MiniWoB / CompWoB@gpt-3.5 / CompWoB@advanced):

| Agent | MiniWoB | CompWoB (gpt-3.5) | CompWoB (advanced) | Reverse-order (advanced) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RCI (comb) | 90.6 | 28.7 | **56.0** (gpt-4) | 43.5 (gpt-4) |
| AdaPlanner | 92.9 | 20.6 | 29.5 (text-davinci-003) | — |
| Synapse (best) | 98.5 | 25.4 | 41.4 (gpt-4) | — |

> [!WARNING]
> `gpt-4` roughly doubles RCI's composition score (28.7 → 56.0) but leaves it ~35 points below its 90.6% base-task score, and reverse-order still tops out at 43.5%. Even the strongest available general LLM does not make compositional web tasks reliable — "we need much better LLMs to realize deployable systems."

### 6.4 What Determines Task Complexity on the Web?

Correlations (Figure 5) between average agent success and task statistics, across WebGUM, HTML-T5, RCI, AdaPlanner, Synapse (all p < 0.01):

| Statistic | Correlation with success | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesized success rate** (product of base-task success rates) | **R = +0.691** | Compositional difficulty is dominated by base-task difficulties multiplied together. |
| **Instruction tokens** | R = −0.579 | Longer instructions → harder. |
| **Max HTML subtree depth** | R = −0.433 | Deeper HTML → harder. |
| HTML token count / element count / human-synthesized rate | weaker | Length and element count matter less than depth. |

> [!TIP]
> HTML complexity is driven by **depth, not length**. In deeper subtrees, elements near the root become distant after traversal; this sparsity confuses planning. Practical predictors of a hard composed task: many instruction tokens and deep HTML.

## 7. Discussion

- **Generalizable prompting methods:** Synapse and RCI show "over-fitting" to base MiniWoB tasks. Since you cannot pre-tune optimal self-improvement iterations or decomposed prompts for every instruction/composition (even with an optimal retriever), prompt-method *generalisation* deserves more attention than raw base-task scores.
- **Agent-specialized LLMs:** `gpt-4` helps but doesn't reach base-task levels (RCI 90.6 → 56.0; Synapse 98.5 → 41.4). Transferred LMAs improve with balanced data but remain far from sufficient. The path forward: agent-specialised LLMs with enhanced reasoning/generalisation via instruction-tuning or RLHF/AIF, on top of better prompting.
- **Parsing complex instructions to executable plans:** LMAs are fragile even under the simplest reverse-order rephrasing. Requiring users to phrase requests carefully and concisely hurts UX; better planning modules that parse complex instructions into correct executable plans are an open direction.

## 8. Conclusion

The paper reaches a conclusion *contrary to prior optimism*: prompted LMAs are strong single-task solvers but **collapse on unknown compositions**, whereas transferred (finetuned) LMAs are weaker on basics but **handle composition far better**. All LMAs suffer catastrophic degradation from even the simplest reversed-order instructions, and compositional difficulty is driven by instruction length and HTML depth. Building robust, generalisable LMAs for composition is a prerequisite for safe real-world deployment.

**Summary of all conditions (Table 5, average / max success):**

| Agent type | Base MiniWoB | CompWoB | Reverse-order | Advanced models |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Prompted LMA** | 94.0% / 98.5% | **24.9% / 28.7%** | 18.0% / 19.2% | 42.3% / 56.0% |
| **Transferred LMA** | 85.4% / 95.2% | **54.8% / 61.5%** | 31.0% / 34.3% | – |

**Ethics:** capable web-automation assistants could improve productivity and accessibility, but misuse threatens cyber-security and could enable scams; concrete guidelines and regulation are needed.
