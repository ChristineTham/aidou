# Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2310.01798-llms-cannot-self-correct-reasoning-yet.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2310.01798-llms-cannot-self-correct-reasoning-yet.pdf) · Jie Huang, Xinyun Chen, Swaroop Mishra, Huaixiu Steven Zheng, Adams Wei Yu, Xinying Song, Denny Zhou (Google DeepMind; University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign), 2023/2024 · Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2024 · arXiv:2310.01798v2 [cs.CL], 14 Mar 2024.
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording and figures.

## Abstract

This paper critically tests whether LLMs can improve their own reasoning by *self-correcting*, and finds they cannot — not on their own. Its central construct is **intrinsic self-correction**: the model revises its initial answer using only its own capabilities, with **no external feedback** (no ground-truth labels, tools, or human input). The authors show that reported self-correction gains in prior work depend on **oracle labels** (knowing the right answer), and that without those labels intrinsic self-correction leaves reasoning accuracy flat or *worse*.

**Key points**

- **Intrinsic self-correction degrades reasoning.** Across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, and Llama-2, accuracy *drops* after self-correction on every reasoning benchmark tested (GSM8K, CommonSenseQA, HotpotQA). E.g. GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA falls from **75.8 → 38.1** after one round; Llama-2 on GSM8K falls **62.0 → 43.5 → 36.5** over two rounds.
- **The prior "gains" came from oracle labels.** With oracle labels used to decide when to stop, GPT-4 on GSM8K goes **95.5 → 97.5** and GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA **75.8 → 89.7** — but the label is the ground truth itself, so the setting is unrealistic (if you already have the answer, you don't need the model).
- **The failure is that LLMs cannot judge their own reasoning's correctness.** In answer-change analysis, models flip *correct → incorrect* more often than *incorrect → correct*. On GSM8K GPT-3.5, correct→incorrect (8.8%) exceeds incorrect→correct (7.6%); on CommonSenseQA GPT-3.5 it is a stark 39.8% vs 5.8%.
- **Two related evaluation pitfalls inflate self-correction claims:** (1) **multi-agent debate** is really disguised *self-consistency* and underperforms plain self-consistency at equal response budget (debate 83.2 vs self-consistency 85.3 at 6 responses); (2) **sub-optimal initial prompts** — improvement can come from putting task detail in the feedback prompt that should have been in the initial prompt.

**Takeaways**

- Do **not** expect an LLM to fix its own reasoning errors by re-examining its answer with a generic "review your answer" prompt — that tends to make reasoning worse, not better.
- Self-correction **does** work, but only with a **reliable external feedback signal**: a verifier/critic model, code execution against unit tests, external tools (search engines, calculators), or human/oracle feedback. The feedback source, not the "self", is what supplies the gains.
- When evaluating any new self-correction method, control for **inference cost** (compare against self-consistency with the same number of responses) and put **equal effort into the initial prompt** so gains aren't just from a better feedback-side instruction.

## 1. Introduction

- LLMs are powerful text generators but raise concerns about accuracy, reasoning, and safety of outputs.
- **Self-correction** — refining responses based on feedback to prior outputs — has been proposed as a remedy (Madaan et al. 2023 Self-Refine; Shinn et al. 2023 Reflexion; Kim et al. 2023 RCI; Bai et al. 2022; Pan et al. 2023, and others).
- **The paradox the paper attacks:** *If an LLM can self-correct, why doesn't it just give the correct answer the first time?*
- The paper defines **intrinsic self-correction** — the model rectifies its initial responses **based solely on its inherent capabilities, without the crutch of external feedback**. This setting matters because high-quality external feedback is often unavailable in practice, and because it isolates the model's intrinsic capability.
- **Main finding:** contrary to the optimism in prior work, LLMs *struggle* to self-correct reasoning intrinsically; in most cases performance **deteriorates** after self-correction.
- The prior improvements (e.g. Kim et al. 2023; Shinn et al. 2023) come from using **oracle labels** to guide the process; the gains **vanish** when oracle labels are removed.
- Two further measurement problems identified:
  - **Inference-cost fairness:** self-correction uses multiple LLM calls, so it must be compared to baselines with equivalent inference cost. Multi-agent debate (Du et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2023) is no better than **self-consistency** (Wang et al. 2022) at an equal response count.
  - **Prompt design:** claimed gains sometimes stem from a *sub-optimal initial prompt* — the feedback prompt smuggles in task information the initial prompt lacked. Folding that information into the initial instruction beats self-correcting.

## 2. Background and Related Work

- Self-correction asks whether models can **recognize the correctness of their own outputs** and produce refined answers. Ideal case: the model solves a math problem, makes a step error, notices it, revisits, corrects, and produces a better solution.
- The literature's definition of "self-correction" **varies**, causing ambiguity. The pivotal distinction (per Pan et al. 2023) is the **source of feedback**:

> [!NOTE]
> **Internal feedback** relies on the model's own knowledge and parameters to reassess its outputs. **External feedback** incorporates inputs from humans, other models (critic/verifier models), or external tools and knowledge sources (search engines, calculators, code executors).

- Prior reasoning work (Kim et al. 2023 RCI; Shinn et al. 2023 Reflexion) uses **oracle labels** about answer correctness to guide correction — but such high-quality external feedback is usually unavailable in practice, and ideally the LLM itself should judge correctness.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The paper's operating definition: **intrinsic self-correction = self-correction without any external or human feedback.** Unless explicitly stated (e.g. "with oracle feedback"), all uses of "self-correction" in the paper mean intrinsic self-correction.

- Roadmap of the critique: existing techniques (i) **decrease** reasoning performance when oracle labels are not used (§3); (ii) perform **worse** than non-self-correction methods at the same number of model responses (§4); and (iii) give less effective outcomes when informative initial prompts are used (§5).

**Table 1 — Summary of issues in prior self-correction evaluation**

| Method | Issue |
| --- | --- |
| RCI (Kim et al. 2023); Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023) | Use of oracle labels (§3) |
| Multi-Agent Debate (Du et al. 2023) | Unfair comparison to self-consistency (§4) |
| Self-Refine (Madaan et al. 2023) | Sub-optimal prompt design (§5) |

## 3. LLMs Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Intrinsically

### 3.1 Experimental setup

- **Benchmarks** (chosen because prior oracle-label self-correction showed big gains on them):
  - **GSM8K** (Cobbe et al. 2021): 1,319 grade-school math word problems. Kim et al. (2023) reported ~7% gain from self-correction.
  - **CommonSenseQA** (Talmor et al. 2019): multiple-choice commonsense; dev set of 1,221 questions. Kim et al. (2023) reported ~15% gain.
  - **HotpotQA** (Yang et al. 2018): open-domain multi-hop QA, closed-book, 100 questions, exact-match metric; Shinn et al. (2023) reported big gains.
- **Models:** GPT-3.5-Turbo (`gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`) and GPT-4 (accessed 2023/08/29) for the oracle-label runs; additionally GPT-4-Turbo (`gpt-4-1106-preview`) and Llama-2 (`Llama-2-70b-chat`) for intrinsic self-correction.
- **Sampling:** GPT-3.5-Turbo uses the full set; other models use 200 randomly sampled questions per dataset (100 for HotpotQA) to reduce cost. Max **two rounds** of self-correction. Temperature 1 for GPT-3.5 and GPT-4; temperature 0 for GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2 (to span decoding algorithms).
- **Three-step prompting** (following Kim et al. 2023; Shinn et al. 2023):
  1. Prompt for an **initial generation** (this also serves as the *Standard Prompting* baseline).
  2. Prompt the model to **review** its generation and produce feedback.
  3. Prompt the model to **answer again with the feedback**.
- A default feedback prompt for GPT-4-Turbo/Llama-2: *"Assume that this answer could be either correct or incorrect. Review the answer carefully and report any serious problems you find."*

### 3.2 Results

**Self-correction with oracle labels** (stop the loop once the ground-truth-verified answer is correct):

**Table 2 — GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 with oracle labels**

| | GSM8K | CommonSenseQA | HotpotQA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GPT-3.5 Standard | 75.9 | 75.8 | 26.0 |
| GPT-3.5 Self-Correct (Oracle) | **84.3** | **89.7** | **29.0** |
| GPT-4 Standard | 95.5 | 82.0 | 49.0 |
| GPT-4 Self-Correct (Oracle) | **97.5** | **85.5** | **59.0** |

> [!CAUTION]
> These gains are an artifact of the oracle. For math problems, having the ground-truth label makes deploying the LLM pointless — the numbers are only "indicative of an oracle's performance," not of genuine self-correction ability.

**Intrinsic self-correction** (no labels; the model must decide on its own when to stop / whether to keep its answer): **accuracy drops for all models across all benchmarks.**

**Table 3 — GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, intrinsic**

| | # calls | GSM8K | CommonSenseQA | HotpotQA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GPT-3.5 Standard | 1 | 75.9 | 75.8 | 26.0 |
| GPT-3.5 Self-Correct (round 1) | 3 | 75.1 | 38.1 | 25.0 |
| GPT-3.5 Self-Correct (round 2) | 5 | 74.7 | 41.8 | 25.0 |
| GPT-4 Standard | 1 | 95.5 | 82.0 | 49.0 |
| GPT-4 Self-Correct (round 1) | 3 | 91.5 | 79.5 | 49.0 |
| GPT-4 Self-Correct (round 2) | 5 | 89.0 | 80.0 | 43.0 |

**Table 4 — GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2, intrinsic**

| | # calls | GSM8K | CommonSenseQA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GPT-4-Turbo Standard | 1 | 91.5 | 84.0 |
| GPT-4-Turbo Self-Correct (round 1) | 3 | 88.0 | 81.5 |
| GPT-4-Turbo Self-Correct (round 2) | 5 | 90.0 | 83.0 |
| Llama-2 Standard | 1 | 62.0 | 64.0 |
| Llama-2 Self-Correct (round 1) | 3 | 43.5 | 37.5 |
| Llama-2 Self-Correct (round 2) | 5 | 36.5 | 36.5 |

- The **CommonSenseQA collapse for GPT-3.5 (75.8 → 38.1)** and the **Llama-2 GSM8K collapse (62.0 → 36.5)** are the most dramatic degradations.
- **Different feedback prompts don't rescue it.** The authors tried three feedback prompts on GPT-4-Turbo (Table 5) and Llama-2 (Table 6); performance varied by prompt but *every* prompt still failed to beat standard prompting. Even the gentlest phrasing (*"Verify whether your answer is correct, and provide an explanation"*) gave GPT-4-Turbo 91.0/91.0 on GSM8K vs 91.5 standard, and hurt Llama-2 badly (GSM8K 62.0 → 58.0 → 41.5).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Core empirical claim:** without oracle labels, intrinsic self-correction *consistently decreases* reasoning performance — across four model families, three benchmarks, multiple feedback prompts, and both greedy (temp 0) and sampled (temp 1) decoding.

### 3.3 Why does performance decrease?

**Empirical analysis (Figure 1 — answer changes after two rounds):**

For each model/benchmark, the paper classifies outcomes as No Change, Correct⇒Incorrect, Incorrect⇒Correct, Incorrect⇒Incorrect.

| Model / Benchmark | No Change | Correct⇒Incorrect | Incorrect⇒Correct | Incorrect⇒Incorrect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GSM8K (GPT-3.5) | 74.7% | 8.8% | 7.6% | 8.9% |
| CommonSenseQA (GPT-3.5) | 42.8% | 39.8% | 5.8% | 11.6% |
| GSM8K (GPT-4) | 90.5% | 8.0% | 1.5% | — |
| CommonSenseQA (GPT-4) | 90.5% | 5.0% | 1.5% | 3.0% |
| GSM8K (GPT-4-Turbo) | 96.0% | 2.5% | 0.5% | 1.0% |
| CommonSenseQA (GPT-4-Turbo) | 88.0% | 6.0% | 1.0% | 5.0% |
| GSM8K (Llama-2) | 40.0% | 31.0% | 5.5% | 23.5% |
| CommonSenseQA (Llama-2) | 40.0% | 35.5% | 8.0% | 16.5% |

Readings from the figure:

- On GSM8K, GPT-3.5 keeps its answer 74.7% of the time; among the rest it is **more likely to change a correct answer to an incorrect one than the reverse** (8.8% vs 7.6%). *"The fundamental issue is that LLMs cannot properly judge the correctness of their reasoning."*
- On CommonSenseQA, GPT-3.5 changes its answer far more often (No Change only 42.8%) with a huge **39.8% correct⇒incorrect**; false options often look relevant, so the review prompt biases the model to switch. Llama-2 behaves similarly (35.5% correct⇒incorrect).
- **GPT-4 and GPT-4-Turbo change answers least** (No Change 88–96%), likely because they have higher confidence in initial answers and are more robust to the review-prompt bias — which is *why* they degrade less, not because they correct well (incorrect⇒correct stays tiny, 0.5–1.5%).
- (HotpotQA change-analysis is omitted: the source paper's sample size of 100 is too small for meaningful statistics.)

**Figure 2 examples (GSM8K, GPT-3.5):** one case where review turns a wrong "$18 gift bags" answer into the correct "$24" (success), and one where review turns a correct "$75.00 yogurt" answer into the wrong "$37.50" (failure) — illustrating the two-way flipping.

- Note on Table 2: the oracle-label runs use ground truth precisely to **prevent** the correct⇒incorrect changes — and *preventing those mischanges is the whole crux* of making self-correction work. Intrinsic self-correction lacks that guardrail.

**Intuitive explanation.** If the model is well-aligned and the initial prompt is well-designed, the initial response is already near-optimal for that prompt and decoding algorithm. A feedback prompt is effectively an *extra* prompt that skews the model toward a response tailored to the combined input; for reasoning it offers no extra advantage and can bias the model **away** from the optimal response, causing the drop.

## 4. Multi-Agent Debate Does Not Outperform Self-Consistency

- Multi-agent debate (Du et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2023a): multiple instances of one LLM critique and debate each other's answers. The authors replicate Du et al. (2023) with `gpt-3.5-turbo-0301`, **3 agents, 2 rounds**, on the *full* GSM8K test set (Du et al. used only 100 examples).
- Baseline: **self-consistency** (Wang et al. 2022) — sample multiple responses, majority-vote.

**Table 7 — Multi-agent debate vs self-consistency (GSM8K)**

| Method | # responses | GSM8K |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard Prompting | 1 | 76.7 |
| Self-Consistency | 3 | 82.5 |
| Multi-Agent Debate (round 1) | 6 | 83.2 |
| Self-Consistency | 6 | **85.3** |
| Multi-Agent Debate (round 2) | 9 | 83.0 |
| Self-Consistency | 9 | **88.2** |

- Both beat standard prompting, but **at equal response budget, self-consistency wins** (85.3 vs 83.2 at 6 responses; 88.2 vs 83.0 at 9). Multi-agent debate is only marginally better than self-consistency at 3 responses.
- **Interpretation:** multi-agent "debate" is better understood as a route to **consistency** across generations — its mechanism mirrors self-consistency, differing only in whether voting is model-driven or count-based. The gains are from *self-consistency*, **not** from *self-correction*. To argue LLMs self-correct via debate, one must exclude the effect of selecting among multiple generations.

## 5. Prompt Design Issues in Self-Correction Evaluation

- If a task's criteria can be stated in the initial instruction (e.g. "output must contain certain words," "code must be efficient," "sentiment positive"), the fair comparison puts those criteria **in the initial prompt**, not only in the feedback prompt. Otherwise an apparent gain may just be the extra instruction, not the self-correction step.
- **Case study — Constrained Generation (from Madaan et al. 2023 Self-Refine):** generate a coherent sentence using all of 20–30 given concepts; metric is concept coverage. Madaan et al.'s original initial prompt **does not clearly state** that all concepts must be included; self-correction "helps" by having the model find missing concepts and add them.
- The authors add one line to the initial prompt — *"Write a reasonable paragraph that includes \*ALL\* of the above concepts"* — and this stronger baseline beats Self-Refine's post-correction result.

**Table 8 — Constrained Generation (CommonGen-Hard, concept coverage)**

| Method | # calls | CommonGen-Hard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard Prompting* | 1 | 44.0* |
| Self-Correct* | 7 | 67.0* |
| Standard Prompting* | 1 | 53.0 |
| Self-Correct* | 7 | 61.1 |
| **Standard Prompting (ours)** | 1 | **81.8** |
| Self-Correct* (on ours) | 7 | 75.1 |

<sub>* Prompts/results from Madaan et al. (2023) or their prompts replicated with `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`.</sub>

- The authors' improved initial prompt (**81.8**) far exceeds Madaan et al.'s self-corrected result (67.0), and applying the self-correction prompt on top of the stronger baseline **again drops** performance (81.8 → 75.1). The "gain" was a prompt-design artifact.

## 6. Conclusion and Discussion

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Main conclusion:** current LLMs struggle to self-correct their reasoning **without external feedback**. Expecting them to inherently recognize and fix their own reasoning mistakes is "overly optimistic so far." Approach self-correction claims with a discerning eye.

The paper's guidance on when/how self-correction *does* work and how to evaluate it fairly:

- **Leverage external feedback for correction.** When valid external feedback is available, use it:
  - **Code execution / unit tests** as a *perfect verifier* — Chen et al. (2023b) self-debugging: feeding execution results into the feedback prompt significantly improves code generation.
  - **External tools** — Gou et al. (2023) CRITIC: LLMs verify and correct better when interacting with search engines, calculators, etc.
  - **Trained verifier / critic models** on high-quality data — Cobbe et al. (2021); Lightman et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023b Shepherd) — to verify or refine outputs and supply feedback.
  - **Human feedback** — we often guide LLMs ourselves toward desired content.
  - Enabling LLMs to interact with the environment and learn from available feedback is a promising direction.
- **Evaluate against baselines with comparable inference cost.** Self-correction adds LLM calls; always include an inference-cost analysis and compare to strong multi-response baselines like **self-consistency**. A further implication: develop models (via alignment) more likely to decode the optimal solution directly, avoiding the need for multiple generations.
- **Put equal effort into prompt design.** Include the complete task description in the initial prompt rather than reserving part of it for feedback; invest equal effort in both, or results mislead.

## 7. Limitations and Broader Impact

- **Scope:** the work evaluates **reasoning**. Self-correction may still help in **other domains** — e.g. aligning responses to preferences, altering style, or improving **safety** (Bai et al. 2022 Constitutional AI; Ganguli et al. 2023 moral self-correction; Madaan et al. 2023).

> [!NOTE]
> **Key distinction:** LLMs can adequately assess *some* properties of their responses — e.g. whether a response is inappropriate/unsafe (Ganguli et al. 2023) — but they **struggle to identify errors in their own reasoning**. Self-assessment ability is domain-dependent.

- Consistent with prior findings: LLM self-correction weakens substantially without external feedback (Gou et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023a) and is easily biased by **misleading feedback** (Wang et al. 2023a).
- **Call to action:** ambiguity persists in the community — some papers bury label-usage details or fail to clarify that their "self-correction" actually uses external feedback. The paper urges a critical perspective and research into methods that *genuinely* enhance reasoning.

**Reproducibility:** experiments use GPT-3.5 / GPT-4 via the OpenAI API and open-source Llama-2; specific kernels (e.g. `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`) and access times are reported; prompts from prior work are reused and the authors' own prompts are in Appendix A.
