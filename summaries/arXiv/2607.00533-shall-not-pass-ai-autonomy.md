## Abstract

A mixed-methods study of **448 professional developers at Microsoft** maps where developers accept AI autonomy across software-engineering work. Most accepted AI producing work **under their oversight** (median accepted level L3), but acceptance was lowest for **identity-defining, human-facing, and design-oriented** work. Two cognitive appraisals hold the line: **task accountability** lowers willingness to let AI *act* (produce artifacts), and **task identity** lowers willingness to let AI *decide* on the developer's behalf. Task demand pushes the other way (more delegation of decisions); more AI experience and risk tolerance raise accepted autonomy.

**Key points**

- **N = 448 professional developers** at Microsoft (an AI-forward firm with 50,000+ developers), yielding **1,535 open-ended task-responses** (1,476 with complete predictor data for the models).
- **Mixed-methods design:** an open-ended survey (from a random-stratified sample of 8,000; 1,193 responses, 14.9% rate, filtered to 448); responses classified onto a **five-level autonomy scale** (Endsley & Kiris: L1 none → L5 full automation) by a rule-based algorithm run through an **AI council of three frontier LLMs** (gpt-5.4, gemini-3-flash, claude-sonnet-4.6) reconciled by majority vote; human validation (κ = 0.94; human–council κ = 0.95/0.92; three-rater α = 0.93); mixed-effects regression (CLMM for level, GLMM at each boundary) plus reflexive thematic analysis.
- **Two boundaries:** the **action boundary (L2→L3)** — AI moves from advising to producing artifacts; and the **decision-making boundary (L3→L4)** — the developer's sign-off goes from required to optional (veto only).
- **Main determinants of willingness to grant autonomy:**
  - **Task Accountability** ↓ crossing the *action* boundary (OR = 0.82, p = .027) — feeling answerable makes developers unwilling to let AI produce the artifact.
  - **Task Identity** ↓ overall accepted autonomy (β = −0.13, p = .017) and ↓ crossing the *decision-making* boundary (OR = 0.78, p = .01) — developers keep the work that defines them.
  - **Task Demand** ↑ crossing the decision-making boundary (OR = 1.20, p = .03) — heavy workload increases willingness to hand off decisions.
  - **AI experience** and **risk tolerance** ↑ accepted autonomy (β = 0.26, p = .007; β = 0.33, p = .013) and ease crossing the decision boundary.
  - **Task type:** lowest autonomy for Meta-work (OR = 0.33) and Design & Planning (OR = 0.42) vs. Development; higher for Quality & Risk (OR = 1.67).
- Notably, **Task Value and (raw) SE Experience and Technophilia did *not* predict** accepted autonomy.

**Takeaways**

- Preferences for AI autonomy reflect **how developers cognitively experience their work**, not just tool capability or trust. Accountability governs "may AI *act*?"; identity governs "may AI *decide*?".
- The authors offer a **"Cascading Locks"** model of meaningful-work design — accountability is the first gate (action boundary), identity the second (decision boundary), with AI fluency, risk tolerance, and demand as the "water" that lifts autonomy — plus anti-patterns (deferred answerability, throughput stampede, shrinking fence, hollow orchestrator, severed pipeline) to design against.

## Introduction

The question has shifted from *whether* AI can do software work to *should it, which parts, and how much*. AI moves faster and reaches deeper, so a single decision to defer has downstream effects: developers can miss the work that builds skill and end up "rubber-stamping" work they no longer understand; defects pass through many hands, becoming costlier to fix and harder to attribute. Prior work studied AI *adoption* and *where developers want support*, but not **where they want control to remain human**. Building on Endsley's levels of automation, the paper distinguishes the **action boundary** (AI acts/produces artifacts) from the **decision-making boundary** (AI decides on the developer's behalf), and asks:

- **RQ1.** What level of AI autonomy do developers accept across SE tasks, and what predicts where they draw the line?
- **RQ2.** What predicts when developers let AI cross the action and decision-making boundaries?

## Theory and Hypotheses

Adopts the **five-level Endsley & Kiris autonomy scale** (who decides, who acts):

| Level | Developer | AI |
|---|---|---|
| L1 · None | Decide, Act | – |
| L2 · Decision Support | Decide, Act | Suggest |
| L3 · Consensual | Decide, Act | Act (takes effect after sign-off) |
| L4 · Monitored | Veto | Decide, Act (by default) |
| L5 · Full Automation | – | Decide, Act |

Drawing on **cognitive appraisal theory**, work design, and automation research, four task appraisals are hypothesised as predictors:

- **H1 Task Value** (importance) → lowers accepted autonomy. *(Not supported.)*
- **H2 Task Identity** (identification/craft) → lowers accepted autonomy. *(Supported.)*
- **H3 Task Accountability** (felt responsibility) → lowers accepted autonomy. *(Supported only at the action boundary.)*
- **H4 Task Demand** (cognitive effort) → *raises* accepted autonomy. *(Supported only at the decision boundary.)*
- **H5** Accepted autonomy differs across task types. *(Supported.)*
- **H6** Value/identity/accountability lower, demand raises, the odds of crossing each boundary. *(Partially supported.)*

Controls: SE and AI experience; individual traits (risk tolerance, technophilia).

## Method

- **Setting:** Microsoft; survey following Choudhuri et al.'s appraisal design over a grounded SDLC task taxonomy (Development; Design & Planning; Quality & Risk; Infrastructure & Ops; Meta-work).
- **Instrument:** validated scales for value, identity, accountability, demands, risk tolerance, technophilia; two open-ended questions per category ("where do you want AI to play the biggest role…" / "what aspects do you *not* want AI to handle and why?"). 10–12 min; attention checks; randomised order; piloted with 40 developers.
- **Sampling:** emailed 8,000 random-stratified developers → 1,193 responses (14.9%). Dropped incomplete (152), patterned (59), failed attention (98), no AI experience (24), unsubstantive open-text (412) → **448 developers, 1,535 task-responses** (1,476 with complete predictors).
- **Classification:** rule-based algorithm (marker + hyponym tables) mapping each Want/Resist pair to the highest tolerated level; ~8% got boundary labels (e.g. L2–L3), treated as interval-censored in models. Run via an **AI council** of three frontier LLMs from different providers (gpt-5.4, gemini-3-flash, claude-sonnet-4.6), majority-vote reconciled; validated by two human researchers (one external, to guard circularity): κ = 0.94 (researcher–researcher), 0.95/0.92 (human–council), α = 0.93 (three-rater).
- **Modelling:** CLMM (ordinal, five-level) for RQ1; two logistic GLMMs (action L2→L3; decision L3→L4) for RQ2; z-standardised predictors, participant random intercept; all VIF < 2. Robustness checks (linear mixed model; binary ≤L3 vs. >L3) reproduced directions and significance.

## Results

### RQ1 — Accepted autonomy levels

- **Finding 1:** Most developers accepted AI-produced work **without ceding decision-making** (≤L3). Median accepted level = **L3**; **74% of responses at or below L3**, 26% above (L4 optional-veto 10%; L5 full automation 16%).
- **Finding 2:** In **system-facing** work (Development; Quality & Risk; Infrastructure & Ops), all three categories had a median of L3 — AI as a collaborator under approval; bounded execution/verification delegated, context-sensitive judgment retained.
- **Finding 3:** In **design and human-facing** work, median accepted level = **L2** (decision support only); mentoring, sensitive client communication, learning, and creative thinking often retained entirely at **L1**. The distinction was toil/automatable vs. work needing interpretation, judgment, empathy, accountability — not technical vs. non-technical.

**Predictors of accepted autonomy (CLMM, n = 1,476):**

| Predictor | Effect | Stat |
|---|---|---|
| **Task Identity** | ↓ accepted autonomy | β = −0.13, p = .017 (H2 supported) |
| Task Value | n.s. | p = .373 (H1 not supported) |
| Task Accountability | n.s. overall | p = .276 |
| Task Demand | n.s. overall | p = .373 |
| **AI Experience** | ↑ | β = 0.26, p = .007 |
| **Risk Tolerance** | ↑ | β = 0.33, p = .013 |
| SE Experience | n.s. | p = .393 |
| Technophilia | n.s. | p = .898 |
| Design & Planning (vs. Development) | ↓ | OR = 0.42, p < .001 |
| Meta-work (vs. Development) | ↓ (strongest resistance) | OR = 0.33, p < .001 |
| Quality & Risk (vs. Development) | ↑ | OR = 1.67, p = .004 |
| Infrastructure & Ops | n.s. | p = .053 |

- **Finding 4:** Of the four appraisals, only **Task Identity** predicted accepted autonomy, negatively — developers keep the tasks that define them professionally even when they accept AI *could* do them.
- **Finding 5:** **AI experience and risk tolerance** were positively associated with accepted autonomy; SE experience and technophilia were not.
- **Finding 6:** **Task type** mattered — Meta-work and Design & Planning drew the most resistance; Quality & Risk the least (verification already embedded, lowering perceived cost of AI action).

### RQ2 — Crossing the boundaries (GLMMs)

| Predictor | Action boundary (L2→L3) | Decision boundary (L3→L4) |
|---|---|---|
| **Task Accountability** | **OR = 0.82*** (lowers odds) | 0.91 n.s. |
| **Task Identity** | 0.89 n.s. | **OR = 0.78*** (lowers odds) |
| **Task Demand** | 0.97 n.s. | **OR = 1.20*** (raises odds) |
| Task Value | n.s. | n.s. |
| AI Experience | 1.27 (marginal) | **OR = 1.29*** |
| Risk Tolerance | 1.40 (marginal) | **OR = 1.46*** |

- **Finding 7:** **Accountability lowers the odds of crossing the action boundary** (OR = 0.82, p = .027). When developers feel accountable, they won't let AI produce reusable artifacts (though they welcome suggestions): "marking my approval puts my name on it and makes me partially responsible for it" (P120). This is where automation errors become attributable to the individual.
- **Finding 8:** At the decision boundary, **identity and demand pull opposite ways** — Task Identity lowers the odds of crossing (OR = 0.78, p = .01: keep judgment over identity-relevant work), while Task Demand raises them (OR = 1.20, p = .03: heavy workload makes offloading decisions appealing — "there is simply too much work to do to want to keep doing it myself," P56).
- **Finding 9:** **AI experience (OR = 1.29) and risk tolerance (OR = 1.46)** raise the odds of crossing the decision boundary — traits distinguish how readily developers grant authority once appraisals set whether they retain it.
- **Summary:** accountability governs whether AI may *produce the artifact*; identity and demand govern whether AI may *decide without sign-off*; AI experience and risk tolerance ease the crossing.

## Cascading Locks of Meaningful Work Design

A framing metaphor: the "vessel" is AI's autonomy on one task; canal locks raise it a gate at a time. **Accountability** is the first gate (action boundary), **identity** the second (decision boundary); they cascade in order. **AI fluency and risk tolerance** are the "water" that lifts the vessel; **demand** adds a second inflow at the upper gate. The paper names anti-patterns to design against (Table 6):

- **Accountability retention** → *deferred answerability* / *right-shifted quality*: automating review moves answerability from before-commit to after-failure. Design fixes: make the L4 veto require real engagement (not a rubber stamp); hold developers answerable for *how* they reviewed, not just the shipped product.
- **Demand differentiation** → *throughput stampede*: volume metrics conflate toil with judgment-building work, so deadlines offload both. Fix: measure the two demands separately; route toil to AI, protect judgment-building work.
- **Identity evolution** → *shrinking fence* / *hollow orchestrator* / *expertise commoditization* / *thought homogenization*: anchoring craft on "what AI can't do yet" is unstable. Fix: define the core by the standard of *judgment* the role requires, written into role definitions and promotion criteria.
- **Identity formation** → *severed pipeline* / *cognitive debt* / *mentoring-by-bot*: automating entry-level work removes the scaffolding juniors need. Fix: keep a share of entry work as practice, not throughput.

## Conclusion

The two boundaries answer to different appraisals: **accountability** predicts whether AI may act/produce artifacts; **identity and demand** predict whether it may decide on the developer's behalf. Because these reflect how developers relate to their work, the accepted level shifts as AI capability, experience, and the work co-evolve. A design response pinned to a fixed list of tasks to withhold is brittle; one grounded in meaningful-work design endures. Boundaries should be set deliberately through role/workflow design rather than left to tool defaults.

**Threats to validity:** cross-sectional (associations, not causation); self-selection and self-report; single AI-forward organisation limits generalisability (though its pro-AI culture, if anything, biases toward a conservative read of retained autonomy); inferring level from brief free text is imperfect (mitigated by the AI council, external recoder, and qualitative triangulation).

## Relation to the book

This paper is a strong, quantitative anchor for **§4.3.2 *Delegation and Review*** and **§4.1.4 (accountability)**. It shows *where* professional developers refuse to grant AI authority and, crucially, *why* — separating two distinct refusals. **Accountability** ("my name goes on it") is what makes developers keep AI at suggestion-level and produce the artifact themselves — direct support for the book's argument that accountability cannot be delegated. **Identity** (task identity, craft, human-facing/design work) is what makes them keep the *decision* rather than accept an optional veto — supporting the case that delegation-with-review has principled limits. The five-level Endsley–Kiris scale and the action-vs-decision boundary distinction give the book a precise vocabulary for "delegation and review," and the "Cascading Locks" anti-patterns (deferred answerability, the L4 veto degrading into a rubber stamp, the hollow orchestrator) illustrate exactly the failure modes the discipline of review is meant to prevent. Cite N = 448, the L3 median (74% at ≤L3), and the accountability/identity odds ratios (0.82 at the action boundary; 0.78 at the decision boundary), with the single-organisation caveat.
