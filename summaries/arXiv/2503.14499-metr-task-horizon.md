# Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2503.14499-metr-task-horizon.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2503.14499-metr-task-horizon.pdf) — Thomas Kwa, Ben West, et al. (Model Evaluation & Threat Research, METR), *Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks*, arXiv preprint arXiv:2503.14499 (v3, 25 Feb 2026); accepted at NeurIPS 2025, 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499
> This is an empirical research paper from METR, an AI-evaluation non-profit. It proposes a new capability metric and reports a trend line; the forward projections are the authors' own extrapolations, which they explicitly flag as uncertain. NOTE ON VERSIONS: the original March 2025 release reported the best model then (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) at a ~50-minute 50% time horizon; this later version (v3) evaluates newer models and reports o3 at ~110 minutes. The 7-month doubling trend is stable across versions.

## Abstract

The paper argues that raw benchmark scores tell us little about what an AI system can actually do in the real world, and proposes a human-anchored metric to fix that: the **50%-task-completion time horizon** — the length of task, measured by how long human professionals typically take to do it, that a model can complete with a 50% success rate. METR timed skilled humans on 170 software and research tasks (RE-Bench, HCAST, and 66 new short "software atomic action" tasks), ran 12 frontier models from 2019–2025 against those tasks, and fit a logistic model to find each model's 50% horizon. The headline finding: this horizon has been **doubling roughly every seven months since 2019** (measured doubling time 207 days), with the best model at the time of this version, o3, sitting at about a 110-minute horizon (GPT-2, by contrast, was ~2 seconds). The single most citable sentence: *"frontier AI time horizon has doubled approximately every seven months since 2019, though the trend may have accelerated since 2024."* It matters for a chapter on AI's future because it turns a slippery question ("how capable is AI getting, and how fast?") into a single, human-legible, exponentially rising number — and because naive extrapolation of that number puts month-long autonomous software work somewhere around 2028–2030.

## The metric: task-completion time horizon

Existing benchmarks have three problems the authors want to sidestep: they use artificial rather than economically valuable tasks; they are often adversarially selected for what current models fail at (biasing the human comparison); and individual benchmarks saturate quickly, leaving no common yardstick across models of "vastly different capabilities (e.g., GPT-2 versus Claude 3.7)."

Their proposal: track progress by the **task completion time horizon** — the duration of tasks a model can complete at a given success probability. Because a model won't reliably complete *all* tasks of a given length, they operationalise this as the **X%-time horizon**: the length of task (in human time) the model completes about X% of the time. The main metric is the **50% time horizon**.

## Method

**Task suite (170 tasks, wide difficulty range):**
- A subset of **HCAST**: 97 diverse software tasks, 1 minute to 30 hours.
- **RE-Bench**: 7 difficult ML research-engineering tasks, all 8 hours long.
- **Software Atomic Actions (SW AA)**: 66 new single-step tasks (1–30 seconds) representing short segments of developer work, added so the suite can measure pre-2023 models.

Example tasks by length (Table 1): "which file is a shell script?" (3 seconds); simple Wikipedia research (1 minute); fix a bug in molecular-dynamics input files (9 minutes); write a JSON-transform script by inferring rules from examples (56 minutes); write custom CUDA kernels to speed up a backtesting tool ~30x (8 hours).

**Human baselining:** over 800 human baselines totalling **2,529 hours**. 558 baselines (286 successful) from HCAST and RE-Bench; 249 (236 successful) from SW AA. 148 of 169 tasks have human baselines; 21 HCAST tasks rely on researcher estimates. Baseliners were skilled professionals in software engineering, ML, and cybersecurity, mostly from top-100 universities, with ~5 years average relevant experience. A task's "length" is the geometric mean time of successful human baselines.

**Model evaluation:** 12 frontier (plus 4 near-frontier) models from 2019–2025, ~8 runs per agent/task pair. Continuous scores are binarised at a task-specific threshold representing human performance.

**Fitting the horizon:** a logistic regression (inspired by Item Response Theory), `p_success(agent, task) = σ((log h_agent − log t_task) · β_agent)`, where `h_agent` is the learned 50% time horizon. Success rate falls off with task length in a way well fit by an exponential (R² ≈ 0.80 regressing model success against log human time). Tasks were weighted by the inverse square root of their family size to stop large families dominating.

## The headline trend

Regressing log(time horizon) against model release date gives a **doubling time of 207 days (~7 months)**, 95% bootstrap CI **166–240 days (roughly ±19%)**, over the six years 2019 to early 2025. Error bars on individual model horizons are wide but highly correlated across models (sampling easy vs. hard tasks shifts all estimates together), so the authors are more confident in the *slope* than in any single model's horizon.

Endpoints: **GPT-2 ≈ 2 seconds; o3 ≈ 110 minutes**, with o3 succeeding at several tasks over 4 hours. o3 lies significantly above the long-run trend line (p = 0.006), which the authors say "may imply the trend in 2024 and early 2025 is faster." Excluding SW AA, the 2023–2025 horizon growth is about 20% faster than the 2019–2025 rate.

**80% vs 50%:** the 80%-success-rate horizon shows a near-identical doubling time (204 days) but is **4–6x shorter** in absolute terms — i.e. models that occasionally succeed on hard tasks still can't do moderate-length tasks *reliably*. The data are too limited to measure horizons at very high (e.g. 95%) success rates.

## What drives the improvement (qualitative analysis)

From transcripts of 31 failed GPT-4-1106 runs and 32 failed o1 runs, the gains appear driven by **greater reliability, better ability to adapt to mistakes (rather than repeating failed actions), stronger logical reasoning/code generation, and improved tool use**. Failure categories shifted: GPT-4's failures were dominated by "repeating failed actions" (12 of 31); o1's by "premature task abandonment" (16 of 32). Models still struggle in "messier" environments — those without clear feedback loops, or where the agent must proactively seek out information.

## External validity and robustness

The authors run three checks because their tasks don't perfectly represent average real-world work:
1. **SWE-bench Verified:** applying the same method to this industry-standard benchmark reproduces an exponential trend, but with a *shorter* doubling time (~70 days vs ~143 days for their own 2024 models). They attribute the steeper slope partly to SWE-bench's annotator time estimates underestimating how long contractors actually take on the easiest tasks.
2. **"Messiness" scoring:** tasks scored against 16 messiness factors (resource-limited, novel, dynamic environment, etc.). Models perform worse on messier tasks at a given length, but the *trend over time* is similar for low- and high-messiness subsets — no evidence of a messiness-specific plateau.
3. **Internal pull requests (uncontaminated):** human speed varied enormously (contractors took 5–18x longer than repo maintainers). AI performance was worse than maintainer-time would predict but consistent with contractor-time.

## Extrapolation and the authors' own caveats

Extrapolating the trend to a **1-month (167 work-hour) horizon** — chosen because Ngo's "1-month AGI" would exceed human performance at valuable work, and because one month is roughly when new hires start generating value — the naive projection lands the arrival of 1-month software AI **between mid-2028 and mid-2030** (80% CI; central estimate mid-2029). If instead the faster 2024–2025 trend holds, it could arrive as early as **2027, or even late 2026**.

The authors caveat this heavily:
- **External validity / task realism:** realistic tasks rarely have automatic scoring and often involve other agents, resource constraints, dynamic environments, and high reliability requirements. "It may be that benchmarks like HCAST and SWE-Bench Verified are insufficient for forecasting real-world AI capabilities."
- The abstract itself is conditional: *"If these results generalize to real-world software tasks, extrapolation of this trend predicts that within 5 years, AI systems will be capable of automating many software tasks that currently take humans a month."*
- **Trend may change:** agency training and automation of AI R&D could speed it up; compute scaling could slow it. A true AGI could technically have an infinite horizon.
- **Metric is relative:** time horizon is always measured relative to a domain, task distribution, and the baseliners' skill and context, and is hard to measure for very long horizons or near-100% reliability.
- Confidence in the 2024–2025-only trend is low (only ~7 frontier models in that span). Even the trend's own uncertainty, the authors stress, is smaller than the uncertainty from external-validity and future-change concerns.
