## Abstract

An observational study of over five years and ~52,000 players on the free chess platform lichess.org, asking who actually benefits when high-quality AI feedback is freely available but its use is voluntary. The central thesis: because motivated, higher-skilled people *self-select* into using AI feedback, the apparent learning benefit of AI is an illusion of selection — and this same selection mechanism means AI *widens* the skill gap and *reduces* intellectual diversity at the population level.

**Key points**

- **Sample:** 403,010 game-level observations from **52,251 human players** on lichess.org, spanning over five years; players analysed against 129 labelled AI bots, capped at each player's first 50 games. Move quality scored by Stockfish 16 across ~70 million moves.
- **Self-selection:** Higher-skilled and more-motivated individuals seek AI feedback more, and use it more productively (especially after losses). Using a control-function (instrumental-variable) approach, the authors show the apparent learning gain is driven by endogenous motivation/effort, not by AI: **once selection is controlled, AI feedback has no average causal effect on performance.**
- **Skill gap widens:** Because higher-skilled players adopt AI more and more effectively, the realised effect of AI is larger for them (Generalized Random Forest: low-skill effect 0.021 vs high-skill 0.037), so broad AI availability **increases** rather than closes the skill gap.
- **Intellectual diversity declines — causally:** Drawing on a single centralised AI (Stockfish), players converge on common opening strategies. Leveraging **42 platform updates as natural experiments** (24 AI-related, 18 unrelated), AI-related updates causally reduce platform-level opening diversity (β ≈ −0.14, p = 0.043).
- The authors name the mechanism **"access-effort decoupling"**: universal access lowers the barrier to *use* but not the barrier to *learning*, so the distribution of effort — not access — determines who gains.

**Takeaways**

- Merely deploying AI does not lift everyone; without attention to motivation and skill-in-use, AI acts as an **amplifier of existing skill and motivation, not an equaliser**.
- The authors explicitly generalise the diversity worry to generative AI: millions routing their thinking through a few frontier models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) raises an epistemological concentration-of-cognition concern.
- Verbatim (short, for reference): the abstract states "apparent learning gains disappear once endogenous motivation is accounted for" and that "AI access widens the skill gap"; the discussion concludes "AI does not add value automatically."

## 1. Introduction

AI is hoped to raise performance both in the moment and long-term through learning and human-capital development, and some hope it could "level the playing field" by removing skill differentials. But feedback-seeking and technology adoption are driven by **individual motivation**, not access alone — the choice to seek AI feedback is *endogenous* to individual characteristics. Most experimental AI studies force exposure, which identifies a causal effect under mandate but leaves open what happens when people choose for themselves and population outcomes depend on *who selects in*.

The setting — lichess.org — offers a rare combination: voluntary access to high-quality AI feedback, granular observability of individual choices, and a large population over years. Players can have the AI retrospectively analyse a completed game, receiving move-by-move feedback on move quality, advantage gained/lost, and the best available move. Usage is popular but heterogeneous: 15% of games are analysed; 29% of individuals analyse at least one of their first 50 games; the modal user analyses none; average 1.2 (SD 4.2); most-active user analysed 49 of 50.

Headline findings, stated up front:

- Motivated individuals self-select into AI use, creating an *appearance* of effectiveness; once selection is accounted for, **AI feedback has no average causal effect on learning.**
- Higher-skilled individuals benefit disproportionately (use it more, more productively) → **AI increases the skill gap.**
- AI feedback **causally reduces intellectual diversity** as players converge on common strategies from the same centralised AI.

**Two contributions:** (1) to the literature on AI and work — showing self-selection masks much of the performance gain attributed to AI, and that even a technology with *no* causal individual effect can compound inequality when adoption correlates with pre-existing advantage; (2) to organisational-learning theory — linking AI-mediated individual optimisation to degraded collective intellectual diversity, a new micro-level mechanism for the exploration-exploitation tradeoff operating through *coupled individual trajectories via shared algorithmic signals*.

## 2. Theory and Research Questions

Feedback-seeking is "the conscious devotion of effort to determine the correctness and adequacy of behavior for attaining valued goals" (Ashford & Cummings, 1983); individuals weigh its costs and benefits.

**How AI feedback differs from human feedback:** (1) it *scales* — detailed feedback to many people in many situations; (2) as AI reaches expert level it can help even the highest-skilled, who lack access to elite mentors; (3) it carries **no social cost**, so people may feel higher psychological safety seeking corrective feedback. Because AI feedback is instant, socially costless, and cognitively easy to access, the barrier to use is low — which can encourage *uncritical* engagement (replaying wins, avoiding the hard work of understanding failures) rather than developmental use.

### 2.1 Why Learning from AI Feedback May Be Endogenous

Three layers of self-selection (endogeneity):

| Layer | Mechanism | Who selects in |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Whether to seek AI feedback at all** | Goal importance, task identification, competence-motivation spirals; low social cost | More motivated, and (via image concerns) higher-skilled |
| **(b) In which situations** (wins vs losses) | Ego-protection → avoid negative feedback; high motivation/self-esteem needed to face failures | Motivated, higher-skilled, higher task self-esteem seek feedback after losses |
| **(c) How deeply they engage** | Deep reflection needs motivation + cognitive scaffolding; only those with "AI interrogation practices" benefit (Jussupow et al., 2021) | Theorised but *not directly measured* here |

Together these produce **"access-effort decoupling"**: observed correlations between AI feedback and improvement may reflect pre-existing individual differences, not causal AI effects. The paper can instrument (a) and (b); (c) is theorised only, making the estimates a *conservative* test.

### 2.2 Collective Consequences of AI Feedback

Individual choices aggregate into unintended collective patterns:

- **Skill gap.** Two competing theories: AI as *equaliser* (marginal value of advice is decreasing in ability, so low-skilled gain more — supported by Noy & Zhang 2023, Dell'Acqua et al., etc.) vs AI as *complement* to skill (benefit depends on judging AI quality and knowing when to use it — a skill itself; low-skilled may ignore advice or misuse outputs). Which dominates matters for labour-market inequality and de-skilling.
- **Diversity loss.** When many consult the same centralised source, beliefs/strategies converge, replacing heterogeneous private knowledge with shared knowledge — "algorithmic monoculture" (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021). Amplified by specialisation (narrowing exploration). Population-level homogeneity can hurt long-term problem-solving and competitive advantage.

### 2.3 Research Questions

- **Individual level:** Who seeks AI feedback and why, accounting for endogenous motivation (overall, and after successes vs failures)?
- **Collective level:** Does AI access increase or decrease the skill gap over time, and how does it affect long-term intellectual diversity?

## 3. Setting and Data

**Why chess:** AI is broadly available and known to be high-quality (so adoption reflects choice, not trust); adoption is fully voluntary; performance has no practical ceiling (even elites benefit, ruling out rational non-adoption).

- **Platform:** lichess.org — nonprofit, all features free, >90 million games/month. AI feedback powered by **Stockfish** (strongest chess AI; Stockfish 16 Elo ≈ 3,641 — for scale, peak Magnus Carlsen at Elo 2,882 would win ~12 of 1,000 games against it).
- **Identification trick:** the platform shows *whether* a game was analysed but not *by whom*. So the authors study **human-vs-bot** games (129 bots identified Sep 2022), where the human is almost certainly the analyser. Bot games are mostly training.
- **Cleaning:** 1,054,640 bot games → remove bot-vs-bot, non-standard, and unrated games; keep each player's first 50 games (only 5% play >50; ~53 days on average to reach 50, SD 144). **Final dataset: 403,010 game-level observations from 52,251 human players.**
- **Dependent variables:** *AI feedback* (dummy: game analysed or not) and *performance* (move accuracy — Stockfish 16 scored all ~70,037,446 moves, ~35/game, simulating up to 1.5M moves per position; aggregated with Lichess's own accuracy formula).
- **Controls:** game length, game type (ultrabullet→classical), win/loss, common opening, bot skill; player Elo, tenure (years on platform) and tenure², experience (cumulative bot games), and playing white.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Seeking AI Feedback (logistic panel regression + control function)

- Strong **aversion to seeking feedback after losses** (β = −1.61, p < .001): ~11% chance to analyse a loss vs ~21% a win.
- **Higher-skilled players seek AI feedback more** (β = 0.06, p = .031), and are much more likely to seek it after *failures* (loss × skill interaction β = 0.24, p < .001).
- **Control-function correction** (four instruments capturing time-varying motivation/effort: recent losses, time between recent games, inexperience dummy, opponent strength) reveals **endogeneity**: the raw loss-aversion is largely driven by unobserved motivation shocks. Corrected, analysing losses becomes *positive* (β = 0.14, p = .004) — motivated people know losses are learnable — while the skill × loss interaction is robust (β = 0.23, p < .001).
- **Key insight:** higher-skilled, more-motivated, more-engaged individuals are the ones who seek AI feedback on losses.

### 4.2 Learning from AI Feedback (panel regression with user + time fixed effects, control function)

- **No effect of past AI feedback on current performance overall** (β = 0.00, p = .552). This null hides opposing effects: feedback **on losses helps** (β = 0.05, p < .001), feedback **on wins hurts** (β = −0.05, p < .001). Holds against human opponents too (Appendix Table A3).
- Second control-function stage (instruments: awareness dummy = has-used-before; supply-side = total AI analyses by others that day, capturing server availability). Residuals are large and significant (access residual +0.33; productive-use residual −5.17, both p < .001), confirming **endogenous self-selection**.
- **Once endogenous channels are controlled, the direct causal effect of every form of AI feedback is zero** (total, on losses, on wins). Motivated individuals would have learned just as much without AI. AI works only as a *complement to effort and pre-existing skill*.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The paper's core individual-level result: conditional on motivation and effort, AI feedback does **not** improve performance. "AI does not add value automatically." Performance gains attributed to AI are endogenous to motivation/discipline.

### 4.3 The Population Skill Gap (Generalized Random Forest heterogeneous effects)

Adoption is *non-uniform* across the skill distribution. GRF finds statistically and practically significant heterogeneity: the realised effect of AI feedback is **higher for high-skilled** (median-split: low = 0.021 [CI 0.019–0.023] vs high = 0.037 [CI 0.034–0.040]). These realised (not endogeneity-corrected) effects are what actually reshape the population — so where AI is widely available, low-cost, and high-quality, **it is the vehicle through which skill advantages compound**, widening the gap.

### 4.4 Population-Level Intellectual Diversity

- **Descriptive:** grouping players by past AI feedback, opening-move diversity (1 − Gini) *falls* as AI exposure rises (ρ = −0.59, p = 4.9 × 10⁻⁶).
- **Causal (RDiT + natural experiments):** a daily platform-level opening-diversity time series (2020-11-27 to 2023-06-09), analysed around **42 major platform updates** (24 AI-related, 18 unrelated) via stacked difference-in-differences with 15-day windows, plus 100 placebo events. **AI-related updates causally decrease diversity** (β = −0.14, p = 0.043); placebos are near-zero; *unrelated* updates *increase* diversity (via tactics/puzzle features). So the net AI effect overcomes the baseline upward pull of ordinary updates. Robust to 30-day windows and time controls.
- **Individual mechanism (Appendix):** more AI feedback raises the likelihood a player repeats a recent opening (β = 0.04, p = 0.03), and this **specialisation is even stronger for higher-skilled players** (skill × AI interaction β = 0.02, p = 0.026) — convergent updating plus narrowing repertoires produce population homogenisation.

## Discussion

- Descriptively, people learn from feedback on failures, not successes — but these gains are **entirely driven by endogenous selection** (motivation, effort). Learning concentrates among the higher-skilled and highly motivated.
- Instead of lifting those with most to gain, **AI disproportionately complements the higher-skilled, amplifying the skill gap** — the empirical signature of access-effort decoupling.
- The diversity finding extends prior work (Doshi & Hauser 2024; Meincke et al. 2025) by identifying a *micro-level* mechanism — convergent updating + specialisation from a centralised AI — producing a macro-level loss of diversity.
- **Generalisation to generative AI:** LLMs are already a feedback source (line-by-line writing/analysis suggestions), and most interactions route through a few frontier models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, ByteDance) with similar training data — concentrating cognitive influence, an epistemological as well as market-structure concern.
- **Limitations:** single domain (chess); AI quality and trust are unusually well-established here, so trust plays a smaller role than in other settings; an alternative reading of reduced diversity is *quality convergence* (players converging on objectively better play), which the authors cannot fully rule out but argue is not the full story since the reduction concentrates among AI-exposed players.
- **Practical implications:** don't assume rolling out AI lifts everyone's skill; AI alone won't close the skill gap without interventions addressing motivation and training people how to use AI well; beware unintended homogenisation undermining competitiveness. In hard tasks without direct performance incentives, **AI acts as an amplifier of existing skill and motivation, not an equaliser.**

## Relation to the book

This paper is a cornerstone empirical source for **§6.5** on using AI well and who actually benefits. Its finding is the pessimistic, mechanism-level counterpart to the book's thesis: when AI is simply made available and its use is left to the individual, the gains do **not** accrue evenly or automatically. Self-selection means motivated, already-skilled users pull further ahead, so **AI widens rather than closes skill gaps**, and centralised AI use erodes collective intellectual diversity. This grounds the book's argument that access is not the same as benefit — the decisive variable is *how* a person engages (effort, discipline, knowing when and what to ask), exactly the delegation-and-judgement capability the book argues determines who profits from AI. Pair it with Idan & Anand (2026), whose RCT names that capability — "AI Interaction Competence" — and shows structured workflows can partly equalise the divide; together the two sources frame §6.5's problem (AI amplifies gaps) and its lever (the complementary human skill, teachable through scaffolds).
