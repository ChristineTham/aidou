# Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2507.09089-metr-ai-dev-productivity.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2507.09089-metr-ai-dev-productivity.pdf) · Joel Becker, Nate Rush, Beth Barnes & David Rein (Model Evaluation & Threat Research, METR), 2025 · arXiv:2507.09089v2 [cs.AI], 25 Jul 2025 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089>.
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording, figures, and the ~30 pages of appendices.

## Abstract

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) run by METR measuring how early-2025 AI coding tools affected the productivity of experienced open-source developers on their own mature repositories. The headline is counter-intuitive: allowing AI made tasks take **19% *longer*** — a slowdown — even though everyone expected a large speedup.

**Key points**

- **Design:** 16 experienced developers completed **246 real tasks** (avg 2.0 hours each) on large, mature repos they averaged **~5 years** and ~1,500 commits on (23,000 stars, ~1.1M lines of code on average). Each task was randomly assigned to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed. When allowed, developers mostly used **Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet**.
- **Headline result:** allowing AI **increased** completion time by **19%** (regression estimate). This is a slowdown, not a speedup.
- **The perception gap** — everyone predicted the opposite:
  - Developers *forecast* a **24% speedup** before starting.
  - Developers *post-hoc estimated* a **20% speedup** after finishing — still convinced AI helped, despite being 19% slower.
  - **ML experts** predicted a **38% speedup**; **economics experts** predicted **39%**.
- **Mechanism (from 143 hours of labelled screen recordings):** with AI, developers spent *less* time coding and reading/searching, and *more* time prompting the AI, waiting on generations, reviewing/cleaning AI output, and idle. They accepted **<44%** of AI-generated code.
- **Factor analysis:** of 21 hypothesised contributors, evidence supports 5 (chiefly over-optimism about AI, high repo familiarity, large/complex repos, low AI reliability, tacit repo context), is mixed for 10, and argues against 6 (incl. unfamiliar IDE, cheating, non-robust estimator).

**Takeaways**

- Impressive AI *benchmark* scores do not automatically translate into real-world productivity for experienced engineers on codebases they know deeply — measure in the field, not just on synthetic tasks.
- Self-reported and forecast productivity is untrustworthy here: developers were confidently, systematically wrong about their own speedup even after using the tools.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Key caveat — do NOT over-generalise.** This is a *setting-specific* result: experienced devs, high familiarity, large/mature/high-standard codebases. The authors explicitly state it does **not** show AI fails to help in general. Greenfield projects, unfamiliar codebases, less-experienced developers, better-elicited or future models could all still see speedup. The paper is a measurement of one setting, not a claim that AI never helps.

## Introduction

Software development is a key domain for forecasting AI capability. Frontier systems look strong on software *benchmarks* and on lab experiments with *synthetic* tasks — but those tasks trade realism for scale (self-contained, low prior-context, algorithmic metrics), so it is hard to infer real-world impact. Existing *field* experiments measure outcomes like lines of code or PR count, which AI can inflate without raising true productivity (more verbose but equivalent code; splitting work into smaller PRs).

To close this inferential gap, METR ran an RCT: 16 developers, 246 tasks (2.0h avg) on well-known repos (avg 23,000 stars) they regularly contribute to. Tasks were **defined before randomisation** (limiting AI-driven changes to the outcome measure itself), then coin-flipped to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed. Developers typically had tens-to-hundreds of hours of prior LLM use.

Results contradicted every prediction:

| Estimate | Direction | Magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| Developer forecast (before) | speedup | −24% |
| Developer post-hoc estimate (after) | speedup | −20% |
| ML experts | speedup | −38% |
| Economics experts | speedup | −39% |
| **Observed (regression)** | **slowdown** | **+19%** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The central finding: allowing early-2025 AI tools **slowed experienced developers down by 19%**, exposing a large disconnect between *perceived* and *actual* AI impact on productivity.

To understand why, the authors manually labelled 143 hours of screen recordings (~29% of total developer hours) at ~10-second resolution, plus source-control statistics, interviews, surveys, and subset analyses. They identified **21 candidate factors** in four categories: (a) direct productivity loss, (b) experimental artifact, (c) factors raising human performance, (d) factors limiting AI performance. Evidence supports 5, is mixed/unclear for 10, and argues against 6. The slowdown is broadly robust across design decisions; experimental artifacts cannot be entirely ruled out but appear unlikely to be the primary cause.

### Background (prior literature)

| Prior finding | Study | Why it doesn't settle the question |
|---|---|---|
| 56% / 21% speedup on coding tasks | Peng et al.; Paradis et al. | **Synthetic** tasks (e.g. a basic HTTP server) — unrepresentative, likely close to LLM training data |
| 65% more requirements satisfied | Weber et al. | Synthetic tasks |
| 14–51% output gains | Yeverechyahu; Gambacorta; Cui et al. | Outcome measures (lines, commits, PRs) **not fixed in advance** — inflatable without true productivity gain |

- **Heterogeneous effects:** micro-level evidence generally finds AI benefits *less-experienced* workers more, compressing performance distributions — which motivates this study's focus on *highly skilled* developers, an under-researched setting.
- Other domains (e.g. CBRN uplift) show mixed AI results; some non-software domains show large gains.

The study's contributions: (1) tests Feb–Jun 2025 frontier models; (2) uses live, unfiltered open-source tasks; (3) uses a **fixed** outcome measure (speedup on pre-defined tasks); (4) recruits engineers with years of repo-specific expertise; (5) collects rich time-use, AI-suggestion, and qualitative data.

## Methodology

### Developers and repositories

- **Developers:** 16 experienced engineers (typically >10 years' experience), regular contributors averaging **5 years** on their repo (**59% of the repo's lifetime**) and **~1,500 commits**. Paid **$150/hour**. 93% had used LLMs like ChatGPT; only **44%** had used Cursor.
- **Repositories:** large and mature — averaging **23,000 stars, ~1,100,000 lines of code, 4,900 forks, 20,000 commits, 710 committers**, with very high contribution-quality bars.

### Experimental design

- Each developer supplies real issues (bug reports, feature requests, work items). Issues are forecast for time (a difficulty proxy), then randomised by a simulated fair coin flip. ~Half the issues were tracked for the experiment rather than formally on the repo; all represent real intended work. Developers cap issues at ~2 hours or split larger ones.
- AI-allowed: any AI tools (or none) permitted. AI-disallowed: no generative AI; LLM-based tab-autocomplete (e.g. Copilot) disallowed, plain search engines allowed.
- Work flows through each repo's normal process: developer implements → submits **PR** → peer review → changes → merge. High review standards mean merged PRs rarely contain flaws. Developers self-report time spent before and after PR review.

### AI tools and training

- Two access modes: web UIs (e.g. chatgpt.com) and **Cursor Pro** (a VSCode fork adding LLM chat plus an agent that searches/edits files, runs bash, and iterates). METR provided Cursor Pro subscriptions and basic live training (prompting, accepting changes, reverting checkpoints) but did **not** require its use.

### Effect estimation

The estimand is the percentage change in completion time due to AI:

> [!NOTE]
> **S = E[completion time with AI allowed] / E[completion time with AI disallowed] − 1.**
> −50% = tasks take half as long with AI; 0% = no change; +100% = twice as long. Negative = "speedup", positive = "slowdown" (a mild abuse of language — the quantity is a percentage change in *time*, not a rate).

- Uses total self-reported implementation time (pre- + post-review). Unmerged/unreviewed issues have post-review time imputed at the per-condition mean.
- S is estimated via a **log-linear regression** on total time, using forecasted difficulty to reduce noise. Adapted regressions handle subset analyses.

### Supplementary data collection

- **Developer forecasts & post-hoc estimates** — per-issue time with/without AI (difficulty proxy + calibration check), plus an exit estimate of AI's effect.
- **Expert forecasts** — from **54 ML experts** and **34 economics experts**, given detailed study design and the AI-experience mix; incentivised via a (deliberately simple, technically improper) scoring rule.
- **Cursor analytics** — for 13 developers: most-used model, and suggested vs accepted lines of AI code.
- **Exit interviews/surveys** and **qualitative notes** (clustered via inductive coding; possible bias from targeted probing questions acknowledged).

### Screen-recording activity labels

128 recordings (143 hours) were manually labelled; after filtering for cheating, broken recordings (>10%), and self-report/recording discrepancies (>20%), **74 recordings / 84 hours** remained. Activities span 8 high-level labels (writing code; testing/debugging; reading/searching; Git/environment; prompting AI; waiting on AI; reviewing AI output; idle/other), each in one of 27 fine-grained categories, at ~10-second resolution.

## Results

Developers completed **136 AI-allowed** and **110 AI-disallowed** issues. AI-allowed issues took **19% more time** on average, with nearly all quantiles of the completion-time distribution showing AI-allowed issues taking longer.

> [!NOTE]
> The **raw** ratio of average times gives a larger **34%** slowdown; the 19% regression estimate is smaller because AI-allowed issues happened to be forecast as slightly harder after randomisation (not statistically significant), which the regression corrects for.

### Forecasts

- Developers forecast a **24% speedup**; post-hoc estimated a **20% speedup** — despite being **19% slower**. Their beliefs about AI's usefulness were reversed.
- Yet developers were well-calibrated on *relative* difficulty: Pearson correlation of forecast vs actual time was **0.64** (AI-allowed) and **0.59** (AI-disallowed).
- Experts overestimated even more: **economics 39%**, **ML 38%** speedup.

### Activity labels

On the 74 labelled issues, when AI was allowed developers spent a **smaller** share of time actively coding and reading/searching, and a **larger** share prompting AI, waiting on AI generations, reviewing AI outputs — and were somewhat more **idle**. (Waiting on AI alone was ~4% of time; reviewing/cleaning AI output ~9%.)

### Factor analysis

Of 21 a-priori factors: **5 supported, 10 mixed/unclear, 6 argued against.** The authors caution against over-indexing on any single subset result (not powered for multiple comparisons). Legend: 🡇 direct productivity loss, ⚙ raises developer performance, 🤖 limits AI performance, 🧪 experimental artifact.

**Factors likely contributing to slowdown (5):**

| Factor | Type | Key observations |
|---|---|---|
| Over-optimism about AI usefulness | 🡇 | Forecast −24%, post-hoc −20% speedup → overuse of AI despite negative effect |
| High developer familiarity with repos | ⚙ | Slowed *more* on high-prior-exposure / low-external-resource issues; avg 5 yrs & 1,500 commits |
| Large & complex repositories | 🤖 | Devs report AI worse in big/complex code; repos ~10 yrs old, >1.1M LOC |
| Low AI reliability | 🤖 | <44% of AI generations accepted; major cleanup common; ~9% of time reviewing/cleaning AI output |
| Implicit repository context | 🤖 + ⚙ | AI fails to use tacit knowledge/context |

**Factors with unclear/mixed effect (10):** experimentally driven overuse of AI; unrepresentative task distribution; AI increasing issue scope (47% more lines per forecasted hour in AI-allowed issues); bias from issue-completion order; sampling bias in recruitment; trading speed for ease (AI is "less effortful"); low-quality initial PRs; below-average AI-tool use (no clear learning effect over first 30–50h of Cursor); AI generation latency (~4% of time waiting); suboptimal elicitation (few tokens sampled — though prior positive-speedup work also sampled few tokens).

**Factors unlikely to contribute (6):** unfamiliar development environment (devs on comparable IDEs still saw ~24% slowdown); cheating/under-use of AI (AI used in all but 16.4% of allowed labelled cases; only 3 cheating instances in 54 recordings); issue dropout; non-robust outcome measure (alternatives yield similar slowdown); non-robust estimator (alternatives similar); non-frontier model usage (devs mostly used then-frontier models).

## Discussion

The evidence indicates recent AI slows down *experienced* open-source developers with *moderate* AI experience on *large, familiar* repos — suggesting real-world AI capability may lag benchmark results. Both experts and developers dramatically overestimated AI's usefulness even after extensive hands-on use, underscoring the value of field experiments with robust, pre-fixed outcome measures over forecasts and surveys.

### Key caveats

> [!WARNING]
> **Setting-specific factors.** The slowdown does *not* imply AI rarely helps. High repo familiarity and repo size/maturity — both shown to drive the slowdown — do not apply to many settings. Results are consistent with **small greenfield projects or unfamiliar codebases seeing substantial speedup**.

> [!WARNING]
> **AI-specific factors.** Higher reliability, lower latency, or better elicitation (more inference compute/tokens, better prompting/scaffolding, repo-specific fine-tuning) could plausibly turn this setting positive.

> [!TIP]
> **Agents can make real progress.** Preliminary (forthcoming) evidence: fully autonomous Claude 3.7 Sonnet agents often correctly implement the *core* functionality of study issues, though they fail full requirements (missing docs, failing lint/style, missing tests). Given the recent pace of progress, significant speedup in this setting may not be far off.

## Extended discussion (Appendix B) — what this study does NOT show

The authors explicitly forestall misreadings. The study does **not** provide evidence that:

| Misreading to avoid | Clarification |
|---|---|
| AI doesn't speed up most developers | These devs/repos aren't claimed to represent most software work |
| AI doesn't help outside software | Only software was studied |
| Future AI won't speed up *this* setting | Progress is hard to predict; AI has advanced a lot in 5 years |
| There's no better way to use *today's* AI here | Better prompting/scaffolding or repo-specific training could yield speedup |
| More AI-experienced devs wouldn't see speedup | Plausible that much more experience helps |
| These devs weren't sped up on *any* task | Some developers did experience speedup on some issues |

## Appendix C — Factor analysis (selected detail)

The five supported factors, in more depth:

- **C.1.1 Over-optimism about AI (🡇):** Since AI use was optional, one might expect S bounded at ~0% (a developer noticing they're slower would stop). Instead persistent over-optimism (forecast −24%, post-hoc −20%) likely drives *overuse* despite the negative effect.
- **C.1.2 High familiarity (⚙):** Using self-scored "Prior Task Exposure" (1–5) and "External Resource Needs" (1–3), developers were slowed *more* on high-exposure, low-resource-need issues. Qualitatively, AI helped on *unfamiliar* work (a first-time frontend test; Git hooks; an unknown helper function) but not where the developer was the dedicated maintainer ("there is no way agent mode can do better than me"). Consistent with familiarity effects in Noy & Zhang and Cui et al.
- **C.1.3 Large/complex repos (🤖):** Developers report the AI "made weird changes in other parts of the code", "started editing random other parts of the file", with failures tied to large file/edit size.
- **C.1.4 Low AI reliability (🤖):** <44% of AI generations accepted; frequent major cleanup.
- **C.1.5 Implicit repo context (🤖 + ⚙):** AI fails to use tacit knowledge/context the maintainer holds.

Appendices D–G (not reproduced in detail) cover the regression/confidence-interval methodology, heterogeneous-effect estimation, expert-forecast distributions, and recruitment/training/labelling procedures.
