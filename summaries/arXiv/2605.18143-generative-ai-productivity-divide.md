## Abstract

A randomized controlled experiment (n = 179, Texas A&M engineering students as analogs of early-career knowledge workers) on self-studying a technical domain (LLMs) with vs without LLM assistance. GenAI access raised *mean* performance but distributed gains very unevenly. Gains were **not** predicted by GPA or prior knowledge, but by **AI Interaction Competence (AIC)** — the ability to elicit, filter, and verify model outputs. High-AIC participants gained a lot; low-AIC ones saw limited or even negative marginal returns. A **conceptual-map scaffolding** intervention reduced outcome variance without lowering the mean, showing standardized workflows can mitigate the new inequality.

**Key points**

- **Design:** RCT, 179 participants; 3 consecutive days, ≥3 hours/day, self-study of LLMs; baseline (any resources *except* LLMs) vs LLM condition (free ChatGPT only); pre-exam (15 items) and post-exam (28 LLM items); $50 base + $150 bonus for top 10%.
- **Mean effect:** LLM group outperformed baseline (post-exam M = .56, SD = .26 vs M = .48, SD = .23; treatment coefficient positive, p < .05) — an approximate **17% productivity lift**. Baseline participants also dropped out far more (20 vs 9; χ², p < .01) and 69% of participants preferred LLMs over other resources.
- **Not GPA, not prior knowledge:** GPA × treatment insignificant (p = 0.59); pre-exam (prior topic knowledge) × treatment insignificant (p = 0.2). GenAI *flattened* these traditional inequality axes.
- **AIC is the decisive moderator:** (Treatment × AIC) positive and significant (p < .05). "AIC thus supplanted traditional academic credentials as the most consequential moderator of performance." Three-way interaction (Treatment × prior-knowledge × AIC, p < .05): novices benefit most from LLMs **only if** they have high AIC; low-AIC novices gain little despite more "room to grow".
- **New dispersion:** among novices, standard LLM use *increased* variance by ~47% in SD (more than doubling variance). **Scaffolding** (conceptual roadmap + sequencing) cut that dispersion by ~40% (≈ one-third) **without lowering the mean** (scaffolded novices SD .14 vs unguided .22). Extra time-on-task (4 vs 3 hrs) produced **no** gain.

**Takeaways**

- Access is the *beginning*, not the endpoint: the binding constraint is **alignment** — whether people have the interactional fluency (AIC) to convert AI outputs into consistent performance.
- The managerial lever is *capability design*: short (60–90 min) AIC micro-training plus light scaffolds (prompt templates, verification/review checklists — SOPs for AI) raise the performance floor and cut variance by about a third.
- Verbatim (short, for reference): "AIC thus supplanted traditional academic credentials as the most consequential moderator of performance"; scaffolding "compresses heterogeneity in outcomes by disproportionately aiding weaker extractors."

## 1. Introduction

GenAI is transforming knowledge work, with documented productivity gains *and* striking variation in who benefits. A long management tradition holds that technology alone creates no value — payoff depends on complementary human/organisational capabilities. GenAI *heightens* this: unlike routine-standardising automation, LLMs require iterative problem-solving (formulating prompts, interpreting probabilistic outputs, verifying quality), so productivity depends on **interactional skills that are tacit, unevenly distributed, and rarely taught**.

The authors name this **AI Interaction Competence (AIC)** — "the ability to elicit, evaluate, and apply model outputs effectively" — a new dimension of human capital. High-AIC workers extend their productivity frontier; low-AIC workers may fail to extract value or compound errors. Hence a **new productivity divide**: the technology raises the *mean* but widens *dispersion*.

**Three contributions:** (1) causal micro-evidence that individual capability heterogeneity drives uneven returns; (2) introducing AIC as a theoretically grounded construct bridging human-capital and digital-complementarity literatures; (3) showing process design (conceptual scaffolding + micro-training) can manage variance. Framing: GenAI adoption is a problem of **capability design**, not tool procurement.

## 2. Literature Review

Technology creates value only with complementary capabilities (Brynjolfsson & Hitt; Aral, Brynjolfsson & Wu; etc.). Skill-biased technical change explains why such technologies raise average productivity while widening inequality: as automation substitutes for routine tasks, the value of abstract reasoning, coordination, and adaptability rises. GenAI continues this by automating linguistic/cognitive subtasks while amplifying the advantage of those who can **direct, evaluate, and verify** outputs — i.e., AIC. Field experiments (call centres, writing, consulting — Brynjolfsson/Li/Raymond; Dell'Acqua et al.; Eloundou et al.) show GenAI boosts mean productivity but introduces new cross-user variance. This paper adds *controlled causal micro-evidence* isolating AIC and showing lightweight process interventions reduce variance without lowering the mean.

## 3. Research Questions

| RQ | Question | Short answer found |
|---|---|---|
| **RQ1** | Do workers' self-perceptions of knowledge align with actual knowledge? | Yes, and in good faith (ML self-assessment ρ = .71; LLM ρ = .6), but the dimensions they *can* introspect on are **not** the ones that drive outcome inequality. |
| **RQ2** | Do GenAI tools beat traditional resources? | Yes — higher scores, less dropout, strong preference. |
| **RQ3** | Does GenAI reduce or expand inequality in acquiring expertise? | It **compresses** inequality along GPA/prior-knowledge lines but **expands** it along a new axis: AIC. |
| **RQ4** | What mechanisms drive the distributional effects? | Preference≠ability mismatches; conceptual-map scaffolding (equaliser); extra time (no effect). |

## 4. Experimental Overview

A four-stage design mirroring an enterprise GenAI rollout: **profiling → baseline assessment → randomized upskilling intervention → post-intervention evaluation.** Randomize access to a **GenAI condition** (LLM-assisted) vs a **baseline condition** (traditional resources, no LLM). Within the GenAI condition, less-experienced participants are further split into process variants — *scaffolding* (conceptual roadmaps + sequencing), *time-on-task* (higher hourly budget), and *peer-collaboration* — representing common managerial levers. Primary outcome: individual task performance (a productivity proxy); secondary: engagement/attrition and resource preference.

**AIC defined operationally** as the skills/habits to: (1) formulate precise, goal-oriented prompts; (2) filter and verify outputs for accuracy, bias, and relevance rather than accepting them at face value; (3) iterate interactively, combining human judgment with machine suggestions.

## 5. Methodology

- **Participants:** 179 at Texas A&M, mostly undergraduates across engineering disciplines (plus a few business students); disciplinary diversity helps separate novice vs advanced learners. Students are used as tractable analogs of early-career knowledge workers doing self-directed technical learning under time pressure.
- **Why study LLMs as the topic:** layered knowledge structure (general ML vs LLM-specific), architectural breadth (good for scaffolding tests), high intrinsic motivation (minimises attrition), and a recursive twist — using LLMs to study LLMs.
- **Pre-survey:** age, gender, GPA, learning preferences, and self-assessed knowledge of ML / AIC / LLM architecture (a deliberate tripartite decomposition).
- **Pre-exam:** 15 multiple-choice items on two orthogonal dimensions — a *familiarity anchor* (2–4 awareness items, e.g. "hallucinations", to avoid floor effects) and a *generality–specificity* split (general ML vs LLM-specific). 30 minutes, no external resources; participants not told how it would be used.
- **Classification:** **advanced** = ≥8 correct; **novice** = <8 correct.
- **Randomization (two stages):**
  - **Group A (Baseline):** any resource *except* LLMs (textbooks, YouTube, peers, articles). Split A_novice / A_advanced.
  - **Group B (LLM):** **only** the free version of ChatGPT (paid prohibited). Split B_novice / B_advanced.
  - Within **B_novice**, four scaffolding sub-arms: *baseline* (same as B_advanced), *time* (4 hrs/day not 3), *scaffolding* (conceptual roadmap + recommended sequencing), *peer* (documented collaboration).
- **Post-exam:** 28 multiple-choice items, LLM-only content (no general ML), overlapping some pre-exam items to measure gains; 1 hour; one-page cheat-sheet allowed (to emphasise comprehension); 3 open-ended numerical tie-breakers.
- **Incentives:** $50 base for all; top 10% get +$150.
- **Outcome variable:** normalized to the unit interval, Yᵢ = points scored / questions available (post-exam: 1 point fully correct, 0.5 partial, 0 incorrect).

## 6. Results

### 6.1 RQ1 — Calibration

Self-assessments correlated meaningfully with measured performance (good-faith responses): ML knowledge ρ = .71 (p < .01), LLM-specific ρ = .6 (p < .01), AIC only modest ρ = .46 (p < .05) — and AIC was *inferred from behaviour*, not self-assessed. Crucially, the dimensions people could introspect on (ML, LLM knowledge) did **not** moderate treatment gains; the skill that did — **AIC** — was invisible to conventional exams. Implication: self-assessments and prior-knowledge tests cannot predict who benefits most from AI-mediated learning.

### 6.2 RQ2 — GenAI vs traditional resources

- **Performance:** LLM group M = .56 (SD .26) vs baseline M = .48 (SD .23); OLS with controls (pre-exam, GPA, gender, study preference) → treatment coefficient positive, significant (p < .05).
- **Attrition (revealed preference):** 20 baseline dropouts vs 9 LLM (χ², p < .01) — lack of LLM access undermined persistence.
- **Stated preference:** 69% preferred LLMs > YouTube (67%) > lectures (64%) > textbooks (40%).
- **Validity:** completer-sample analysis; randomization balance confirmed (Mann–Whitney U, no significant demographic/education differences).

### 6.3 RQ3 — Inequality: reduced on old axes, expanded on a new one

- **GPA × treatment** insignificant (p = 0.59); **pre-exam × treatment** insignificant (p = 0.2). GenAI did *not* preferentially reward the already-advantaged — a notable null that challenges the assumption that tech magnifies prior-knowledge gaps. GenAI *flattened* these traditional dimensions.
- **AIC × treatment** positive and significant (p < .05). AIC is the **decisive moderator** — "the emergent 'currency' of learning in AI-mediated environments." A skill "not formally taught, not evenly distributed, and not easily signaled by existing academic markers."
- **Three-way interaction** (Treatment × pre-exam × AIC, p < .05): rescaled, **novices benefit more from LLMs — but only if high-AIC**; low-AIC novices gain substantially less despite more room to grow.
- **Distributional upshot:** GenAI compresses inequality on conventional lines but *expands* it along AIC — "inequality of a new kind."
- **Learning-gain shape (Figure 1, exploratory):** in the no-LLM condition, gains decline with prior knowledge (diminishing returns / room-to-improve); in the standard-LLM condition this downward slope is weaker/flatter — LLMs make the knowledge space "less bounded" (tailored explanations, follow-ups, examples). But very-low-prior-knowledge participants gain no obvious extra advantage: open-ended LLM use imposes *navigational demands* (knowing what to ask, interpreting answers, spotting incomplete/misleading output) — reinforcing that access alone is insufficient.

### 6.4 RQ4 — Mechanisms

- **Preference ≠ ability:** LLM-preferring participants had higher AIC (Mann–Whitney U, p < .05), but the preference–ability correlation was only small–moderate (r = 0.26). Two subgroups: **Group 1 "enthusiastic but unskilled"** (low AIC, still prefer LLMs — possibly overconfidence); **Group 2 "skilled but reluctant"** (high AIC, prefer other methods — often value social/peer learning LLMs can't provide; logistic model coefficient positive but not significant, p ≈ 0.1, low power). Preferences can't be taken as proxies for competence; access alone won't guarantee equity.
- **Scaffolding (conceptual maps) as equaliser:** scaffolded novices M = .45 (SD .14) vs unguided LLM novices M = .38 (SD .22), weakly significant (.05 < p < .10). The (scaffolding × AIC) interaction was **negative** (weakly significant) — scaffolding helps **low-AIC** participants most, high-AIC little. It **compresses heterogeneity by disproportionately aiding weaker extractors**, lowering variance (SD .14 vs .22) — levelling the floor.
- **More time = diminishing returns:** 4-hr novices M = .37 (SD .22) ≈ 3-hr novices M = .38 (SD .23); no significant treatment effect. Simply adding exposure doesn't reduce inequality without efficient strategies (some reported frustration).

## 7. Discussion

- GenAI **alters what it means to be "able"**: prior knowledge, GPA, and accumulated expertise lose predictive power once LLM access is universal; performance hinges on eliciting, interpreting, and operationalising outputs — i.e., AIC. AIC explains how GenAI can *simultaneously* equalise (lowering entry barriers) and stratify (rewarding those fluent with algorithms).
- Consistent with human–AI complementarity theory: general-purpose technologies raise average productivity but *increase variance* because returns depend on complementary human assets. GenAI is "not merely a substitute for labor but a multiplier of competence."
- **Quantified managerial pattern:** GenAI access ≈ **+17% productivity**; but dispersion among novices rose ~**47% in SD** (more than doubling variance); scaffolding cut dispersion by ~**40% (≈ one-third) without lowering the mean**. The challenge is not access but **alignment**.
- **Levers:** targeted micro-training (60–90 min) on prompting logic, verification heuristics, and synthesis structure; light scaffolds (standardized prompt templates, review checklists) — "SOPs" for human–AI interaction. Treating AI as pure cost-saving/automation risks widening internal capability gaps. Managing variance via training and process design is a **strategic capability**, not a peripheral HR issue.

## Relation to the book

This RCT is a central empirical source for **§6.5** on using AI well, and it supplies the *complementary* half of the argument to Riedl & Bogert (2024). Where Riedl & Bogert show that mere AI access widens gaps through self-selection, Idan & Anand name and measure the human capability that decides *who* benefits: **AI Interaction Competence** — the ability to elicit, filter, and verify model outputs. That construct is essentially the book's own delegation-and-judgement thesis operationalised: what matters is not credentials, prior knowledge, or grades ("what mattered most was not years of coursework, grades, or accumulated disciplinary knowledge") but the disciplined skill of directing and checking the model. The paper also gives §6.5 an actionable, hopeful lever: **structured workflows / conceptual-map scaffolds reduce the variance**, disproportionately helping weaker users, so the divide is not fixed — it can be narrowed by teaching the skill and standardising the process. Cite it alongside Riedl & Bogert to frame §6.5's tension (AI amplifies inequality) and its resolution (a teachable complementary skill, supported by scaffolds and SOPs).
