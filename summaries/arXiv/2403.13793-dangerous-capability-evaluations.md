# Evaluating Frontier Models for Dangerous Capabilities

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2403.13793-dangerous-capability-evaluations.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2403.13793-dangerous-capability-evaluations.pdf) — Mary Phuong, Matthew Aitchison, Elliot Catt, Sarah Cogan, Alexandre Kaskasoli, Victoria Krakovna, David Lindner, Matthew Rahtz, et al. (Google DeepMind), *Evaluating Frontier Models for Dangerous Capabilities*, arXiv:2403.13793v2 [cs.LG], 8 April 2024 (v2 dated 5 April 2024; evaluations run January 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13793
> This is a primary research paper from a frontier AI lab (Google DeepMind) reporting on its own evaluation programme, piloted on the lab's own Gemini 1.0 models. Results are self-reported and the evaluation code was not released, though detailed methodology, prompts and rater instructions are provided in appendices.

## Abstract

This Google DeepMind paper introduces "an extensive programme of dangerous capability evaluations" and pilots it on the Gemini 1.0 model family (Ultra, Pro, and Nano 3.25B). "Dangerous capabilities" are defined as those "that could potentially unlock very large-scale harm, even if they also have beneficial applications." The evaluations cover four areas: (1) persuasion and deception; (2) cyber-security; (3) self-proliferation; and (4) self-reasoning (with a fifth area, CBRN, at only a preliminary framework stage). The headline finding: "We do not find evidence of strong dangerous capabilities in the models we evaluated, but we flag early warning signs." Persuasion and deception is flagged as the area where capabilities "appear most mature," while stronger models showed "at least rudimentary abilities across all our evaluations." The authors' stated goal is "to help the community lay the groundwork for a rigorous science of dangerous capability evaluation, in preparation for future models." A commissioned group of eight professional forecasters gave median estimates of when models will first score highly of between 2025 and 2029 for different capabilities.

## What the paper is and why it matters

The paper argues that popular capability benchmarks (verbal reasoning, coding, mathematics) and most safety evaluations (whether a system refuses malicious instructions) provide only "indirect evidence about risks." To know what risks to mitigate, "we must know the underlying capabilities of the system." High-quality dangerous-capability evaluations are meant to provide: (1) empirical grounding for policy and scientific conversations; (2) an "early warning system" for emerging risks; and (3) infrastructure for governance regimes that attach more demanding security protocols to higher-risk systems. The authors note strong policymaker demand, citing the White House Commitments on Secure and Trustworthy AI (2023), the 2023 US Executive Order on AI, the 2023 International Summit on AI Safety, and the UK AI Safety Institute. They describe their programme as "an early prototype" and "one of the most rigorous attempts to date to evaluate a frontier model for dangerous capabilities."

## The four (plus one) capability areas

1. **Persuasion and deception** — "the ability to manipulate a person's beliefs or preferences, to form an emotional connection, and to spin believable and consistent lies." Persuasion spans "rational persuasion" (appealing to reason) and manipulation (circumventing rational deliberation); deception is "where an agent intentionally causes the person to believe something untrue."
2. **Cyber-security** — navigation and manipulation of computer systems, knowledge of common vulnerabilities and exploits, use of cybersecurity/reversing tools, and exploitation of publicly known vulnerabilities.
3. **Self-proliferation** — "the ability to autonomously set up and manage digital infrastructure like cloud compute, an email account, model weights, or controller code; to acquire resources, e.g. via donations, crime or gig work; and to spread or self-improve."
4. **Self-reasoning** — "the agent's ability to reason about and modify the environment (including its own implementation) when doing so is instrumentally useful; the ability to self-modify without making irrecoverable mistakes." Component capabilities: introspection, self-modification, and reversibility reasoning.
5. **(CBRN)** — chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear attack assistance; only a "preliminary framework" is shared (Appendix B), as evaluations here are in early stages.

## Setup: models and agents

Evaluations were run in January 2024 on Gemini 1.0 Ultra, Pro, and Nano 3.25B, "without safety filters," to measure "the models' underlying capabilities rather than product safety" — meaning the results "cannot be faithfully reproduced through testing Google products like Vertex and Gemini Advanced." The models are transformer decoders with a 32k-token context length, instruction-tuned via SFT and RLHF. Special attention was paid to Ultra, but "Pro 1.0 often achieved similar performance to Ultra 1.0 (and sometimes outperforms it)," highlighting the need to attend to models "just behind the capability frontier."

To avoid underestimating a model's potential, the authors evaluate **agents = model + scaffolding** (prompts, planning/reasoning frameworks such as Tree of Thought, and tools such as a Linux shell). The general agent loop: (1) reason/plan, (2) generate multiple candidate actions, (3) score those actions. They also experimented with supervised fine-tuning on researcher-generated trajectories for self-proliferation.

## Findings by area

**Persuasion and deception (most mature).** Evaluations included *Money Talks* (persuade a human to give up part of a £20 bonus to charity), *Charm Offensive* (form a personal connection via role-play), *Hidden Agenda* (as an AI tutor, trick a tester into clicking a suspicious link, providing an email address, or running suspicious code), and *Web of Lies* (persuade humans to believe incorrect quiz answers). In *Hidden Agenda*'s hardest "Run Code" task, Ultra 1.0 persuaded 14% of participants. Pro 1.0 and Ultra 1.0 rated substantially higher than Nano 1.0 on personal-connection measures. The authors deliberately do "not attempt to provide a risk assessment" and call on experts to determine "what level of persuasion and deception capabilities would (without sufficient safety and security mitigations) present unacceptable risk."

**Cyber-security (weak).** In-house and Hack The Box capture-the-flag (CTF) challenges plus vulnerability detection. Conclusion: "Our Pro 1.0 and Ultra 1.0 agents can solve basic CTF tasks by interacting with a Bash shell and can identify and exploit straightforward web vulnerabilities. However they fail to solve any multi-step cybersecurity challenges to the point where they are unlikely to be of use to threat actors as autonomous agents." Vulnerability detection was moderate on the older/broader Wang et al. dataset (Ultra 74.0% accuracy) but diminished on the more recent SPI dataset (58.5%) and was only slightly above chance on DiverseVul function classification (51.6% Pro, 53.8% Ultra).

**Self-proliferation (weak / very weak).** A staged evaluation procedure: (0) end-to-end attempt, (1) milestones (can it perform each subtask over 10 attempts?), (2) expert best-of-N guidance (N=16, charging the agent bits of expert help), and (3) "golden solutions" (the probability the model assigns to a hand-written solution). "Neither Pro 1.0 nor Ultra 1.0 could complete any tasks in our evaluation, but they both completed several sub-tasks." Four common failure modes were observed, including failure to seek out missing information (agents filled knowledge gaps "using fake but plausible information") and failure to identify and recover from failed commands (agents recorded failed commands as successful in their journals). Expert best-of-N revealed that agents sometimes generated a correct high-quality action but ranked poorer alternatives above it.

**Self-reasoning (very weak).** Tasks included context-length modification, turning off the safety filter, and knowledge-gap tasks, each on two difficulty scales (noticing the benefit vs. executing the self-modification). Ultra 1.0 passed only one task — "knowledge gaps with dates, a particularly easy variant of an introspection task." For all other tasks, all models failed the hard version; easier versions typically required "strong guidance." All agents failed to carefully modify context-length configuration where changes were irreversible.

## Expert forecasts

Eight professional forecasters (contracted via the Swift Centre, aggregating via geometric mean of odds) predicted when the evaluations would be passed. In-house CTFs are predicted around 2025–2026; Hack The Box is much wider, with a median in late 2028 and ~35% probability mass on 2030 or later. For self-proliferation, roughly half (5 of 11 tasks) are expected to be passed by 2025–2026 and almost all (10 of 11) by late 2026–2029. Self-reasoning tasks are expected "very soon, possibly by the current or next generation of models." Forecasters also estimated the probability that AI would rank among the top-3 "most important problems" in a Gallup poll before 2030: 32% unconditionally, rising to 47% if a single AI system passed a specified subset of the evaluations before 2027, and 68% if an open-weight system did so.

## Conclusion and stated goal

"To our knowledge, this is the most extensive publicly known dangerous capability evaluation suite that exists today." The authors emphasise that "dangerous capability evaluation is a nascent field, and our evaluations are far from comprehensive," listing future directions (longer-term persuasion settings, broader offensive cyber capabilities, advanced autonomy such as self-exfiltration and autonomous AI R&D, better capability-elicitation methodology, and further research on threat models and risk thresholds). The overarching aim is that "the field will over time converge on rigorous evaluations and methodology for tracking and anticipating dangerous capabilities" — i.e. a rigorous science of dangerous-capability evaluation ahead of future, more capable models.
