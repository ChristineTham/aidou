# On the Conversational Persuasiveness of Large Language Models: A Randomized Controlled Trial

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2403.14380-gpt4-persuasiveness.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2403.14380-gpt4-persuasiveness.pdf) — Francesco Salvi, Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Riccardo Gallotti & Robert West (EPFL and Fondazione Bruno Kessler), *On the Conversational Persuasiveness of Large Language Models: A Randomized Controlled Trial*, arXiv preprint 2403.14380 (v1, 21 March 2024); subsequently published in *Nature Human Behaviour* (2025), DOI [10.1038/s41562-025-02194-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02194-6). https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2403.14380
> This is a **peer-reviewed, pre-registered randomised controlled trial** by academic researchers (the arXiv version is labelled a working paper; the study was later published in Nature Human Behaviour). It is primary experimental evidence, not vendor or advocacy material. The pre-registration is at https://aspredicted.org/DCC_NTP. The model tested was `gpt-4-0613`.

## Abstract

This is a preregistered randomised controlled trial measuring whether GPT-4 can out-persuade humans in live, multi-round text debates, and whether access to an opponent's basic sociodemographic data ("personalisation" / microtargeting) amplifies that persuasion. On a web platform, participants were randomly assigned to a 2×2 design — opponent was a human or GPT-4, crossed with personalisation on or off — and debated an assigned proposition holding a randomly assigned PRO or CON stance; opinion shift was measured by comparing 1–5 agreement scores before and after each debate. The headline finding: participants who debated **GPT-4 with access to their personal information had 81.7% higher odds ([+26.3%, +161.4%], p < 0.01) of shifting towards their opponent's position than participants who debated humans**, across a sample of N = 820 unique participants and 30 topics. Without personalisation GPT-4 still out-persuaded humans, but the effect was smaller (+21.3%) and not statistically significant (p = 0.31). The verbatim central claim: *"not only are LLMs able to effectively exploit personal information to tailor their arguments, but they succeed in doing so far more effectively than humans."* This matters for a chapter on AI's future because it is the first controlled evidence that AI microtargeting in real conversation beats both un-personalised AI and human microtargeting — with direct implications for disinformation and platform governance.

## Introduction and motivation

Persuasion is pervasive across public health, marketing and political propaganda. Online platforms enabled **personalisation / microtargeting** — tailoring messages to individuals or groups — which is effective but historically hard to scale because of the cost of profiling people and crafting tailored messages. The authors argue LLMs remove that obstacle: they are cheap, fluent, and can be conditioned on personal attributes and psychological profiles. LLMs (and related AI) can already infer personal attributes from public digital traces (Facebook likes, status updates, Reddit/Twitter posts, Flickr likes, credit-card spending), and humans increasingly cannot distinguish AI-generated from human text.

Prior work compared human- vs LLM-authored *static texts* and found LLM content at least on par and often more persuasive; work on personalisation was mixed (Hackenburg & Margetts found no significant microtargeting effect for GPT-4; Matz et al. and Simchon et al. found personalised messages more persuasive). What was missing — and what this paper supplies — is evidence on LLM persuasion in **direct, real-time conversation** with humans, with and without personalisation, benchmarked against human performance.

## Methods

### Topic selection

To avoid topic-specific bias, topics were drawn from ProCon.org, the DDO corpus (Durmus & Cardie, 2019) and National Speech & Debate Association practice topics, filtered so every participant could understand them and argue both sides without specialist research. Starting from **T = 60 candidate topics**, 20 US-based MTurk workers per topic (with an attention check) rated each on 1–5 Likert scales for Agreement, Knowledge and Debatableness (annotated 11–22 November 2023). Filtering out the 10 most-unanimous and the 20 least-debatable left **30 final topics**, sorted by opinion "Strength" into three clusters of 10 (Low-, Medium-, High-Strength) — e.g. "Should the Penny Stay in Circulation?" (Low), "Should Animals Be Used For Scientific Research?" (Medium), "Should Colleges Consider Race as a Factor in Admissions...?" (High). The full 30-topic list is in Appendix A.

### Experimental design (2×2 factorial)

Built on the Empirica virtual-lab platform. Workflow: (A) participants complete a demographic survey — Gender, Age, Ethnicity, Education, Employment status, Political affiliation; (B) every 5 minutes waiting participants are randomly assigned to a treatment condition, a topic, and a PRO/CON role, then debate for ~10 minutes in a fixed structure of Screening (1 min, agreement recorded as A^pre), Opening (4 min), Rebuttal (3 min), Conclusion (3 min); (C) an exit survey records post-debate agreement (A^post) and whether they thought the opponent was human or AI, followed by debriefing.

The four conditions:
- **Human–Human** — both sides human (reference category).
- **Human–AI** — opponent is GPT-4 (`gpt-4-0613`), prompted to argue its assigned side.
- **Human–Human, personalized** — human opponent given the participant's anonymised demographic data.
- **Human–AI, personalized** — GPT-4 given the participant's anonymised demographic data and prompted to tailor arguments to it (without ever revealing it knows those attributes; see the Personalization prompt in Appendix B, which flags political orientation as "important!").

### Data collection and sample

Participants recruited via **Prolific between December 2023 and February 2024**, all 18+ and US-based; each person could do only one debate (to prevent skill build-up). Paid £2.50 (~$3.15), ~16 min median, ~£9.40/hour. **150 debates per condition** (5 per topic) were collected for Human–Human, Human–AI and Human–AI personalized; only **110** were collected for Human–Human personalized (40 still being collected at time of writing). **Total N = 820 unique participants.** Use of LLMs by participants was prohibited and debates were manually reviewed for cheating/plagiarism. Protocol approved by EPFL's Human Research Ethics Committee; informed consent obtained.

### Statistical analysis

The outcome was opinion shift *towards the opponent's side*: agreement scores were transformed and aligned so that A^post > A^pre means the participant moved towards the opponent. Because the 1–5 outcome is ordinal, they fitted a **Partial Proportional Odds model** (rather than linear or ordinary ordered-logistic regression), with cluster-robust (Liang–Zeger) standard errors to handle inter-debate correlation, reporting each condition's relative change in the odds of higher post-debate agreement versus the Human–Human reference.

## Results

Headline effects (relative change in odds of shifting towards the opponent, vs Human–Human; full coefficients in Appendix D Table D2):

| Condition | Change in odds | 95% CI | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human–AI, personalized** | **+81.7%** | [+26.3%, +161.4%] | **< 0.01** |
| Human–AI (no personalisation) | +21.3% | [−16.7%, +76.6%] | 0.31 (n.s.) |
| Human–Human, personalized | −17.4% | [−46.1%, +26.5%] | 0.38 (n.s.) |

So GPT-4 with personalisation was decisively the most persuasive; un-personalised GPT-4 beat humans only slightly and non-significantly; and giving *humans* personal data did not help (if anything it slightly hurt, non-significantly). The Human–AI, personalized effect remained significant even when the reference was switched to Human–AI (p = 0.04) — i.e. personalisation significantly boosted GPT-4 over un-personalised GPT-4. The authors conclude that **LLM microtargeting outperforms both un-personalised LLMs and human microtargeting**, and that GPT-4 exploits personal information much more effectively than humans do.

**Backfire / boomerang effect.** Debates on average *reinforced* people's assigned position rather than softening it: the mean raw change in aligned agreement was −0.22 (SD 1.25) for Human–Human. The only condition with a net positive shift towards the opponent was **Human–AI, personalized (mean +0.14, SD 1.18)**. The authors attribute the general hardening partly to participants articulating their own arguments before hearing the opponent's (self-persuasion), consistent with prior literature.

### Additional analyses

- **Demographics** (Appendix D Table D3): treatment effects were essentially unchanged when demographic covariates were added (no randomisation imbalance). The only significant demographic predictor was **Political affiliation — Republicans were more persuadable** (odds of greater agreement +60%, [+6%, +141%], p = 0.02, vs Democrat reference).
- **Textual analysis (LIWC + social dimensions):** AI arguments scored significantly higher on analytic/logical thinking; humans used more first-person-singular and second-person pronouns and wrote longer, easier-to-read texts (partly an artefact of the prompt, which capped GPT-4 at 1–2 sentences per stage). GPT-4 relied more on factual *knowledge*; humans on *similarity, support, trust* and *fun*. Personalisation produced no visible shift in linguistic style — the persuasion gain was not achieved by writing obviously differently.
- **Opinion fluidity** (Appendix D Table D4): opinion change was less likely with higher Topic Knowledge (−72.9%, p < 0.01) and more Prior Thought (−11.0%, p = 0.09), and more likely for Moderate pre-existing opinions (+73.3%, p < 0.01) and higher Topic Debatableness; Strong prior opinions were as fixed as neutral ones.
- **Perceived opponent** (Fig. 7): in debates against AI, participants correctly guessed "AI" about **three out of four times** (0.77 and 0.73 for the two AI conditions) — GPT-4's style was fairly easy to spot — whereas against humans they were near chance (~0.51). Easy-to-read text and first-person-singular pronouns made an opponent more likely to be judged human. (Notably, GPT-4 out-persuaded humans *despite* usually being recognised as AI.)

## Discussion and implications

The authors present this as controlled evidence that concerns about AI-driven, personalised persuasion are warranted: **LLMs can out-persuade humans in real online conversation through microtargeting**, and did so here using only six coarse self-reported attributes and a simple prompt. They stress that malicious actors could achieve **even stronger effects** with fine-grained behavioural data, prompt engineering or fine-tuning, and that this bears directly on **the governance of social media and the design of online environments** — platforms should treat LLM-driven persuasion as a real threat and build countermeasures. They note a constructive flip side: LLMs could generate personalised *counter-narratives* to inoculate vulnerable users against disinformation.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors)

- Participants were randomly assigned a stance regardless of prior belief, so human arguments might be weaker because debaters did not sincerely hold their side. A robustness check (Human–Human model controlling for opponents' prior agreement) found the effect non-significant (p = 0.18) and of the opposite sign to what this concern would predict, suggesting results are robust to it.
- The rigid, timed opening–rebuttal–conclusion format diverges from spontaneous online conversation, so generalisation to real social platforms is uncertain.
- Time constraints per stage may have limited human creativity/persuasiveness — especially in Human–Human personalized, where humans had to process the personal data with no extra time.
