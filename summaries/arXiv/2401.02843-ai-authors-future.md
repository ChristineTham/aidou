# Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [2401.02843-ai-authors-future.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2401.02843-ai-authors-future.pdf) — Katja Grace, Harlan Stewart, Julia Fabienne Sandkühler, Stephen Thomas, Ben Weinstein-Raun, Jan Brauner & Richard C. Korzekwa (AI Impacts, and others), *Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI*, arXiv preprint 2401.02843 (v3, cs.CY), January 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843
> This is a **self-reported expert-opinion survey** (the "2023 Expert Survey on Progress in AI", ESPAI), not a measurement of AI capability or a forecast validated against outcomes. It reports what 2,778 published AI researchers *believe* about the future. The authors themselves stress that AI researchers are experts in AI research, not in forecasting, and that "AI experts' predictions should not be seen as a reliable guide to objective truth."

## Abstract

This is the largest survey of its kind: 2,778 researchers who had published in six top-tier AI venues (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, IJCAI, JMLR) were asked, in the fall of 2023, to forecast the pace and impacts of AI progress. Its central, most-citable finding is that the aggregate forecast gives a 50% chance of "high-level machine intelligence" (unaided machines outperforming humans on every task) by 2047 — thirteen years earlier than the 2060 forecast from the same team's very similar 2022 survey. Most respondents expressed substantial uncertainty and put real weight on catastrophic outcomes: depending on how the question was worded, between 37.8% and 51.4% gave at least a 10% chance to advanced AI causing outcomes as bad as human extinction. The paper matters for a chapter on AI's future because it is the field's own aggregate view of both timelines and risk, from a large, methodologically careful expert sample — while being explicit that expert forecasting is unreliable and framing-sensitive. Verbatim: "the chance of unaided machines outperforming humans in every possible task was estimated at 10% by 2027, and 50% by 2047."

## The survey (method)

- **Population and sample.** 2,778 respondents, all having published peer-reviewed work in 2022 at one of six top AI venues (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, IJCAI, JMLR). This is the third in a series (2016, 2022, 2023); the 2023 edition roughly quadrupled the 2022 sample by expanding from two venues to six. Survey run October 2023 on Qualtrics; preregistered; approved by the University of Bonn Ethics Committee.
- **Response rate.** 15% of those contacted took the survey — described as within the normal range for large expert surveys (surveys sent to >20,000 people typically get ~10%).
- **Question formats.** Likert scales, probability estimates, and future-year estimates. Most questions were randomly assigned to only a subset of the 2,778, to keep each participant's survey short — so most questions were *not* answered by all participants (individual-question n's range widely, e.g. HLMI n=1,714; FAOL n=774).
- **Framing controls.** To probe framing effects, timing questions were posed in two randomly assigned framings: "fixed-years" (estimate probability by a given year) and "fixed-probabilities" (estimate the year for a given probability). Each participant's three year-probability pairs were fitted to a gamma cumulative distribution; aggregate forecasts are the mean over individual distributions.

## Results on AI progress

### The 39 tasks
- All but four of 39 tasks were forecast to have at least a 50% chance of being feasible within ten years. "Feasible" was defined as "one of the best resourced labs could implement it in less than a year if they chose to."
- Milestones with ≥50% chance within a decade included building a payment-processing website from scratch, writing a song indistinguishable from a hit artist's (e.g. Taylor Swift), autonomously fine-tuning an open-source LLM, and folding laundry.
- The six tasks expected to take longer than ten years: output the differential equations governing a virtual world after time in it (12 years), physically install home electrical wiring (17 years), write a high-quality ML paper (19 years) or replicate one (12 years), prove publishable top-journal maths theorems (22 years), and solve a Millennium Prize–type problem (27 years).
- **Shift since 2022:** for the 32 tasks in both surveys, 21 moved earlier and 11 moved later; on average the 50th-percentile expected year shifted 1.0 year earlier (SD=2.0, SE=0.18).

### High-Level Machine Intelligence (HLMI) and Full Automation of Labour (FAOL)
- **HLMI** ("unaided machines can accomplish every *task* better and more cheaply than human workers"; assuming human science continues undisrupted; n=1,714): aggregate 50% chance by **2047**, down 13 years from **2060** in 2022. (For comparison, the estimate moved only one year earlier — 2061→2060 — over the six years between the 2016 and 2022 surveys.) Aggregate 10% chance by **2027**, down two years from 2029 in 2022.
- **FAOL** ("all *occupations* are fully automatable"; n=774): aggregate 50% chance by **2116**, down 48 years from **2164** in 2022 (difference significant, p=.0052). 10% chance by 2037.
- **The HLMI–FAOL gap:** the 50% FAOL date is consistently >60 years later than the 50% HLMI date (about a 70-year gap in 2023), across 2016/2022/2023, despite the two concepts being nearly identical. The authors call this "surprising," do not fully explain it, and treat it as likely a framing effect.
- **Last occupations to be automated** (write-in, categorised by O*NET): top five categories were Computer & Mathematical (91), Life/Physical/Social Science (77), Healthcare Practitioners & Technical (56), Management (49), and Arts/Design/Entertainment/Sports/Media (39).
- **Demographic difference:** respondents whose undergraduate education was in Asia expected HLMI 11 years earlier than those from Europe, North America, or elsewhere.

### Framing and disagreement
- The fixed-years framing again produced later predictions: the 50%-chance-of-HLMI year was 34 years out under fixed-years vs 17 years under fixed-probabilities. Even the larger figure is shorter than 2022's combined 37-year forecast.
- On a "meta" question about whether they disagreed with the typical AI researcher on HLMI timing (n=671): 44% said "Not much," 46% "A moderate amount," 10% "A lot."
- The authors repeatedly note the wide **spread**: "Participants expressed a wide range of views on almost every question." The narrower confidence interval in 2023 vs 2022 reflects the larger sample, *not* respondents converging.

### Causes of progress and intelligence explosion
- Halving any of five inputs over the past decade was estimated to have reduced AI progress; halving the *falling cost of computing* was seen as having the largest effect, and halving "researcher effort" and "progress in AI algorithms" the least — but with a wide spread cutting against the narrative that cheap compute is the dominant driver.
- On an "intelligence explosion" (feedback loop making technological progress >1 order of magnitude faster within 5 years of HLMI): the median put the argument's likelihood at "41–60%"; median probability of explosive progress 2 years after HLMI was 20%, and 80% for 30 years after. Results "remarkably stable since 2016." The median participant did not expect a rapid explosion but gave it substantial credence.

### Traits and explainability
- For 11 alignment-relevant traits of state-of-the-art AI in 2043 (n≈649–667), all 11 were seen as reasonably likely, with much uncertainty. Only "Take actions to attain power" had a median below "even chance" — notable because it is arguably the most sinister. Large majorities thought 2043 systems would likely: find unexpected ways to achieve goals (82.3%), talk like a human expert on most topics (81.4%), and frequently behave surprisingly (69.1%).
- On explainability by 2028 (n=912): most thought it *unlikely* that users could know the true reasons for an AI's choices; only 20% gave better-than-even odds.

## Results on social impacts

### Concerning scenarios
- Of 11 potential AI scenarios (n=1,345), each was rated "substantial" or "extreme" concern by more than 30% of respondents. The most-concerning (by % rating substantial/extreme): spread of false information / deepfakes (86%), manipulation of large-scale public opinion (79%), dangerous groups making powerful tools e.g. engineered viruses (73%), authoritarian population control (73%), and worsened economic inequality (71%).

### How good or bad will HLMI be?
- Views were diverse and not reducible to an optimism/pessimism axis: 64% assigned non-zero probability to *both* extremely good and extremely bad scenarios. **68.3%** found good outcomes more likely than bad; **57.8%** considered extremely bad outcomes (e.g. human extinction) a nontrivial possibility (≥5%). Even among net optimists, 48.4% gave ≥5% to extremely bad outcomes; among net pessimists, 58.6% gave ≥5% to extremely good outcomes.
- On extremely bad outcomes specifically: median 5%, mean 9%. **38%** put ≥10% on extremely bad outcomes; one in ten put ≥25%.
- Since 2022 the share putting ≥10% on extremely bad outcomes fell from 48% to 38%, and the mean fell from 14% to 9.0%.

### Human extinction
- Three separate extinction-framed questions (each participant saw one) gave medians of 5% or 10% and means of 14.4%–19.4%. Depending on wording, **between 41.2% and 51.4%** estimated a greater than 10% chance of human extinction or severe disempowerment — somewhat higher than the 38% from the "how good/bad" question.
- Summarising across four questions, the paper states: "between 38% and 51% placed at least 10% chance on advanced AI bringing these extinction-level outcomes."

### Preferences on pace and safety
- No consensus on whether faster or slower global AI progress over five years would be best: much slower 4.8%, somewhat slower 29.9%, current speed 26.9%, somewhat faster 22.8%, much faster 15.6%.
- **70%** of respondents thought AI safety research should be prioritised more (or much more) than currently — up slightly since 2022 (n=1,329).
- On Stuart Russell's alignment problem: 41% called it a "very important problem" and 13% "among the most important"; 36% called it "harder" and 21% "much harder" than other AI problems — but respondents did *not* generally think it more valuable to work on today than other problems.
- On a meta question, 10.7% said public concerns about AI risk are "Almost entirely" due to misunderstandings of AI research, 44% "To a large extent," 29.1% "Somewhat."

## Limitations stated by the authors

- **Forecasting is hard, even for experts.** Subject-matter experts forecast poorly in general; these participants have no known special forecasting skill. Wide disagreement across respondents limits how many can be near the truth.
- **Framing effects are large.** The fixed-years/fixed-probabilities gap (and a cited related study where estimates of existential risk varied by nearly six orders of magnitude with wording) suggests even aggregate answers are not an accurate guide to truth.
- **Participation bias.** 15% response rate; the authors looked for and did not find response bias large enough to affect results (Appendix D).
- **Sample change from 2022.** Four new venues were added, but the NeurIPS/ICML subset of 2023 respondents held very similar opinions to the full sample.
- The authors' overall stance: these forecasts are "unreliable" but are among the best-informed educated guesses available, and should be one input among many (hardware trends, economic analyses, professional forecasters), not treated as objective truth.
