## Abstract

A qualitative interview study (**14 semi-structured interviews** — 8 juniors, 6 seniors — in **South Korea**, analyzed with Reflexive Thematic Analysis) into *how* generative AI is reshaping early-career software engineering. Its central concept is **Absorption**: GenAI redirects entry-level work into senior–AI workflows, so the developmental work through which juniors once became seniors no longer reaches them. Three consequences follow — juniors lose the "productive struggle" that builds expertise, university-classroom norms structurally reproduce this loss, and a senior–junior perceptual asymmetry blocks self-correction.

**Key points**

- **Method:** qualitative; **14 semi-structured interviews** (8 juniors at the threshold of the workforce, 6 seniors with ≥6 years' experience) in South Korea, Dec 2025–Feb 2026, ~50 min each, in Korean, analyzed via **Reflexive Thematic Analysis** (Braun & Clarke). Explicitly a mechanism study — the sample cannot speak to prevalence.
- **Absorption (the foundational pattern):** GenAI dramatically expands senior individual capacity, so lower-complexity entry-level tasks (code generation, initial debugging, documentation) are handled by senior + AI and never delegated to juniors. One startup founder cut junior headcount "with no impact whatsoever" and asked what a junior can do better than "a 100,000-won Claude subscription."
- **Consequence 1 — Erosion of competence (RQ1):** juniors lose the *productive failure* / *desirable difficulty* (Kapur; Bjork) through which expertise formed — "output without understanding"; they "don't know what [they] don't know." Verification skill, the hard-won human ability, no longer develops ("the cost of code generation is cheap, but the cost of code verification is expensive").
- **Consequence 2 — Collective pressure (RQ2):** in universities, GenAI use is no longer individual choice but a norm enforced by grading curves and peers ("Everyone except me was using GPT … so I had no choice"); faculty tacitly permit it. Structural, not personal.
- **Consequence 3 — Misaligned perspectives (RQ3):** seniors (past the threshold) see the situation as manageable and assume juniors will still gain hands-on experience; juniors experience the threshold receding ("that's not really mine"). Neither side can correct it alone — "Who is going to become the next senior? … nobody has a solution."

**Takeaways**

- The decline in junior hiring is not just job displacement; GenAI is absorbing the **developmental scaffolding** (Lave & Wenger's *legitimate peripheral participation*) that reproduces senior expertise across generations. The pathway was already *unprotected* (informal, dependent on individual circumstance); GenAI has made that consequential.
- Because the erosion operates at organizational and institutional scales and resists self-correction, the fix must be **deliberate institutional design** — in classrooms, workplace onboarding, and how juniors are evaluated — not individual effort. (Analogy: aviation manual-flying rules and nuclear-operator simulator requalification deliberately preserve skills automation would otherwise erode.)

## Introduction

GenAI (Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude) now performs much of the code generation, debugging, and documentation once given to entry-level engineers, and junior hiring has contracted sharply: US entry-level postings at the 15 largest tech firms fell **25% (2023–2024)**; US programmer employment fell **27.5% (2023–2025)**; in South Korea major firms largely suspended open junior recruitment. GenAI clearly boosts individual productivity, but this raises an underexamined collective question: **if entry-level work no longer reaches juniors, who becomes the next generation of seniors?** The authors do not dispute displacement is occurring; they argue the absorbed tasks (minor bug fixes, routine implementation, debugging) are the **apprenticeship scaffolding** through which juniors grow (Lave & Wenger 1991) — so what disappears is the very mechanism that produces skilled professionals.

**Why South Korea:** a critical (not representative) case where the dynamics appear early and clearly — work-related AI usage reaches **51.8%** (almost double the US rate), open cohort recruitment has collapsed, and labour-market structures push adjustment costs onto new entrants rather than incumbents.

**Three research questions:** (RQ1) what developmental capacities are juniors losing? (RQ2) through what structural mechanism is productive struggle obstructed? (RQ3) why do these dynamics persist without self-correction? Contribution is threefold — empirical (the Absorption pattern + three consequences), theoretical (extending learning theory and situated cognition from the individual to organizational/institutional scales), and normative (restoring junior growth requires institutional design, not individual effort).

## Background and Related Work

- **GenAI and the labour market:** structural shift in workforce demand, not just economic cycles — UK analysis of ~11M job postings (2018–2024) found AI-role demand grew 21% while degree requirements fell 15%; GenAI is shifting work away from entry-level tasks, narrowing the bottom of career ladders. South Korea: IT-sector postings fell 43% (2023–2024), entry-level only 4.4% of postings; the Bank of Korea frames this as **seniority-biased technological change** concentrating costs on new entrants. Caveat: causally linking GenAI to the hiring decline is hard — AI-exposed entry-level jobs weakened in early 2022, before ChatGPT's release.
- **GenAI and the nature of work:** AI accelerates individual tasks and resets efficiency as the baseline expectation; employers now expect **AI literacy as baseline competency**. A Korean Delphi survey found creative problem-solving, AI-utilization, and domain understanding displacing raw coding skill as the sought competencies — raising entry barriers.
- **Deskilling** (Braverman): a recurring concern that automation absorbs tasks once requiring deliberate practice. Novices show diminished ownership ("If the machine is as good as me, then what use am I?"); a study of novice programmers found GenAI created an *illusion of competence* and widened the gap between strugglers and fast progressers. Most prior work studies students *or* workplaces in isolation; this study interviews **both** juniors and seniors.
- **Theoretical foundations:** situated cognition (Lave 1988; Brown, Collins & Duguid 1989) and **Legitimate Peripheral Participation** (Lave & Wenger 1991) — competence develops through participation, moving from peripheral low-stakes tasks to central roles. **Productive Failure** (Kapur 2016) and **desirable difficulties** (Bjork 1994): conditions that hurt short-term performance improve long-term learning and transfer; smooth early success often signals superficial encoding. Removing lower-stakes entry-level tasks therefore disrupts the very structure through which expertise is reproduced.

## Method

- **Design:** qualitative, semi-structured interviews to explore how GenAI reshaped skill expectations and career-development pathways for early-career software engineers in South Korea.
- **Participants (14):** purposive sampling from the authors' networks. **8 juniors (J1–J8)** — within two years of an undergraduate ECE/CS degree, all having experienced *both* pre- and post-GenAI periods during undergrad. **6 seniors (S1–S6)** — ≥6 years' experience across two large enterprises, two mid-sized companies, two startups (game, e-commerce, robotics, etc.). Sample adequacy judged by *information power*, not data saturation.
- **Data collection:** Dec 2025–Feb 2026, in Korean, ~50 min each, audio-recorded with consent; IRB-approved (SNU); transcribed (CLOVA Note) then hand-corrected; quotes are English translations.
- **Analysis:** **Reflexive Thematic Analysis** (Braun & Clarke), six phases (familiarization → coding → candidate themes → review → naming → write-up), with an audit trail and active search for disconfirming cases. Themes generally required support from both groups, except where the *asymmetry itself* was the finding.

## Findings

Four themes: one foundational pattern (**Absorption**) and three consequences answering the RQs. The paper's argument map (Figure 2): Absorption + Collective Pressure (RQ2) produce Erosion of Competence (RQ1); Misaligned Perspectives (RQ3) prevent self-correction.

### Absorption of Junior Opportunities into Senior Workflows

GenAI dramatically expanded seniors' individual capacity, letting them handle tasks once delegated to entry-level engineers — so that work **no longer reaches juniors**. A startup founder (S4) cut many juniors "with no impact whatsoever" and saw nothing a junior does better than "a 100,000-won Claude subscription." Across S1–S6, AI absorbed routine, lower-complexity tasks (code generation, initial debugging, documentation).

> [!NOTE]
> **Boundary condition.** One large-enterprise senior (S1) said juniors' work "hasn't really changed much" — the sole such account. The contrast suggests a tentative hypothesis: **absorption operates most freely where institutional structures for junior development are weak or implicit** (startups filtering by efficiency calculus) and is dampened where formal onboarding/training pipelines exist (large enterprises).

### Erosion of Competence through Lost Failure Experiences (RQ1)

As GenAI absorbed low-complexity tasks, the **productive struggle** embedded in them vanished. All juniors reported achieving good results without the struggle they once needed; what remained was **"output without understanding"** ("black boxing"). Metacognitive damage: "I don't know what I don't know"; "I've only seen the good examples, so I don't know what the bad ones look like." One junior (J4) contrasted two courses on the same subject with equal grades — one built durable knowledge, the other "just felt like I got better at using GPT" — showing the institutional signal (grade) can stay stable while actual learning diverges, and current assessment fails to detect it. Seniors converged on the same missing competency ("knowing what should *not* be done") built through years of mistakes; the human skill failure produces is **verification** — "the cost of code generation is cheap … the cost of code verification is expensive" — which juniors, shielded from failure, no longer develop.

### Collective Pressure as a Barrier to Productive Struggle (RQ2)

The loss is *structurally reproduced*, not individually chosen. In universities, GenAI use has become the norm; grading curves plus peer use coerce it: "Everyone except me was using GPT … so in the end, I had no choice but to use it too." Others: "Not using it is just stupid"; "If I don't use it, I feel like I'm falling behind." The pressure is institutional too — faculty tacitly overlook it ("I don't think there was a single professor who told us not to use it"). One junior articulated the *desirable-difficulty* insight ("the process has to be somewhat inefficient — that's what learning requires") and used AI anyway, because the structure left no alternative. Individual resistance existed but was overridden by time pressure and structural constraint.

### Misaligned Perspectives Across Experience Levels (RQ3)

Why does it persist? Seniors and juniors perceive the same situation **incommensurably**. Seniors find it manageable, implicitly assuming juniors will still accumulate hands-on experience and later combine it with AI fluency ("a junior lacks domain knowledge — I think he can easily fill that gap using AI") — yet the very experience they value is exactly what the GenAI-saturated educational context no longer provides. Some seniors even recognize the tension yet hold the expectation anyway ("we can't wait for that time anymore"). Juniors experience it as the threshold receding — "the core is hollowed out"; "that's not really mine." One senior: "We are in a position to evaluate AI … because of twenty years of accumulated experience. The next generation won't be … Therefore, we are fine." Optimistic seniors direct their confidence at a *future* AI-native generation, not today's in-between juniors. Because those who can act (seniors/organizations) don't perceive the conditions juniors face, and those who feel it can't change it, **the conditions for self-correction are structurally absent** — captured by S4: "Who is going to become the next senior? … nobody has a solution."

## Discussion

- **Absorption and the unprotected pathway:** seniors described their own growth as *unsystematized* accumulation of menial hands-on work (willingly doing grunt work, self-directed expansion), dependent on individual circumstance rather than institutional design. The same S4 whose own path was built on such work now sees it substitutable by "a 100,000-won Claude subscription." Absorption proceeds via ordinary organizational rationales (efficiency, headcount, productivity), and **no one is positioned to notice it is blocking the pathway they themselves grew through** — because costs land later, on a different group, than the decision-makers. What was already unprotected is further eroded.
- **Extending learning theory and situated cognition:** productive failure and desirable difficulties were theorized at the *individual-learner* scale; the findings show the conditions they require are set at **organizational and institutional** scales (workplace task elimination; classroom collective normalization). So productive failure must be **designed** at those scales, not left to learner choice. Situated cognition explains the persistence: when practice itself transforms rapidly, perception of the change diverges asymmetrically by experience level, so individual interventions (mentorship, advice) cannot overcome it.
- **Implications for institutional design:** if self-correction won't happen, conditions must be re-established institutionally. S6's onboarding — **"first step, one step, big step"** — takes newcomers from the smallest real change (fix a missing period, commit, open a PR, ship to production) up to self-directed problem-finding ("learning by doing"). Three leverage points: (1) university education — preserve/mandate courses whose objectives AI can't fulfil, making "reducing AI dependency" a quality criterion; (2) hiring/evaluation — assess juniors on their capacity to recognize their own knowledge gaps and errors when using GenAI; (3) workplace onboarding — preserve designated learning spaces as stepping stones, not just efficiency. Cross-field precedents: **FAA SAFO 13002** preserving manual-flying practice; **NRC** simulator requalification for nuclear operators — institutional design deliberately preserving developmental conditions automation would erode.

## Limitations

- Sample of **14** supports mechanism analysis, not prevalence/magnitude claims.
- **Cross-sectional** — senior/junior comparison reflects positional (situated-cognition) difference, not longitudinal change; tracing how today's juniors actually develop needs future longitudinal work.
- Situated in **South Korea** (collapsed structured recruitment, rigid employment protection, world-leading GenAI adoption); generalization to other institutional configurations is a question for comparative research.
- Purposive sampling via personal networks introduces selection effects; deliberate homogeneity (all ECE/CS, all juniors spanning pre/post-GenAI) aids comparison but limits transfer to other fields/cohorts.

## Ethical Considerations & Adverse Impact

IRB-approved; informed consent; data retained 3 years. The authors flag three misreadings to avoid: (1) the findings do **not** show juniors are economically substitutable — reducing junior hiring *worsens* the structural problem; (2) perceptual asymmetry does **not** absolve seniors — it *heightens* the duty of institutional design; (3) the South Korean context should not be used for direct policy transfer.

## Relation to the book

A well-targeted qualitative source for **§6.4 (future of work)** that supplies the *mechanism* behind the entry-level employment decline the book discusses. Where macro statistics show junior hiring falling, this study explains *why it matters beyond headcount*: GenAI is absorbing the apprenticeship scaffolding (Lave & Wenger's legitimate peripheral participation) through which juniors become seniors, so the risk is not only lost jobs but a broken pipeline for producing skilled professionals — "Who will become the next senior?" The book-worthy concepts are **Absorption** (junior work redirected into senior–AI workflows) and the loss of **productive struggle / desirable difficulty** (Kapur; Bjork), which pairs naturally as the deskilling counter-case to the augmentation-can-help evidence in Contractor & Reyes 2026 (§6.5): the same technology builds human capital when it scaffolds effort and erodes it when it substitutes for the struggle. It also complements the cross-national distribution argument — South Korea as a leading-edge "critical case" (51.8% work AI usage) where the dynamic appears first. Citing notes: qualitative, n = 14, single-country, cross-sectional — cite it for the mechanism and its three consequences, not for magnitudes; flag as an arXiv preprint (2026, accepted AIES 2026); and heed the authors' own warning not to let the findings be read as license to cut junior hiring.
