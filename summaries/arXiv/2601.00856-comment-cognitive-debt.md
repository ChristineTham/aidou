# Commentary on Kosmyna et al. (2025), "Your Brain on ChatGPT" — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2601.00856-comment-cognitive-debt.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2601.00856-comment-cognitive-debt.pdf) · Miloš Stanković (Univ. of Vienna & TU Dresden), Ella Hirche, Sarah Kollatzsch, Julia Nadine Doetsch (TU Dresden), 2026 · arXiv preprint [2601.00856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856), commenting on Kosmyna et al. (2025), [arXiv:2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872).

## Abstract

This is a critical commentary — a would-be peer review — of Kosmyna et al.'s widely publicised "cognitive debt" preprint, which used EEG to argue that ChatGPT-assisted essay writing erodes neural engagement, memory, and ownership. The commenters praise the dataset and pipeline but argue the study is statistically underpowered and methodologically underspecified, so its headline conclusions should be read far more conservatively: reduced EEG connectivity does not cleanly equal reduced cognitive engagement, and there is no strong evidence that LLM use harms performance itself.

**Key points**

- **Underpowered for its claims.** An a priori G*Power analysis (rmANOVA, f = 0.25, α = .05, power = .95, 3 groups × 4 measurements, r = .50) says ~**N = 159** is needed; the study had **54** (18/group), with only **18** in Session 4 — and some topic-specific figures rest on **2–4 essays per group**.
- **EEG statistics are opaque.** ANOVAs were capped at **1000** by unstated criteria and their results not reported; FDR correction was applied only "when multiple comparisons were involved" with no stated FDR level or threshold, and p-values were compared only to α = .05 — undermining confidence that correction was applied consistently.
- **"Significant connection" is relative, not absolute.** It was defined as a directed coupling *stronger in one group than another*, so fewer significant connections does **not** mean weaker overall neural activity — a distinction the original blurs. Effect sizes should have been used instead of a "highly significant" vs "only significant" split.
- **Reporting inconsistencies.** A phantom "Brain-only-to-Search-Engine" group appears in Figure 12 (p. 42); Session 4 quoting-correctness direction is reversed between results (p. 37) and discussion; "55 completed" vs "54 analysed" with no stated exclusion criterion; a satisfaction claim (p. 143) that contradicts the reported data.
- **Overinterpretation.** Constructs like "cognitive agency" and essay "value" outrun what the interviews measured (perceived authorship). Counter-evidence is downplayed: the LLM group actually showed *stronger* beta/certain-band connectivity than Brain-only or Search-Engine in places, and the Search-Engine group — also using an external tool — showed **no** quoting impairment (Search vs Brain-only, p = 1), which cuts against a monotonic "task delegation → cognitive debt" story.

**Takeaways**

- Treat "Your Brain on ChatGPT" as suggestive, hypothesis-generating, non-peer-reviewed work — not settled neuroscience. Its EEG-to-cognition inferences are the weakest link.
- The strongest surviving reading is attentional/motivational difference, not damaged learning ability; the "cognitive debt" framing is speculative pending larger, within-subjects, better-controlled, more transparently reported replication.

## Preamble and framing

The authors open by congratulating Kosmyna et al. for initiating important research, collecting a valuable dataset, and building highly automated NLP analysis/scoring pipelines. They frame their piece as **constructive comments to improve the manuscript's readiness for peer-reviewed publication**, arguing several results "could be interpreted more conservatively." Five stated concern areas:

1. Study design, including limited sample size.
2. Reproducibility of the analyses.
3. Methodological issues in the EEG analysis.
4. Inconsistencies in the reporting of results.
5. Limited transparency in procedures and findings.

## What the original study did (as recapped by the commenters)

- Multimethod investigation of LLM assistance in essay writing: neural activity, behavioural outcomes, and linguistic properties of the essays.
- **Four sessions over four months.** Sessions 1–3 were **between-subjects**: participants fixed in one of three groups — **LLM**, **Search Engine**, or **Brain-only** — using the same tool throughout. Session 4 introduced a **within-participants** switch: LLM group wrote unassisted (**LLM-to-Brain**), Brain-only group switched to LLM (**Brain-to-LLM**).
- **N:** 54 in Sessions 1–3 (18 per group); 18 completed Session 4.
- Original's reported findings: significant group differences; stable within-group linguistic patterns; EEG generally showing reduced neural engagement as external support increased; LLM users had the weakest functional connectivity, lowest sense of ownership, poorest recall; effects did not fully normalise after the Session 4 switch for LLM-to-Brain; overall conclusion that prolonged LLM use may impair cognitive engagement and learning-related processes.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The commenters' central thesis: methodological limitations plausibly drove the reported (over)interpretations. Reduced connectivity ≠ reduced engagement, and the data do not support the strong "cognitive debt" narrative without further controls.

## Critique 1 — Statistical power and sample size (the key limitation)

- **A priori power analysis (G\*Power; Faul et al., 2007):** repeated-measures ANOVA, f = 0.25, α = .05, power = .95, three groups, four measurements, r = .50 → **≈ N = 159** required for adequate power.
- The study's 54 (and Session-4's 18) is therefore **likely underpowered**, especially for Session 4 and per-essay-topic analyses.
- Several **topic-specific figures rest on 2–4 essays per group** — far too few for stable patterns, especially given the high inter-text variability of linguistic/NLP measures.
- Consequence: concerns about **statistical reliability, inflated effect estimates, and interpretability** of neural/behavioural differences. Low power (from small N, small effects, or both) reduces the chance a significant result reflects a real effect (**Button et al., 2013**, "Power failure").

## Critique 2 — Reporting inconsistencies

| Location | Inconsistency |
|---|---|
| p. 42, Figure 12 | A metric reported for a group that doesn't match the rest of the manuscript (e.g. "**Brain-only-to-Search-Engine**") — a group that shouldn't exist; raises selective-reporting concerns. |
| p. 37 vs discussion | Session 4 interview: results section reports **higher quoting correctness for LLM-to-Brain-only** than Brain-only-to-LLM; the discussion describes the **opposite** direction. |
| p. 143 | Discussion says the Brain-only group "reported higher satisfaction," but reported data show Brain-only satisfaction started lowest (Session 1) and never reached the Search Engine group's level. |

These discrepancies "weaken the credibility of the reported findings."

## Critique 3 — Reproducibility / transparency gaps

- **Participant count mismatch:** text says **55** completed all experiments, but only **54** are analysed; exclusion criterion for the dropped participant is unexplained.
- Numerous rmANOVAs reported, but it's unstated whether each comparison was analysed separately or whether **multiple-comparison corrections** were applied.
- **F-statistics often omitted**; p-values given without explaining their derivation.
- Criteria for "**significant connections**" in the EEG analysis are ambiguous — unclear whether they reflect temporal changes, group differences, or a combination.

## Critique 4 — EEG analysis (a central component)

- Procedures **insufficiently described**. Apparently an rmANOVA (2/3 groups × 4 sessions) **per electrode pair**, but the number of ANOVAs was **capped at 1000** with unstated selection criteria; **ANOVA results are not reported**, and it's unclear whether the authors focused on initially significant connections or others.
- Reported results come from single-group/session comparisons, implying **post-hoc tests**. **FDR correction** was applied "when multiple comparisons were involved" (p. 78) — but **no FDR level or predetermined threshold is stated**, and p-values were compared only to **α = 0.05**, undermining confidence that FDR was consistently used, especially with ~1000 ANOVAs plus post-hocs.

> [!CAUTION]
> **The relative-vs-absolute error.** A "significant connection" was a directed coupling *significantly stronger in one group than another*. So **no conclusions about absolute connection strength** follow, and a group with fewer significant connections is **not necessarily** exhibiting weaker overall neural activity. Effect sizes would have been more valid than a "highly significant" / "only significant" dichotomy.

## Critique 5 — Overinterpretation and selective emphasis

- Some between-group comparisons are **discussed without statistical testing or confidence intervals**.
- Conceptual claims — "diminished sense of cognitive agency," reduced "value" of LLM essays — outrun the operationalisations: interviews measured **perceived authorship**, not agency or intrinsic value. Differences may reflect what participants **objectively created themselves**, not a subjective feeling.
- Interview data reported only for a **subset** of participants (pp. 32–33) without justification.
- The "Brain-only has strongest connectivity" claim **oversimplifies**: the LLM group showed **stronger connectivity than the Search/Web-Engine group in certain frequency bands** — a more nuanced picture. Introduction also notes greater **beta-band** engagement for LLM vs Brain-only when counting significant connections consistently.
- Pattern of **selectively emphasising hypothesis-confirming findings**, e.g. under-discussing Brain-only underperformance on satisfaction.

### On the "correct quoting" behavioural marker

- Needs a clearer explanation of how participant citations were matched to essay quotations.
- Confounds like **sentence complexity** (plausibly higher in LLM-generated text) should be controlled.
- Reciting a single statement is a weak proxy for learning/memory encoding — **semantic understanding** is more relevant. Participants were reportedly asked about their argumentation (a better measure of engagement) but **those results were not reported**. So "correct quoting" does not strongly support decreased cognitive engagement.

## Design recommendations for future research

- **Session 4 caution:** repeated exposure to the same prompts risks **familiarity/practice effects**; add control groups using the same tool on a familiar prompt, plus control variables (e.g. specific competencies). A **longitudinal design** (author-proposed) and more **naturalistic** use with sufficient writing time would raise ecological validity; involve **control groups in the crossover phase**; subdivide the writing task to better map EEG to cognitive processes.
- **Within-subjects designs** to handle large individual differences in writing ability; systematic **essay-quality comparison** using both teacher and AI scoring to see whether LLM use improves, impairs, or merely alters writing.
- More **natural settings with adequate time** — participants reported **time constraints**, which may have driven overreliance on tools and a distorted view of usage.

## Overall verdict

- Findings likely reflect **attention-oriented differences** rather than meaningful variability in learning ability, in behavioural or (especially) EEG outcomes. Task-driven **attentional control** may substantially drive results.
- **No strong evidence that LLM use negatively affects performance itself.** In the "ability to quote" task the LLM group did significantly worse, but **Search-Engine vs Brain-only was non-significant (p = 1)** — a null effect. This contradicts a clean "more delegation → more cognitive debt" story, since the Search Engine group used an external tool yet showed no impairment.
- The original states unassisted writing produced stronger connectivity across all bands (notably theta and high-alpha), read as the LLM group using fewer cognitive resources. But **lower connectivity ≠ reduced engagement**: theta/alpha variability may reflect **attention shifts, strategy changes, or lower motivation**. The original's own definition of cognitive engagement (focused attention, emotional involvement, task persistence; p. 16) was only partly examined — **emotional involvement and task persistence got limited treatment**, though LLMs might *increase* these (e.g. reduced frustration). The cognitive-load framework introduced early was **not fully integrated** into interpretation.
- Conclusion: a "compelling and intriguingly consistent narrative," but **many conclusions remain speculative** without further control experiments. The field benefits from larger, more diverse samples, clearer operationalisations, and more detailed statistical reporting.
