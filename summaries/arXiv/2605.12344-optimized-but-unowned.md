# Optimized but Unowned: How AI-Authored Goals Undermine the Motivation They Are Meant to Drive — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2605.12344-optimized-but-unowned.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2605.12344-optimized-but-unowned.pdf) · Vivienne Bihe Chi, Roman Rietsche, Andreas Göldi, Lyle Ungar, Sharath Chandra Guntuku (Univ. of Pennsylvania & Bern Univ. of Applied Sciences), 2026 · arXiv:2605.12344v2 [cs.HC], preprint dated 14 May 2026 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12344>.
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording and figures.

## Abstract

A preregistered between-subjects experiment (N = 470) testing what happens when an LLM, rather than the person, authors someone's personal goals. The finding is a **quality–motivation dissociation**: LLM-authored goals were objectively far better-formed yet felt substantially less owned, and people acted on them far less.

**Key points**

- **The manipulation:** all participants wrote an identical guided reflection on their past year, then were randomly assigned to either write three SMART goals themselves (**self-authored**) or receive three goals GPT-5.2 drafted from that same reflection and retype them verbatim (**LLM-authored**). Only the *source of the final goal text* varied; the reflective input was held constant.
- **Goal quality went up, motivation went down.** LLM goals scored ~2.26 SD higher on a SMART rubric (d = 2.26) but produced lower psychological ownership (d = 1.38), commitment (d = 1.19), and perceived importance (d = 1.13). Predicted higher self-efficacy from cleaner goals was *reversed* — self-authored goals felt more attainable (d = 0.62).
- **Behavioural cost at two weeks:** 72.8% of self-authored participants had acted on two or more goals vs 46.6% of LLM participants (a ~26-point gap; χ²(1) = 22.93, p < .001). Self-authored participants also recalled their goals more accurately despite LLM goals containing more concrete memory cues.
- **Mechanism:** psychological *ownership* mediated the authorship effect on every downstream outcome (84–108% mediated); objective SMART quality mediated *none* of the personal outcomes. Ownership measured at authorship (before any action) predicted later behaviour (32% of the effect on action initiation).
- **Who pays most:** ownership erosion was ~55% larger for users low in trait self-efficacy — the very people most likely to reach for AI help.

**Takeaways**

- For a personal AI operating model this is a **counterweight**: letting the AI author your goals/plays optimises the artifact while stripping the ownership and motivation that make you act on it. Delegating the articulation bypasses the "investment of self, intimate knowing, control" routes through which a goal becomes *yours*.
- The design corollary — **scaffold authorship, don't replace it**: refinement over generation (edit the user's draft, don't produce it), walk the user through SMART dimensions one at a time, and treat "does this feel like your goal?" ownership as a first-class metric, not just task completion or output quality.
- Caveat the study is honest about: the LLM arm received a *complete* draft and retyped it verbatim (the extreme end of delegation). Whether **co-authorship** designs keep ownership while capturing AI's quality gains is untested; outcomes were self-report + self-reported action over only two weeks, not long-run objective attainment.

## 1. Introduction

LLMs are being embedded in goal-setting tools — Notion AI, Motion, AI coaching (BetterUp, Rocky.ai) — that generate plans and articulate goals on users' behalf. The intuitive case: well-structured goals outperform vague intentions (Locke & Latham, 2002), and AI can improve goal structure while cutting cognitive load.

But personal goals differ from the usual AI-assistance artifact. An email or essay is a **deliverable** — the user's relationship with it ends at submission. A personal goal's value is realised only in the *behaviour it drives afterward*, and it is **identity-relevant**: it articulates who the person wants to become. Prior AI-writing work shows AI assistance *lowers ownership* of the resulting text; whether that extends to goals — and carries a behavioural cost — is the open question.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Central question: **When an AI writes a person's personal goals, how does AI authorship affect their motivation to pursue those goals?** Central finding: LLM-authored goals were objectively better-formed yet experienced as substantially less one's own and acted on less, with **psychological ownership** the mediating mechanism.

Three contributions: (1) *empirical* — preregistered evidence that AI authorship can simultaneously improve goal structure and undermine goal motivation; (2) *theoretical* — psychological ownership is the mechanism through which AI authorship of identity-relevant artifacts undermines motivation; (3) *design* — implications for goal-setting/self-regulation tools, with low-self-efficacy users facing the steepest cost.

## 2. Background and Related Work

Two research programs look in different directions and never meet: goal-setting theory asks what makes a goal *structurally* effective; AI-assistance research asks whether AI improves *outputs* (quality, task performance, reliance calibration). Neither addresses what happens when structural quality and the motivational conditions for pursuit come apart. Bridging them needs two more literatures: psychological ownership / self-determination theory, and AI authorship in adjacent domains.

### 2.1 Goal Quality, Goal Source, and Goal-Setting Theory

- Goals drive performance via direction, effort, persistence, strategy — **conditional on commitment, feedback, self-efficacy, ability, task complexity** (Locke & Latham, 2002, 2006).
- **SMART** (Doran, 1981) is a checklist for goal *form*, not a full model of why goals work. Commitment is itself a central condition, so quality can't be judged independently of whether the actor accepts the goal (Klein et al., 1999).
- **Goal source** is the bridge: self-set vs participatively set vs assigned. LLM authorship is a *new* source condition — a complete personal goal generated by an automated system on the user's behalf. This makes **authorship, not just specificity**, central.
- Closest prior work, Schimpf et al. (2026a): LLM chatbots that *scaffolded* goal formulation raised goal quality but **did not increase commitment or intention to act** — strongly complementary. This paper differs by varying the *source of the final goal statement* after a common reflection, then measuring ownership, recall, and action over time.

### 2.2 Goal-Support Technologies in HCI

Reviews find goal-support tools implement the easy *formal* components (specificity, timeframes) while under-supporting the *adaptive* components needed for sustained pursuit (action planning, difficulty tailoring, re-evaluation). Behaviour-change tech often optimises engagement with the technology rather than internalisation of the behaviour. AI-assisted decision-making is evaluated on accuracy/reliance; AI writing on quality/productivity/satisfaction/ownership — frameworks fit for artifacts whose value is realised at delivery, insufficient for goals whose value depends on continued engagement. Schimpf et al. (2026b): an AI career coach beat no-support but not a matched written reflection; its distinctive effect was *perceived social accountability*, not self-concordance.

### 2.3 Psychological Ownership and Autonomous Motivation

Three distinct constructs enter once authorship matters:

| Construct | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Psychological ownership** | The feeling a target is "mine," built through **control, self-investment, intimate knowledge** of the target (Pierce et al., 2001). |
| **Autonomous motivation** (SDT) | Acting from one's own volition rather than external pressure (Ryan & Deci, 2000). |
| **Self-concordance** | Degree to which a goal expresses one's enduring interests and values (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999). |

Ownership is **most directly coupled to the authorship process** — it registers whether the routes of self-investment, intimate knowing, and control were travelled at the moment of creation; autonomous motivation and self-concordance are downstream. So ownership is the predicted operative mechanism.

- Parallels: the **IKEA effect** (effort raises perceived value independent of quality; Norton et al., 2012) and the **"I-designed-it-myself" effect** (Franke et al., 2010). The *labour* of articulating a goal builds value the text alone can't carry.
- **Cognitive offloading** (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) is not inherently harmful, but it is when the offloaded component is the very process that builds understanding and commitment — "**metacognitive laziness**" (Fan et al., 2025): better outputs, less self-regulatory engagement.
- **Self-efficacy** as moderator: low-self-efficacy users may gain most from support, but commitment and persistence themselves depend on perceived capability (Bandura, 1997) — so structured support may carry motivational costs precisely where pursuit is already fragile.

### 2.4 AI Authorship and Scaffolded Goal Support

- AI writing gives the closest evidence: users disclaim ownership of AI text (Draxler et al., 2024); AI writing lowers ownership, but longer/more detailed prompts (more user investment) raise it (Joshi & Vogel, 2025). Transferable mechanism: **ownership depends on the user's experienced contribution**.
- Support is most useful when it engages task-relevant cognitive processes rather than just delivering finished output (Göldi et al., 2024).
- AI-mediated communication and the "algorithmic self" (Hancock et al., 2020; Joseph, 2025): AI increasingly mediates the narratives through which people construct their identities — making authorship consequential even when the text is formally good.
- MindMate (Neshaei et al., 2025): higher writing quality/enjoyment when students did *not* use the agent during writing. Design question: does AI *support* formulation or *replace* it?

### 2.5 Research Gap

Two findings exist but aren't joined: AI can improve formal goal quality; ownership/autonomous-motivation constructs are central to whether goals are committed to and acted on. Quality research treats goals as artifacts to optimise; ownership research asks whether they're psychologically sustained. **The missing test is authorship** — whether a goal is experienced differently when AI vs the user authors the final statement. This study isolates it by holding reflective input constant while varying the source of the final goal text.

## 3. Hypotheses

Two-arm between-subjects design. Three theories make **offsetting predictions**: ownership theory and SDT predict less motivation for what was produced *for* you; goal-setting theory predicts well-structured goals raise attainability/self-efficacy regardless of authorship.

Preregistered confirmatory:

| # | Hypothesis | Prediction |
|---|---|---|
| **H1 Commitment** | Self-set goals produce stronger commitment | Self > LLM |
| **H2 Ownership** | AI-assisted text shows an ownership decrement (prior d ≈ 0.21) | Self > LLM |
| **H3 Goal self-efficacy** | Clearer, more attainable structures raise confidence | **LLM ≥ Self** |

Exploratory (baseline perceptions): **H4 Importance** (Self > LLM), **H5 Difficulty** (LLM feels less difficult), **H6 Clarity** (LLM clearer). Follow-up hypotheses FH1–FH3 parallel H1–H3 at two weeks.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 Study Design

Two-arm between-subjects. All participants consented, gave baseline covariates, wrote an **identical guided reflection** on the past year, then were randomised to draft three personal goals either **themselves** or via an **LLM** from their reflection. Session ended with per-goal ratings + participant-level ownership and perceived-authorship. Two-week follow-up assessed persistence and concrete action. IRB-exempt.

### 4.2 Participants

- **N = 470** U.S. adults on Prolific (preregistered; exceeds 176/condition needed to detect d = 0.30 at 80% power, α = .05).
- Baseline mean age 43.4 (SD 14.1); 53.8% women, 43.6% men, 1.9% non-binary.
- Participants who had already set 2026 New Year's resolutions were **excluded** (so goal-setting happened *in* the study).
- Follow-up: **332 returned (70.6% retention)** — 169 self, 163 LLM.

### 4.3 Measures

- **Per-goal, 7-point Likert:** commitment, goal self-efficacy, importance ("reflects what I truly want"), perceived difficulty, subjective clarity.
- **Participant-level:** psychological ownership (4 items adapted from Pierce et al., 2001, incl. one reverse-coded); **manipulation check** = perceived authorship 0–100 slider ("To what extent do you feel YOU authored these goals?").
- **Objective SMART rubric:** an LLM judge coded each criterion met/unmet → 0–5 per goal.
- **Follow-up:** free-recall task first, *then* original goals shown; binary action-initiation per goal ("did you take at least one concrete action?") + free-response description.

### 4.4 Initial Goal-Setting Procedure

- Baseline: New General Self-Efficacy Scale (Chen et al., 2001, 8 items) as trait moderator; then a ≥100-word past-year reflection using goal-neutral narrative-identity prompts (McAdams, 2001). **Reflection prompts identical across conditions** — so downstream differences trace to authorship, not input.
- **Self-authored:** given a one-paragraph SMART definition, wrote three 30–50-word goals, any life domain. Interface enforced word range and **disabled copy-paste** to block external AI use.
- **LLM-authored:** shown three GPT-5.2-generated SMART goals from their reflection (single inference call; system prompt constrained output to three distinct 30–50-word first-person SMART goals, no medical/legal/crisis content). To standardise typing effort, participants **retyped each goal verbatim** (validated per-word).

### 4.5 Follow-up Procedure

Two weeks later (~5 min): recall task (partial recall allowed) → original goals shown → per-goal binary action + description → re-complete ownership, goal self-efficacy, commitment.

### 4.6 Analysis

Welch's t-tests, Cohen's d; mediation / moderated-mediation via Hayes PROCESS v4.2, 5000-resample bias-corrected bootstraps. Preregistered directional hypotheses one-tailed; others two-tailed. Composites = participant-level means across the three goals.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Initial Goal-Setting Session

**Manipulation check.** Clean separation: self-authored rated authorship M = 94.88 (SD 10.90) vs LLM M = 35.77 (SD 31.22); t(290.3) = 27.40, p < .001, **d = 2.53**.

**Objective goal quality.** An LLM judge (Gemini 3.1 Pro) scored all 1410 goals. LLM goals scored higher on every SMART dimension and composite (Self M = 3.70, SD 0.60; LLM M = 4.69, SD 0.14; **d = 2.26**, p < .001). By conventional criteria, LLM goals were dramatically better-structured.

**Motivational outcomes move opposite to quality.** Omnibus results:

| Hyp. | Measure (1–7) | Prediction | M Self | M LLM | Cohen's d | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Commitment | Self > LLM | 6.05 | 4.53 | **1.19** | Supported |
| H2 | Psychological Ownership | Self > LLM | 6.22 | 4.19 | **1.38** | Supported |
| H3 | Goal Self-Efficacy | LLM ≥ Self | 5.70 | 4.98 | 0.62 | **Reversed** |
| H4 | Goal Importance | Self > LLM | 6.30 | 4.92 | **1.13** | Supported |
| H5 | Perceived Difficulty | Self > LLM | 4.66 | 4.09 | 0.41 | Supported |
| H6 | Subjective Clarity | LLM > Self | 6.07 | 6.18 | −0.15 (p = .10) | Null |

- Ownership showed the largest gap (d = 1.38). H3 **reversed**: self-authored goals felt *more* attainable despite being less structured — structural advantage did not translate into confidence.
- LLM goals felt less difficult (d = 0.41) but were **not** rated subjectively clearer (H6 null), despite being objectively clearer by SMART. Where outcomes track structural properties LLM goals hold their own; where outcomes depend on personal investment they underperform.

**Ownership, not goal quality, mediates the authorship effect** (parallel single-mediator Hayes Model 4):

- **Ownership** significantly mediated the condition effect on *every* downstream motivational outcome (commitment, importance, goal self-efficacy, difficulty, clarity), proportions mediated **84–108%**. For self-efficacy and importance, the direct effect became non-significant once ownership was controlled (**full mediation**).
- The **SMART rubric score mediated none** of ownership/commitment/importance/self-efficacy — it only shaped text-level judgment (83.5% of the perceived-difficulty difference). The H6 null follows: subjective clarity tracks the person's relationship to the goal, not the text's syntactic specificity.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The dissociation: goal *quality* and goal *ownership* operate on different psychological registers. The features that make a goal structurally good do not build the features that make a person commit to it. **"AI tools that optimize purely for SMART quality may be optimizing the wrong thing."**

**Ownership deficit is largest among low-self-efficacy users.** Trait self-efficacy significantly moderated the LLM disadvantage on ownership (b = 0.62, p < .001, ∆R² = .015) and commitment (b = 0.34, p = .03), but not goal self-efficacy (p = .53). Moderated-mediation index = 0.39, 95% CI [0.15, 0.62]; conditional indirect effects significant at every level (−1 SD: ab = −1.57; mean: −1.29; +1 SD: −1.01). **Ownership erosion was ~55% larger for low- than high-trait-self-efficacy users — but substantial for both.** The users most likely to seek AI help pay the steepest cost.

### 5.2 Two-Week Follow-Up

Retention did not differ by condition (χ² = 0.11, p = .74); returners ≈ non-returners on baseline measures (all |d| < 0.10).

**Goal recall and behavioural action.**

- **Recall:** self-condition participants recalled goals more accurately on both topic and component accuracy (both p < .001) — notable given LLM goals had *more* surface memory cues (numbers, deadlines, actions). Recall scored by GPT-4o with a structured rubric.
- **Action:** self-condition acted on more goals (M = 1.95 of 3) than LLM (M = 1.43); t(327.7) = 4.99, p < .001, consistent across all three goals.

> [!TIP]
> Headline behavioural figure: **72.8% of self-authored participants acted on two or more goals vs 46.6% of LLM-authored** (χ²(1) = 22.93, p < .001) — a ~26-point / 26% follow-through gap. The baseline motivational gap translated into a real behavioural gap.

**Ownership mediates action initiation.** Logistic mediation (N = 332): baseline ownership significantly mediated authorship → action (ab = 0.41, 95% CI [0.02, 0.81]), **32% of the total effect**; direct effect stayed significant (partial mediation). Because ownership was measured *at authorship, before any chance to act*, it **predicts** later behaviour rather than merely correlating with other self-reports.

**Persistence.** All three follow-up hypotheses supported: commitment (Self 5.67 vs LLM 4.35, d = 0.93), ownership (5.84 vs 4.23, d = 1.15), goal self-efficacy (5.22 vs 4.55, d = 0.52). Both conditions dropped from baseline; the predicted *steeper* LLM decline in self-efficacy was **not** found (self −0.52, LLM −0.39, p = .84). Commitment dropped more in the self condition (−0.41 vs −0.05) — a **floor effect** (LLM commitment was already low). The shortfall was **immediate and durable**, established at authorship and holding across two weeks — not progressive.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Why quality and ownership come apart

Standard AI-assistance logic — better outputs give greater utility regardless of who produced them — **does not apply to personal goals**. Ownership theory: authoring a goal walks all three ownership routes (invest self, intimate knowing, control) at once; delegating to an LLM walks **none**, so even an excellent goal lacks the ownership-building work. SDT: an externally introduced goal *can* become autonomous through **internalisation**, but under full LLM authorship internalisation mostly didn't happen within two weeks — participants treated LLM goals as *received*, not internalised. Mediation confirms the two registers are dissociated.

### 6.2 Ownership predicts later behaviour

Addresses the demand-characteristic / social-desirability critique: baseline ownership (measured before any action) predicted follow-up action (32% mediated, ab = 0.41), with unambiguous causal direction. Ownership is not a transient feeling — it predicts behaviour.

### 6.3 Connections to AI writing research

Extends Draxler et al. (2024), Joshi & Vogel (2025), Kadoma et al. (2024) — ownership erodes when users delegate to AI. **What this adds is the behavioural cost.** An email is finished when sent (cost of low ownership is social); a goal only *begins* at articulation (cost shows up in everything that follows). Goals are identity-relevant in a way emails aren't.

### 6.4 Goal-setting theory assumes self-authorship

SMART, specificity-difficulty, and commitment models were developed under an implicit assumption that **goals are self-set**. This study exposes a **boundary condition on Locke & Latham's specificity principle**: in personal domains the person has rich self-knowledge no external source can access, so outside specificity carries no new information — the structural improvement is real but "psychologically empty." Specificity gains only motivate when they tell the user something they didn't already know. **Goal quality is necessary but insufficient for pursuit.**

### 6.5 Design implications

> [!TIP]
> **Scaffold authorship; don't replace it.** Concrete moves:
> - **Scaffolded articulation** — walk the user through SMART dimensions one at a time; the user's contribution should be substantive.
> - **Refinement over generation** — an AI that *edits the user's draft* preserves authorship; one that *produces the draft* does not. The three ownership routes run through that first draft.
> - **Ownership as a UX metric** — task completion, efficiency, satisfaction can all rank high while users stay disengaged. Measure ownership directly; a simple in-product check ("Does this feel like your goal?") could route low-ownership users to a collaborative revision flow.
> - **For low-self-efficacy users** — the intuitive fix (do more of the work for them) is exactly wrong; scaffold their articulation without doing the articulating.

### 6.6 AI assistance in identity-relevant domains

The dissociation likely generalises to any domain where value depends on continued engagement and the artifact is **activating rather than terminal** and tied to identity: therapy/counselling, educational planning, creative writing, career articulation, reflective writing. The evaluation question shifts from "does AI produce better outputs?" to "**what is the motivational cost of producing them through delegation, and for whom is it largest?**"

### 6.7 Limitations and Future Work

> [!WARNING]
> - **Extreme delegation only.** The LLM arm received a *complete* first draft and retyped it verbatim — this cleanly isolates the authorship effect but may itself amplify distance, and tests only one point in the design space. **Whether co-authorship / partial-scaffolding designs preserve ownership while keeping AI's quality gains is untested.**
> - **Psychological + self-reported outcomes,** not objective behavioural attainment; real pursuit involves attempt–revision cycles the **two-week window** doesn't capture. Longer longitudinal work is needed to see whether the deficit persists or users eventually internalise LLM goals.
> - **Quality measured only via LLM-judged SMART.** Goals could also be judged for personal fit or value alignment; whether AI would still show a quality advantage there is unclear.
> - Future directions: **personalisation** (AI with access to the user's history/values/constraints may narrow the gap), **mechanism** (think-aloud / experience-sampling of revise/abandon/re-engage trajectories), **generalisation** to adjacent identity-relevant domains.

## 7. Conclusion

The assumption that better outputs are better for users breaks down in personal goal-setting: AI can write objectively better goals than people write for themselves, yet people don't own or act on them. Psychological ownership is a critical driver of motivation, and the act of authoring provides motivational force AI can't replicate without significant cost — here a **26% gap in behavioural follow-through**, most pronounced among low-self-efficacy users. The paradox: the users most likely to seek AI assistance are those most susceptible to its ownership-diminishing effects. **AI must scaffold authorship rather than replace it; in identity-relevant tasks, authorship preservation is as consequential as output quality.**
