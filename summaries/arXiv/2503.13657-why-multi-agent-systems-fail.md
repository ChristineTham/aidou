# Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [2503.13657-why-multi-agent-systems-fail.pdf](../../sources/arXiv/2503.13657-why-multi-agent-systems-fail.pdf) · Cemri, Pan, Yang, Agrawal, Chopra, Tiwari, Keutzer, Parameswaran, Klein, Ramchandran, Zaharia, Gonzalez & Stoica (UC Berkeley + Intesa Sanpaolo), 2025 · arXiv:2503.13657v3 (26 Oct 2025); NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks Track.
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording and figures.

## Abstract

The first systematic empirical study of *why* multi-agent LLM systems (MAS) fail: the authors hand-analyse 150+ execution traces to derive **MAST**, the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy — **14 failure modes in 3 categories** — then use an LLM-as-a-Judge to annotate **1,642 traces from 7 popular MAS frameworks** (MAST-Data). The central finding is a counterweight to MAS enthusiasm: MAS often gain little or nothing over single agents or simple best-of-N baselines, and their failures stem largely from **system-design and coordination flaws**, not just weak base models.

**Key points**

- **MAS underdeliver.** Performance gains over single-agent frameworks or best-of-N sampling are "often minimal." The 7 studied SOTA open-source MAS show a **41% to 86.7% failure rate** (Figure 5): ChatDev on ProgramDev fails 86.7%; MetaGPT 66.7%; HyperAgent (SWE-Bench Lite) 25.3%; AppWorld 74.7%; AG2 (OlympiadBench) 38%; Magentic-One (GAIA) 59%.
- **MAST taxonomy = 3 categories, 14 modes**, built via Grounded Theory from 150 traces (avg >15,000 lines each), 6 expert annotators, validated at **Cohen's κ = 0.88** inter-annotator agreement:
  - **FC1 System Design Issues** (~44% of failures) — 5 modes: disobey task spec (1.1), disobey role spec (1.2), step repetition (1.3), loss of conversation history (1.4), unaware of termination conditions (1.5).
  - **FC2 Inter-Agent Misalignment** (~32%) — 6 modes: conversation reset (2.1), fail to ask for clarification (2.2), task derailment (2.3), information withholding (2.4), ignored other agent's input (2.5), reasoning-action mismatch (2.6).
  - **FC3 Task Verification** (~23%) — 3 modes: premature termination (3.1), no/incomplete verification (3.2), incorrect verification (3.3).
- **Scale of study:** MAST-Data = **1,642 annotated traces**, 7 frameworks (ChatDev, MetaGPT, HyperAgent, AppWorld, AG2/AutoGen, Magentic-One, OpenManus), 4 model families (GPT-4/4o, Claude 3.x, Qwen2.5-Coder, CodeLlama), tasks in coding, math, and general agent. LLM annotator (OpenAI o1, few-shot) reaches **94% accuracy, κ = 0.77** vs. humans, and **κ = 0.79** on unseen systems/benchmarks.
- **Design > model.** "A well-designed MAS can result in performance gain when using the same underlying model." Targeted MAST-guided fixes help but rarely suffice: interventions on ChatDev gave up to **+9.4%** (role fix) and **+15.6%** (adding high-level verification) success — yet completion rates stayed low, arguing for structural redesign, not patches.

**Takeaways**

- Composing agents is not free lift. Adding agents multiplies failure surface (bad prompts/roles, broken coordination, weak verification); by default a MAS "unhappy family" fails in its own idiosyncratic way while a single agent may do as well or better.
- Most failures are *organisational*: unclear specs, collapsed "theory of mind" between agents, and superficial verification — fixable by design, not by waiting for better base models.
- Before reaching for multi-agent, verify it beats a single-agent / best-of-N baseline; if you do build one, use MAST as a diagnostic vocabulary and invest in role clarity, inter-agent communication, and multi-level verification.

## 1. Introduction

- **Definitions.** An **LLM agent** = an entity with a prompt spec (initial state), conversation trace (state), and ability to act (e.g. tool use). A **MAS** = a collection of agents that interact through orchestration to enable collective intelligence — for task decomposition, parallelisation, context isolation, model ensembling, and diverse reasoning.
- **The gap that motivates the paper.** Despite rapid MAS adoption (software engineering, drug discovery, scientific simulation, general agents), performance gains over single-agent frameworks or simple best-of-N sampling "often remain minimal." Empirically, the 7 SOTA MAS studied show **41%–86.7% failure rates**, and there is no consensus on how to build robust MAS. Hence the question: **Why do MAS fail?**
- **Contributions:**
  1. **MAST-Data** — first large-scale MAS failure dataset: 1,642 traces, 7 MAS, 4 model families, consistently annotated; plus **MAST-Data-human**, the inter-annotator study traces with human labels.
  2. **MAST** — first empirically grounded taxonomy of MAS failures (14 modes / 3 categories).
  3. A scalable **LLM-as-a-Judge** annotation pipeline integrated with MAST.
  4. Case studies showing failures often stem from **system design**, not just LLM limits, and need more than superficial fixes.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Epigraph, adapting Tolstoy: *"Successful systems all work alike; each failing system has its own problems." (Berkeley '25).* The paper's thesis in one line — MAS fail in many distinct, characterisable ways.

## 2. Related Work

- **Challenges in agentic systems.** Prior work fixes specific problems (Agent Workflow Memory for long-horizon web nav; DSPy for programming agentic flows; StateFlow for state control) or surveys risks, but none give a fine-grained, empirically grounded taxonomy of *why* MAS fail across diverse systems. Benchmarks (SWE-bench, etc.) give a top-down view of aggregate performance; this paper is **bottom-up**, identifying specific failure modes.
- **Design principles.** Anthropic's "Building Effective Agents" urges modular components and avoiding overly complex frameworks; Kapoor et al. ("AI Agents That Matter") show complexity hinders adoption. This work extends such single-agent guidance to the multi-agent setting with empirical data.
- **Related datasets/taxonomies.** Distinct from human-agent interaction catalogues (Bansal et al.), multi-turn conversation taxonomies, code-generation error studies, AgentEval, AGDebugger, and the Who&When dataset (Zhang et al., failure *attribution* to agents/steps). MAST-Data + MAST are, to the authors' knowledge, the **first empirically derived dataset and taxonomy focused specifically on MAS failure patterns**.

## 3. The Multi-Agent Systems Dataset (MAST-Data)

MAST-Data = 1,642 annotated execution traces from 7 MAS across coding, math, and generic tasks. Two construction challenges: (1) MAS failures rarely have a single clear root cause (convoluted interactions, compounding effects); (2) no standardised failure framework existed.

### 3.1 Data Collection with Grounded Theory

- Collect **150 traces from 5 MAS** (HyperAgent, AppWorld, AG2, ChatDev, MetaGPT), 2 task types (programming, math), examined by **6 human experts**.
- Use **Grounded Theory** (Glaser & Strauss) with theoretical sampling: open coding, constant comparative analysis, memoing, theorizing — iterating to **theoretical saturation** (no new modes emerge).
- Effort: **>20 hours of annotation per expert** for the 150 traces (each averaging >15,000 lines of text).

### 3.2 Standardizing Labels via Inter-Annotator Agreement

- Build MAST from the GT findings; refine through **3 rounds of Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA)**: 3 experts independently label 5 randomly selected traces per round, then resolve disagreements (~10 hours total for disagreement resolution alone).
- Iteratively adjust/add/merge/remove modes until high consensus: final **Cohen's κ = 0.88**.
- Figure 3 example: **FM-2.4 Information Withholding** — a Phone Agent fails to tell the Supervisor that the username must be a phone number, causing repeated failed logins and task failure.

### 3.3 The LLM-as-a-Judge Pipeline

- Manually annotating 1,600+ traces is infeasible, so an **LLM annotator (OpenAI o1)** is prompted with the trace + MAST definitions + few-shot human examples to classify failure modes.
- Validation vs. human experts (Table 2):

| Model | Accuracy | Recall | Precision | F1 | Cohen's κ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| o1 (zero-shot) | 0.89 | 0.62 | 0.68 | 0.64 | 0.58 |
| **o1 (few-shot)** | **0.94** | **0.77** | **0.83** | **0.80** | **0.77** |

### 3.4 Constructing MAST-Data

- Generalisation check: apply finalised MAST + annotator to **2 new MAS** (OpenManus, Magentic-One) and **2 new benchmarks** (MMLU, GAIA — a new general-agent domain). Out-of-domain human IAA yields **κ = 0.79**, showing MAST transfers without modification.
- Final MAST-Data = **1,642 traces from 7 frameworks** (the 5 initial + 2 generalisation + Manus). Each trace gets LLM-annotated failure modes plus a reason. MAST-Data-human released alongside.

> [!NOTE]
> **MAST-Data configuration (Table 1):** first block = 7 MAS × 30 traces each, all human-evaluated (HE), human-annotated (HA) and LLM-annotated (LA). Second block = 100-trace ProgramDev-v2 runs of ChatDev/MetaGPT across GPT-4o, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Qwen2.5-Coder-32B, CodeLlama-7b (LA only). Third block = larger math/QA sets: AG2 on OlympiadBench (206), GSMPlus (193), MMLU (168) and Magentic-One on GAIA (165).

## 4. The Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST)

MAST identifies **14 fine-grained failure modes** mapped to 3 execution stages (Pre-Execution, Execution, Post-Execution) and grouped into **3 categories**. The authors do not claim it is exhaustive. They deliberately focus on failures where **system design, coordination, and verification** — not just base-model limits (hallucination, instruction-following) — can improve reliability.

### The 14 failure modes (Appendix A definitions + prevalence)

Prevalence percentages are from the analysis of 1,642 traces (Figure 1); a second figure (Figure 2, over the annotation subset) gives slightly different numbers — Figure 1 values shown here.

| Category | # | Failure mode | Definition (abridged) | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FC1 System Design Issues** (44.2%) | 1.1 | Disobey task specification | Ignores task's stated constraints/requirements | 11.8% |
| | 1.2 | Disobey role specification | Agent violates its assigned role, may act like another | 1.5% |
| | 1.3 | Step repetition | Redundantly re-does completed steps | 15.7% |
| | 1.4 | Loss of conversation history | Unexpected context truncation, reverts to earlier state | 2.8% |
| | 1.5 | Unaware of termination conditions | Doesn't recognise criteria that should stop interaction | 12.4% |
| **FC2 Inter-Agent Misalignment** (32.3%) | 2.1 | Conversation reset | Unwarranted restart of dialogue, losing context/progress | 2.2% |
| | 2.2 | Fail to ask for clarification | Proceeds on wrong assumptions instead of querying | 6.8% |
| | 2.3 | Task derailment | Deviates from the intended objective | 7.4% |
| | 2.4 | Information withholding | Fails to share data it holds that others need | 0.8% |
| | 2.5 | Ignored other agent's input | Disregards another agent's input/recommendation | 1.9% |
| | 2.6 | Reasoning-action mismatch | Actions diverge from stated reasoning | 13.2% |
| **FC3 Task Verification** (23.5%) | 3.1 | Premature termination | Ends before info exchanged / objectives met | 6.2% |
| | 3.2 | No or incomplete verification | Omits proper checking; errors propagate undetected | 8.2% |
| | 3.3 | Incorrect verification | Verification runs but fails to catch the real error | 9.1% |

> [!NOTE]
> Mapping to stages: FC1 root causes are mostly **pre-execution** design choices though they manifest during execution; FC2 is an **execution**-time coordination breakdown; FC3 is **post-execution** quality control. Some modes span stages.

### FC1. System Design Issues

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Insight 1.** MAS failure is not merely a function of the underlying model; a well-designed MAS can gain performance using the *same* model.

- Causes of "disobey spec" (1.1/1.2) go deeper than instruction-following: (1) MAS design flaws in roles/workflow, (2) poor user prompts, (3) base-LLM limits. A good MAS should interpret high-level objectives from minimal but clear input.
- **ChatDev Wordle example:** asked for a standard Wordle game, ChatDev produces a fixed word dictionary; even after an explicit "no fixed word bank, random new word each day" prompt, it still ships a fixed list *plus new errors* — the failure is in how the MAS interprets specs, not the prompt wording.
- Intervention: improving **agent role specifications alone** yields **+9.4%** success for ChatDev with the same prompt and same LLM (GPT-4o).

### FC2. Inter-Agent Misalignment

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Insight 2.** Context/communication-protocol fixes are often insufficient for FC2; these failures demand deeper "social reasoning" from agents.

- FC2 errors appear even when agents in the same framework talk in plain natural language — so standardising message formats (Model Context Protocol, Agent-to-Agent) is not enough. The deeper issue is a **collapse of "theory of mind"**: agents fail to model other agents' informational needs.
- Diagnosis is hard because similar surface symptoms (e.g. "missing information") can come from withholding (2.4), ignoring input (2.5), or context loss (1.4) — motivating MAST's fine-grained modes.
- Likely remedies: structural improvements to message content **and** model-level advances in communicative intelligence (e.g. targeted training).

### FC3. Task Verification

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Insight 3.** Multi-level verification is needed; sole reliance on final-stage, low-level checks is inadequate.

- Systems with explicit verifiers (MetaGPT, ChatDev) show fewer total failures — but a verifier is **not a silver bullet**; success rates can still be low.
- **ChatDev chess example (FM-3.2):** the program compiles and passes superficial checks but has runtime bugs because it is never validated against actual game rules — unusable despite review phases.
- Many verifiers do only superficial checks (does it compile? any leftover TODOs?) despite being prompted to verify thoroughly. Borrow from traditional software: test before committing, use external knowledge, collect testing output throughout, and check both low-level correctness and high-level objectives.
- Intervention: adding a **high-level task-objective verification step** to ChatDev yields **+15.6%** task success on ProgramDev.

## 5. Towards Better Multi-Agent LLM Systems

### 5.1 Failure Breakdown in MAST-Data

- Failure distributions differ markedly by MAS, reflecting architecture (Figure 4, over 210 traces; category split 41.8% FC1 / 36.9% FC2 / 21.3% FC3):
  - **AppWorld** — frequent premature termination (3.1), plausibly from its star topology and no predefined workflow.
  - **OpenManus** — tends to step repetition (1.3).
  - **HyperAgent** — dominated by step repetition (1.3) and incorrect verification (3.3).
- **Model comparison (MetaGPT, programming):** GPT-4o generally beats Claude 3.7 Sonnet and shows **39% fewer FC1** failures.
- **Design comparison (ProgramDev):** MetaGPT beats ChatDev with **60–68% fewer FC1 and FC2** failures, but has **1.56× more FC3** failures than ChatDev — a trade-off, not a uniform win.
- Conclusion: **no one-size-fits-all** fix; profiles are system-specific.

### 5.2 MAST as a Practical Development Tool

- Aggregate success rates hide *which* problems an optimisation actually solved. MAST gives a structured vocabulary for per-mode failure breakdowns, showing which modes were mitigated and any resulting trade-offs — crucial for understanding *why* an intervention works. Packaged as `pip install agentdash` (Appendix C).

### 5.3 Beyond Model Capabilities: The Primacy of System Design

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The authors **conjecture that improvements in base-model capabilities will be insufficient** to address the full MAST. Good MAS design requires *organisational* understanding — even organisations of sophisticated individuals fail catastrophically if the structure is flawed (cf. Normal Accidents; high-reliability-organisation theory).

- Intervention case studies (Appendix H) achieve **max improvements of 15.6%** with the same model via workflow/prompt changes — but **not all modes are resolved and completion rates stay low**. High reliability likely needs *combinatorial* changes from system organisation to model-level improvements.

## 6. Conclusion

First systematic investigation of MAS failure: MAST-Data (1,600+ traces, 7 frameworks), MAST (14 modes / 3 categories, GT-derived, κ = 0.88), and an LLM annotator. MAS adoption "hinges on achieving greater reliability." All artefacts released publicly.

## Appendix — Key supporting detail

### B. Systems evaluated (architectures and failure rates)

- **Per-system failure rates (Figure 5)**, on different (non-comparable) benchmarks:

| MAS (benchmark) | Model | Success | **Failure** |
|---|---|---|---|
| ChatDev (ProgramDev) | GPT-4o | 13.3% | **86.7%** |
| AppWorld (Test-C) | GPT-4o | 25.3% | **74.7%** |
| MetaGPT (ProgramDev) | GPT-4o | 33.3% | **66.7%** |
| Magentic-One (GAIA) | GPT-4o | 41.0% | **59.0%** |
| AG2 (OlympiadBench) | GPT-4 | 62.0% | **38.0%** |
| HyperAgent (SWE-Bench Lite) | Claude-3.7-Sonnet | 74.7% | **25.3%** |

- **Architectures (Table 3):** MetaGPT = assembly line; ChatDev & HyperAgent = hierarchical workflow; AppWorld & Magentic-One = star topology; OpenManus = hierarchical; AG2 = general agent framework.
- **Closed-source MAS** (e.g. Manus) are excluded from deep annotation because the model and full traces aren't exposed; Manus scored ~60% on ProgramDev by human eval only.

### E. Distinctiveness of failure modes

- Category correlations are low (**0.17–0.32**), and even the most similar modes correlate at most **0.63** — evidence the taxonomy captures largely distinct root causes.

### G–H. Solution strategies and intervention case studies

- **Strategies (Table 4)** pair tactical fixes (clear role/task definitions, self- and cross-verification, conversation-pattern and topology redesign, mutual disambiguation, modular agents) with structural ones (comprehensive verification + unit-test generation, standardised communication protocols, probabilistic confidence measures, RL fine-tuning of roles — MAPPO, SHPPO, Optima).
- **Case studies (Table 5), same base models:**

| Configuration | AG2 GSM-Plus (GPT-4) | AG2 GSM-Plus (GPT-4o) | ChatDev ProgramDev-v0 | ChatDev HumanEval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 84.75 ± 1.94 | 84.25 ± 1.86 | 25.0 | 89.6 |
| Improved prompt | 89.75 ± 1.44 | 89.00 ± 1.38 | 34.4 | 90.3 |
| New topology | 85.50 ± 1.18 | 88.83 ± 1.51 | 40.6 | 91.5 |

- Interventions help but inconsistently: with GPT-4 the topology change is *not* statistically significant (Wilcoxon p = 0.4); with GPT-4o both prompt and topology changes are significant (p = 0.03). Topology changes generally beat prompt changes in reducing failures, but gains are modest — "more comprehensive solutions are required."

### I–J. Open-source models and predictiveness

- On 400 open-source-model traces (Table 6), **Qwen2.5-Coder-32B is far more robust than CodeLlama-7b**, and both fail more than GPT-4o / Claude-3 — a real open-vs-closed gap for MAS tasks.
- Failure counts *do* predict outcome: failed traces have more failures overall. Some modes are near-fatal (1.5 Unaware of Termination, 2.4 Information Withholding appear almost only in failed runs); verification modes (3.2, 3.3) appear even in successful runs, so systems can succeed while carrying systemic verification weaknesses.
- Harder benchmarks raise failure rates (Table 8): AG2/GPT-4o FC totals rise from GSM → MMLU → Olympiad.

### K. Cost

- LLM-annotator API cost averages **$1.8 per trace** (Table 9), scaling with trace length: cheapest AppWorld ($0.37), priciest OpenManus ($4.14).
