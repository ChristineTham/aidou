# The Extended Mind — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [clark-chalmers-extended-mind.pdf](../../sources/personal-productivity/clark-chalmers-extended-mind.pdf) · Andy Clark & David Chalmers, 1998 · *Analysis* 58(1), 7–19 · [doi:10.1093/analys/58.1.7](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/58.1.7) (JSTOR: [3328150](http://www.jstor.org/stable/3328150)).
> Study-guide summary of the full document. See the original for exact wording.

## Abstract

A short philosophy paper that argues the mind is not bounded by skin and skull: when a reliably-coupled external resource does work that we would call cognitive if done in the head, that resource is *literally* part of the cognitive process. Clark and Chalmers call this **active externalism** and defend it with a **parity principle** and the thought experiment of **Otto and his notebook**.

**Key points**

- **Active externalism.** Unlike the "passive" externalism of Putnam and Burge (where meaning is fixed by distal, historical facts), the external features here are active — coupled to the agent, in the loop, and causally driving cognition in the here-and-now.
- **Parity principle.** If a part of the world does a job that we would unhesitatingly count as cognitive were it done inside the head, then that part of the world *is* part of the cognitive process. "Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head."
- **Coupled systems.** Agent + external resource in two-way interaction form a single cognitive system; remove the external part and behavioural competence drops, just as if you removed part of the brain.
- **Otto and Inga.** Inga recalls the museum is on 53rd Street from biological memory; Otto, an Alzheimer's patient, reads it from a notebook he always carries. The cases are functionally on a par, so Otto *believed* the museum was on 53rd Street before consulting the notebook — the notebook holds a genuine standing (dispositional) belief.
- **Criteria for counting as part of the mind.** Drawn from what makes Otto's case clear: (1) the resource is a **constant**, reliably available and typically invoked; (2) its information is **directly and easily accessible**; (3) retrieved information is **automatically endorsed / trusted**; and (4, arguable) it was **consciously endorsed** at some past point.
- **Extends to language, self, and other people.** Language is the central means of extending cognition; the self is likewise extended; and cognition can even be *socially* extended into a trusted, accessible other person.

**Takeaways**

- The test for "part of my mind" is functional and criterion-based, not anatomical: reliability, ready access, and automatic trust, not location inside the skull.
- The paper predates AI, but its logic transfers cleanly: a resource that is reliably available, readily accessible, and automatically trusted becomes infrastructure of how one thinks and works — not an external gadget one merely queries.
- Objections from portability, reliability, access-bandwidth, and perception-vs-introspection are all answered as *shallow* differences; what counts is that the information is there when needed and plays the action-guiding role of a belief.

## 1. Introduction

The paper opens with the boundary question: **"Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?"** Two standard replies exist:

- **Skin-and-skull view** — what is outside the body is outside the mind.
- **Content externalism** (about meaning) — the meaning of our words "ain't in the head," and this carries over into externalism about mind.

Clark and Chalmers pursue a **third position: active externalism**, grounded in the active role of the environment in *driving* cognitive processes. (A footnote notes the authors are listed in order of degree of belief in the central thesis.)

## 2. Extended cognition

Three cases of problem-solving are compared, using mental rotation of shapes:

| Case | Mechanism | Locus |
|------|-----------|-------|
| (1) | Mentally rotate the shapes to test fit into sockets | Internal |
| (2) | *Physically* rotate the on-screen image via a button (faster) | Distributed across agent + computer |
| (3) | *Cyberpunk future*: a neural implant does the rotation as fast as the computer | Internal (implanted) |

The argument: case (3) is on a par with case (1) (both count as cognition); case (2) has the *same computational structure* as (3), only distributed rather than internalised. So by what right is (2) fundamentally different? One cannot appeal to the skin/skull boundary — its legitimacy is exactly what is in question.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The parity principle.** "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is … part of the cognitive process. Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!"

These cases are not exotic. Humans routinely **lean on environmental supports**:

- Pen and paper for long multiplication.
- Rearranging letter tiles to prompt word recall in **Scrabble** (Kirsh 1995).
- The nautical **slide rule** (Hutchins 1995).
- The general paraphernalia of language, books, diagrams, and culture.

The mental-rotation cases are real: they reflect options in **Tetris**. Kirsh and Maglio (1994) calculate that **physical rotation** of a shape through 90° takes about **100 ms + ~200 ms** to select the button, whereas **mental rotation** takes about **1000 ms**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Epistemic vs pragmatic action.** An **epistemic action** alters the world to aid/augment cognition (recognition, search) — e.g. rotating a Tetris shape to *see whether* it fits. A **pragmatic action** alters the world because the physical change is wanted for its own sake (e.g. putting cement into a dam). Epistemic action "demands spread of epistemic credit."

## 3. Active externalism

The agent and an external entity form a **coupled system** in two-way interaction — a cognitive system in its own right. All components play an active causal role and jointly govern behaviour. Remove the external component and the system's behavioural competence drops, just as removing part of the brain would.

**Contrast with passive (Putnam/Burge) externalism:**

| | Passive externalism (Putnam 1975, Burge 1979) | Active externalism (this paper) |
|---|---|---|
| External features | Distal, historical — end of a long causal chain | Present, coupled, in the loop |
| Role in the here-and-now | None (passive) | Ineliminable causal driver |
| Twin behaviour | Physically indistinguishable | Can differ completely |
| Guiding question | Is mind *in the head*? | Is mind *in the present*? |

Because the external features are causally in the loop, active externalism escapes the standard complaint against Putnam/Burge (that external content plays no causal/explanatory role in action). If you retain internal structure but change the external features, behaviour may change completely — so those features are as causally relevant as internal brain features.

This view is reflected in a growing body of cognitive science: **situated cognition** (Suchman 1987), **real-world robotics** (Beer 1989), **dynamical approaches to child development** (Thelen and Smith 1994), and **cognition in collectives of agents** (Hutchins 1995). Seeing cognition as extended is not merely terminological; it changes scientific methodology.

**Two objections answered:**

- **"Cognition = consciousness, and consciousness doesn't extend."** Rejected: much cognitive processing (memory retrieval, linguistic processes, skill acquisition) lies beyond consciousness, so the mere externality of a process is no bar to its being cognitive.
- **The "Naked Mind" / portability objection.** The worry: real cognition must be *portable* — resources we can always bring to bear regardless of environment — and coupled systems are too easily decoupled.

> [!TIP]
> **The real moral of portability is reliability, not internality.** For a coupled system to be part of the core of cognition, *reliable coupling* is required. Most reliable coupling happens inside the brain, but reliable coupling with the environment is possible too. If a calculator or Filofax is "always there when I need it," it is coupled as reliably as needed — part of the basic package of cognitive resources. Occasional loss or malfunction does not disqualify it, since the biological brain, too, temporarily loses capacities in sleep, intoxication, and emotion. "If the relevant capacities are generally there when they are required, this is coupling enough."

The brain may even have **evolved to rely on the environment**: visual systems exploit contingent facts about natural scenes (Ullman and Richards 1984) and computational short-cuts from bodily motion (Blake and Yuille 1992). **Language** is a central means by which cognition is extended (brainstorming around a table; a philosopher who thinks by writing). Learning, too, moulds the brain to complement external structures. Extended cognition is therefore "a core cognitive process, not an add-on extra."

## 4. From cognition to mind

Having argued for extended *processing*, the authors go further: some **mental states** — specifically **beliefs** — can be constituted partly by features of the environment when those features play the right role in driving cognition.

**The Otto-and-Inga thought experiment:**

| | Inga | Otto |
|---|------|------|
| Condition | Ordinary memory | Alzheimer's; relies on environment |
| Store | Biological memory | A notebook carried everywhere |
| Task | Hears of MoMA exhibition, recalls it's on 53rd St, walks there | Hears of the exhibition, looks up 53rd St in notebook, walks there |
| Belief before consulting the store | Standing (non-occurrent) belief that it's on 53rd St | *Also* a standing belief that it's on 53rd St |

The claim: the notebook plays for Otto the same role memory plays for Inga. The information "functions just like the information constituting an ordinary non-occurrent belief; it just happens that this information lies beyond the skin." To say Otto's belief only appears when he opens the notebook is as unnatural as saying Inga's belief vanishes whenever she isn't conscious of it. In both cases the information is **reliably there when needed, available to consciousness, and available to guide action** — exactly what we expect of a belief.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In an explanation, simplicity is power.** Explaining Otto's action by "desire + belief that the museum is on 53rd St" is simpler than "desire + belief that it's at the notebook's address + the fact that the notebook says 53rd St." The extra notebook term must otherwise be added to *every* action Otto takes with the notebook, which is pointlessly complex — like explaining Inga's actions via beliefs about her memory.

**Twin Otto.** Twin Otto is a physical duplicate of Otto from the skin in, but long ago wrote (mistakenly) that MoMA is on **51st Street**. Otto walks to 53rd; Twin Otto walks to 51st. The belief is simply *not in the head*. This mirrors Putnam/Burge, but here the external difference is active and here-and-now, with a direct behavioural impact and a central explanatory role (not the explanatory irrelevance of the passive cases).

> [!NOTE]
> A footnote frames this in Chalmers' terminology: Putnam/Burge twins differ only in **relational content** (secondary intension); Otto and Twin Otto differ in **notional content** (primary intension), the content that governs cognition — because the cognitive system is itself extended to include the notebook.

**The moral:** "there is nothing sacred about skull and skin. What makes some information count as a belief is the *role it plays*," and that role can be played from outside the body. To use "belief" this wider way makes the notion deeper, more unified, more useful in explanation — closer to a natural kind.

**Objections that the Otto/Inga difference is "deep" — all answered as shallow:**

| Alleged difference | Reply |
|---|---|
| Inga has more **reliable** access (someone could take Otto's notebook) | Constancy matters — a *one-off* guidebook wouldn't count — but Otto's access is very reliable; Inga's isn't perfect either (a surgeon, or too much to drink). Mere possibility of tampering doesn't deny belief. |
| Otto's access **comes and goes** (showers, darkness) | Occasional temporary disconnection is fine; Inga's belief doesn't vanish in sleep/intoxication. What counts is that information is *easily available when needed*. |
| Inga has **higher-bandwidth** access | Bandwidth alone doesn't distinguish belief from non-belief. Cf. **Lucy**, whose biological memory has only a low-grade link — she still believes, as long as the info is accessible. |
| Otto accesses by **perception**, Inga by **introspection** | Begs the question: from the standpoint of the coupled system, notebook→brain flow is not perceptual but akin to intra-brain information flow. The only real difference is an associated *perceptual phenomenology*, which shouldn't change belief status (cf. the **Terminator**, who "sees" recalled memories in his visual field yet has standing beliefs). |
| Only **occurrent** beliefs are true beliefs | Too extreme — it rules out most of Inga's (and everyday) beliefs. Once dispositional beliefs are admitted, Otto's notebook has all the relevant dispositions. |

## 5. Beyond the outer limits

How far does the thesis go? The authors raise puzzle cases without giving categorical answers: the label-hanging amnesiacs of *One Hundred Years of Solitude*; whether a Filofax counts as memory; a tampered notebook; the page in front of me before I read it; cognition "spread across the Internet."

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Criteria that make extended belief clearly applicable** (the load-bearing part for working with any trusted tool):
> 1. **Constancy** — the resource is a constant in one's life; in relevant situations one *rarely takes action without consulting it*.
> 2. **Direct availability** — the information is directly available without difficulty.
> 3. **Automatic endorsement / trust** — upon retrieval, one *automatically endorses* the information.
> 4. **(Arguable) Past conscious endorsement** — the information was consciously endorsed at some past point and is there because of it. Its status as a criterion is disputed; the first three are the crucial ones.

As puzzle cases lack these features, the applicability of "belief" gradually falls off. A Filofax consulted before every relevant action resembles Otto's notebook; one often ignored ("I don't know") counts less. **The Internet "is likely to fail on multiple counts, unless I am unusually computer-reliant, facile with the technology, and trusting,"** though certain files on one's own computer may qualify. Intermediate cases may be indeterminate or context-dependent, but central cases remain clear.

> [!NOTE]
> A footnote flags that the constancy and past-endorsement criteria may make *history* partly constitutive of belief; one could give a purely dispositional reading and drop the past-endorsement criterion, or allow a historical component so long as the main burden is carried by features of the present.

**Socially extended cognition.** Mental states could be partly constituted by *other people*: in an unusually interdependent couple, one partner's beliefs may play the notebook-role for the other. What is central is "a high degree of **trust, reliance, and accessibility**." Examples: a waiter as a repository of one's meal preferences (an "extended desire"); beliefs embodied in one's secretary, accountant, or collaborator. The burden of coupling between agents is carried by **language** — not a mirror of inner states but a *complement* to them, a tool that extends cognition in ways on-board devices cannot. The intellectual explosion of recent evolutionary time may owe as much to this linguistically-enabled extension as to any internal development.

**The extended self.** An extended mind implies an extended *self*. We already accept the self outstrips consciousness (dispositional beliefs are part of who I am); those boundaries may fall beyond the skin too. Otto is best regarded as an **extended system** — a coupling of biological organism and external resources. Resisting this would shrink the self to a bundle of occurrent states, threatening its psychological continuity.

**Consequences.** Beyond philosophy of mind and cognitive-science methodology, there are moral and social implications: interfering with someone's *environment* may carry the moral significance of interfering with their *person*; some social activity might be reconceived as more akin to thought than to communication. "Once the hegemony of skin and skull is usurped, we may be able to see ourselves more truly as creatures of the world."
