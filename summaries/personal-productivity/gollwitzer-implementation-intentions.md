# Implementation Intentions: Strong Effects of Simple Plans — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [gollwitzer-implementation-intentions.pdf](../../sources/personal-productivity/gollwitzer-implementation-intentions.pdf) · Peter M. Gollwitzer (Universität Konstanz), 1999 · *American Psychologist*, 54(7), 493–503 · [doi:10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493).

## Abstract

A review of Gollwitzer's research program arguing that a *goal intention* ("I intend to reach X") is far more reliably realised when it is furnished with an *implementation intention* — a simple if-then plan of the form "**When situation X arises, I will perform response Y**". The plan pre-commits the person to a specific cue and response, delegating control of the behaviour to the anticipated situation so that action initiation becomes automatic — swift, efficient, and without conscious intent.

**Key points**

- **Two kinds of intention.** A *goal intention* specifies a desired end state ("I intend to reach X"). An *implementation intention* is subordinate to it and specifies the *when, where, and how* ("When situation X arises, I will perform response Y"), linking an anticipated cue to a goal-directed response.
- **Good intentions alone are weak.** Under Ajzen's theory of planned behaviour, intentions explain only **20–30%** of the variance in behaviour; the gap is largely people who hold good intentions but fail to act ("inclined abstainers").
- **The core finding, with large effect sizes:** for *difficult* Christmas-break projects, ~**2/3** of participants with implementation intentions completed them vs. only ~**1/4** without (Gollwitzer & Brandstätter, 1997, Study 1). Writing a report within 48 hours: **3/4** (implementation intention) vs. **1/3** (control), Study 2. Breast self-examination: **100%** vs. **53%** for strong goal intentions alone (Orbell et al., 1997). Vigorous exercise: **91%** vs. **39%** compliance (Milne et al., 1999). Opiate addicts writing a CV: **80%** vs. **0%** (Remlinger, 1997).
- **Mechanism — delegating control to the situation.** The chosen cue's mental representation becomes highly activated and accessible (better detected, attended, recalled); the cue-response link becomes automatic. Gollwitzer's metaphor: "passing the control of one's behaviour on to the environment", switching from effortful conscious control to control by situational cues. Implementation intentions create "instant habits".
- **Boundary conditions.** They only work atop a *strong* goal intention, and require commitment to the plan itself. Effects persist over time (still present at 48 hours, over 3 weeks) but disappear if the goal is abandoned.

**Takeaways**

- The gain is *not* more motivation — it is settling the *when/where/how* decision once, in advance, so the situation triggers the action instead of willpower having to. This grounds the idea of a reusable "play": a decision made once and re-triggered by a cue, rather than remade each time.
- A pre-committed if-then plan robustly closes the intention–behaviour gap, most dramatically where getting started is hard, where the task is unpleasant, or where the actor has impaired conscious control.
- Implementation intentions help not only with *getting started* but also with *shielding* an ongoing pursuit from distractions, bad habits, and competing goals.

## Introduction: do good intentions deserve their bad reputation?

Good intentions (New Year's resolutions) are folklorically distrusted ("the road to hell is paved with good intentions"; Oscar Wilde). But empirically, strong intentions *are* realised more often than weak ones. The problem is that the relationship is modest:

- Intentions account for only **20–30%** of the variance in behaviour.
- The intention–behaviour link varies drastically by behaviour type, and *past behaviour* is often a better predictor than intention.
- The weak link is largely due to people forming good intentions but **failing to act on them** (Orbell & Sheeran, 1998).

The needed advance is therefore not "intend harder" but a theory of *how* good intentions can be implemented effectively.

## Determinants of effective goal pursuit

Setting a goal is a commitment to a desired outcome, but the distance from goal setting to goal attainment is long: one must get started and persist. Research on goal striving identifies two families of variables.

**How goals are framed:**

| Framing that helps | Contrast that helps less | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Challenging, *specific* goals | Challenging but vague "do your best" goals | Locke & Latham, 1990 |
| *Proximal* goals | *Distal* goals | Bandura & Schunk, 1981 |
| *Learning* goals | *Performance* goals | Dweck, 1996 |
| *Promotion* goals | *Prevention* goals | Higgins, 1997 |

**Self-regulatory skills** for initiating and completing behaviour: shielding the pursuit from distractions via control strategies (emotion control, environment control; Kuhl, 1984), and reconciling conflicting goals through creative integration (e.g. studying + socialising → studying in groups; Cantor & Blanton, 1996).

## Preparing goal pursuit: predeciding beats deciding in situ

Goals can be reached many ways — flexible when coping with failure, but a *curse* when one needs to act swiftly, because the person must still decide *when, where, and how*.

- **In situ decisions** (made only when the moment arrives) are restricted to the present situation and hamper effective pursuit.
- **Anticipative decisions** can weigh the whole array of opportunities and behaviours, select the best, and fire immediately when a relevant situation appears.
- Predeciding also makes action initiation **efficient** (few cognitive resources), possibly without conscious intent, and protects the pursuit from distractions, bad habits, and competing goals.

## Goal intentions versus implementation intentions

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The central distinction (Gollwitzer, 1993):
> - **Goal intention** — structure "**I intend to reach X**", where X is a behaviour or outcome. Translates a desire into a binding commitment obligating the person to realise the goal.
> - **Implementation intention** — structure "**When situation X arises, I will perform response Y**". Subordinate to a goal intention; specifies the *when, where, and how*, linking an anticipated opportunity to a goal-directed response. Here the person commits not their self to an end state but to *responding to a specific situation in a specific way*, in service of the goal intention.

Two mechanisms follow:

1. **On the situation side** — selecting a future opportunity makes its mental representation **highly activated and accessible** (consistent with decisions making decision-consistent information more accessible; Dellarosa & Bourne, 1984). The critical cue is easier to detect, to attend to even while busy, and to recall.
2. **On the behaviour side** — linking a chosen behaviour to the cue **automatises** it: once the cue appears, initiation is swift, efficient, and needs no conscious intent (features of automaticity; Bargh, 1997), rooted in fast memory retrieval. A strong link can form in one mental act and benefits from mental rehearsal.

> [!NOTE]
> Gollwitzer's metaphor: forming implementation intentions is **"passing the control of one's behaviour on to the environment"** (1993, p. 173) — strategically switching from conscious, effortful control to automatic control by selected situational cues.

## Implementation intentions and action initiation

Getting started fails when people are absorbed, ruminating, emotional, or tired, so an opportunity fails to attract attention — or when opportunities are non-obvious or fleeting (e.g. a migraine patient missing the symptom onset for medication). Implementation intentions target exactly this.

| Study | Task | With implementation intention | Without (goal intention only) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997, Study 1 | *Difficult* Christmas-break project | ~2/3 completed | ~1/4 completed |
| Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997, Study 1 | *Easy* project | ~80% | ~80% (no added benefit) |
| Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997, Study 2 | Write report within 48 h | 3/4 (~75%) | 1/3 (~33%) |

> [!TIP]
> Automatisation via implementation intentions gives a **strong benefit only when action initiation is difficult**. When starting is easy (the easy projects, ~80% either way), there is nothing for the plan to add.

## Implementation intentions and unpleasant goal pursuits

Health-promotion / disease-prevention goals are a hard test — immediate costs, long-term rewards — and implementation intentions pass it:

| Study | Behaviour | With implementation intention | Goal intention only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orbell, Hodgkins & Sheeran 1997 | Breast self-examination in next month (strong-intenders) | **100%** | **53%** |
| Sheeran & Orbell 1999 | Daily vitamin pill (2- and 3-week studies) | Fewer pills missed | More missed |
| Orbell & Sheeran (in press) | Resuming activity after joint-replacement surgery | 18/32 activities resumed sooner (at 3-month follow-up) | Later |
| Milne, Orbell & Sheeran 1999 | Vigorous exercise, 20 min next week | **91%** compliance | 39% (motivational intervention); 29% (baseline) |
| Verplanken & Faes (in press) | Eating fruit/vegetables | Increased | — |

Leventhal's classic fear-appeal work (1965, 1967) fits: high fear appeals changed behaviour (stop smoking, tetanus shots) **only** when paired with specific *when/where/how* instructions — i.e. strong goal intentions produce drastic change only when accompanied by implementation intentions.

## Effects in critical populations

Implementation intentions help most where conscious action control is impaired — evidence that the effect runs on **automatic** processes.

- **Opiate addicts in withdrawal** (Remlinger, 1997): asked to write a CV before 5 p.m. With *relevant* implementation intentions, **80%** handed it in; with *irrelevant* ones (specifying lunch time/place), **0%**.
- **Schizophrenic patients** (Schmitt, 1997): on a go/no-go task, forming "Whenever number 3 appears, I will respond particularly fast!" sped responses much more than a control preparation (writing "3" repeatedly).
- **Frontal-lobe patients** (Lengfelder & Gollwitzer, 1998): implementation intentions sped action initiation even under a demanding secondary task; the effect was *stronger* in frontal-lobe patients than in student controls, and strongest in those with the worst Tower-of-Hanoi scores (weakest conscious control). Kimberg & Farah's (1993) model of weakened working-memory associations explains why such patients gain the most.
- **The elderly** (Park, in press): prospective-memory failure comes from not specifying cues ahead and absent-mindedness at the moment; automatic memory is age-invariant, so pre-specifying cues (an implementation intention) should aid prospective memory.

## The mediation of implementation intention effects

### The specified situation

Evidence that the anticipated cue becomes highly activated and accessible:

- **Perceptual** — Steller (1992): using an Embedded Figures Test cue as the critical cue improved detection of the hidden figure.
- **Attentional** — dichotic-listening (Gollwitzer, 1996): critical cue words on the *unattended* channel disrupted shadowing, i.e. they grabbed attention despite effort to ignore them.
- **Mnemonic** — Seehausen, Bayer & Gollwitzer (1994): options named in implementation intentions were recalled better both immediately and 48 hours later.
- **Mediation shown directly** — Aarts, Dijksterhuis & Midden (in press): implementation intention participants collected a free food coupon more successfully *and* were faster in a lexical-decision task on the cue words; the faster (more accessible) responses **mediated** the effect on goal completion.

### The specified goal-directed behaviour

Evidence of automatisation — immediate, efficient, and without conscious intent:

- **Immediacy** — Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997, Study 3: implementation intention participants rebutted racist remarks more immediately when openings arose.
- **Efficiency** — Brandstätter 1992, Study 2: in a dual-task paradigm the go/no-go speed-up for "3" was equally high whether the primary task's cognitive load was low or high.
- **No conscious intent** — Malzacher (1992): after forming an implementation intention to confront an insulting experimenter, participants' reading speeds for negative/positive adjectives shifted when the experimenter's face was flashed *subliminally* (<10 ms), a pattern absent in goal-intention-only participants. Analogy: acting on the cue is like starting to drive when the light turns green — no conscious intent to press the pedal.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **"Instant habits."** Implementation intentions match the speed-up of true habit (Aarts & Dijksterhuis, 1999), and in the BSE study habit predicted behaviour for those *without* implementation intentions but had **zero** predictive power for those *with* them. The difference from habit is origin: habit comes from repeated practice, an implementation intention from **one mental act** of pairing behaviour to cue — yet it is as effective at automatising initiation.

## The strength of implementation intention effects

What makes the effect strong or weak:

- **Commitment to the implementation intention itself** — high vs. low commitment framing (Seehausen et al., 1994) and self-affirming "I strongly intend to follow the plan!" (Steller, 1992) both strengthened effects.
- **Strength of the underlying goal intention** — effects appear only when the goal intention is strong (BSE benefit only for strong intenders; Orbell et al., 1997). If the goal is weak, completed, or abandoned, effects should not fire (and would harm other goals if they did).
- **Abandoned goals** — Seehausen et al. (1994): once told the goal no longer had to be reached, the effect did not vanish instantly but was **completely gone after 48 hours**.
- **Temporal stability** — as mental procedures persist even with little practice, effects last: still observed at 48 hours (Seehausen et al.) and over three weeks (Sheeran & Orbell, 1999), provided the goal intention remains.

## Protecting an ongoing goal pursuit from intrusions

Getting started is only one problem; a pursuit must also be *shielded* from distractions, conflicting habits, and competing goals.

### Resistance to distractions

Schaal & Gollwitzer (1999): participants doing tedious arithmetic while award-winning commercials interrupted. Goal intentions ("I will not get distracted!") protected performance less than implementation intentions. Phrasing mattered:

| Implementation intention type | Form | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Distraction-inhibiting** | "Whenever the distraction arises, I will ignore it!" | Wards off distractions generally |
| **Task-facilitating** | "Whenever the distraction arises, I will increase my efforts!" | Helps only at low/medium motivation; at high motivation, may cause *overmotivation* and undermine performance |

> [!TIP]
> Effective willing is more "cold" skilful cognition than "hot" mobilisation of effort. Advise the distractible (e.g. students doing homework) to plan to **ignore** distractions rather than to try harder.

### Inhibiting unwanted habitual responses

An implementation intention that names the critical stimulus and links it to a new-goal response can block a habitual response (e.g. dessert menu → "order fruit", pre-empting "order cheesecake"). This extends to emotional and cognitive responses:

- **Stereotypes and prejudice** (Gollwitzer, Schaal, Moskowitz, Hammelbeck & Wasel, 1999): plans like "Whenever I see an old person, I tell myself: Don't stereotype!" eliminated automatic activation of age stereotypes (semantic priming); "Whenever I see this person, I will ignore her gender!" removed automatic gender-stereotype activation (primed Stroop); "No prejudice!" plans removed automatic negative evaluation of homeless people (affect priming).

### Blocking competing habitual goals

Drawing on auto-motive theory (Bargh, 1990) — a repeatedly/consistently pursued goal can be triggered by its situation outside awareness — Gollwitzer (1998): furnishing a set goal (e.g. driving at a target speed in a simulator) with implementation intentions stopped antagonistic goals primed outside awareness (being fast/slow) from intruding on driving speed. Practical example: before a party, set the goal "socialise" and plan for it, so the party fails to trigger the bad habit of discussing work.

## Conclusion

Goals are more likely realised when furnished with implementation intentions that link anticipated opportunities to intended behaviours. The strategy **delegates control of behaviour to specified environmental stimuli**, deliberately harnessing automatic control to overcome problems of initiating, distractions, bad habits, and competing goals.

Conditions: implementation intentions must rest on **strong goal intentions**; some types work better than others; and commitment to the plan matters.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why it matters now.** In modern societies, shared habits and automatic goals no longer govern most behaviour, and goals are often fuzzy and conflicting (Oettingen, 1997; Karoly, 1998). Action control through implementation intentions is most valuable precisely where habit cannot be relied on.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Cost — reduced flexibility.** Committing to a specific behaviour in a specific situation can lower openness to alternatives. But the cost is limited: (1) people can cancel the effect by dropping commitment to the goal or the plan; (2) delegating control frees cognitive capacity that can be used to notice alternatives. Rigidity does arise when the specified situation is actually met — but that rigidity is *functional*, protecting the pursuit from intrusions.

It is not yet known whether people appreciate how effective the strategy is; in Gollwitzer & Brandstätter (1997, Study 1) about **two thirds** had formed implementation intentions on their own, suggesting it is a popular tool. Given its easy application and reliably strong effects, it is justified to advise furnishing resolutions with implementation intentions — once formed, the behaviour is triggered automatically when the specified situation is encountered.
