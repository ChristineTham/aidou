# On-the-Job Exposure to AI Among Lower-Income Workers — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [frbsf-ai-exposure-lower-income-workers.md](../../sources/ai-futures/frbsf-ai-exposure-lower-income-workers.md) · Elizabeth Kneebone & Natalie Holmes, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, November 2025 · Community Development Research Brief 2025-03 · doi:10.24148/cdrb2025-03 · <https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/community-development-research-briefs/2025/11/job-exposure-to-ai-among-lmi-workers/>.
> Study-guide summary of the full 37-page report (landing-page markdown is the saved source; the full PDF was read for detail).

## Abstract

A mixed-methods Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco brief measuring how many — and what kinds of — U.S. workers are highly exposed to generative AI on the job, with a focus on **lower-income (low- to moderate-income) households**. Combining an O*NET-based exposure metric with 2023 American Community Survey (ACS) microdata and seven roundtables/listening sessions (~57–60 stakeholders), it finds that while the most-exposed workers are disproportionately higher-earning white-collar workers, **more than 6 million lower-income workers (20% of the exposed workforce)** are also highly exposed — concentrated in office/administrative jobs and service industries — and that stakeholders fear AI's benefits and its risks will be **unevenly shared** absent guardrails and supports.

**Key points**

- **30.6 million workers (>17% of all earners)** are highly exposed to AI (top 25% of occupations by O*NET exposure). Of these, **20% (>6 million)** live in lower-income households (below 80% of area median income).
- Lower-income exposed workers are **more likely than average to work in Office and Administrative Support** — fully **half** of exposed lower-income workers hold such jobs, vs. **one in three** of all exposed workers — and in service industries like **Health Care and Social Assistance** (12.6% vs 9.1%) and the **nonprofit** sector.
- Exposed lower-income workers are **older, more educated, and higher-earning** than lower-income workers overall (median $28,000 vs $23,000; 62% female vs 51%; more likely to hold some college/BA) — the exposed are the relatively better-off within the lower-income group, and their jobs are "often among the better-paid jobs lower-income workers tend to hold."
- Qualitatively, organisations are in a **"learning phase"**; where AI is adopted, **early employment impacts are already apparent** (task shifts, recalibrated hiring, held-off hiring, some position eliminations), often driven by resource/funding constraints.
- Stakeholders stressed **critical thinking / "human skills"** as the new essential, and voiced concern that **uneven AI impacts could worsen outcomes for vulnerable workers** unless "adequate guardrails and supports are in place," pointing to the existing **digital divide** (device/connectivity gaps) as a compounding risk.

**Takeaways**

- AI exposure is **not confined to the affluent**: a large, distinct segment of lower-income workers is exposed, but they have "the fewest resources to navigate any such AI-related job or earnings losses."
- The report frames an emerging **"AI divide"**: those better positioned to adopt AI (with tools, training, philanthropic/flexible funding, digital access) capture augmentation benefits, while lower-income and digitally-underserved workers face exposure and downside risk without commensurate support — supporting the case for training, job-search assistance, and wraparound supports (even a "Trade Adjustment Assistance for the age of AI").

## Introduction

- Gen AI is widely treated as a **general-purpose technology** (like the steam engine, PCs, the Internet; Calvino, Haerle & Liu 2025); it is hard to predict whether benefits will be broadly or unevenly shared.
- As part of the U.S. central bank, the FRBSF studies how the Twelfth District economy — employers, workers, communities — may be affected. Technological transitions shift employment across occupations, industries, and populations.
- **Prior research** finds AI-exposed workers tend to be **higher-earning, white-collar** (Eckhardt & Goldschlag 2025), but impacts will reach a broader swath. The open questions: how broadly will benefits (or downside risks — earnings/job losses) be shared across the income spectrum? The authors' earlier work shows **lower-income workers have the fewest resources** to weather AI-related losses (Kneebone & Holmes 2025).

## Methods

- **Mixed methods.** Quantitative: an **O*NET-based occupational exposure ranking** (Schendstok & Schreiner Wertz 2024, following Kochhar 2023) applied to **2023 ACS microdata**. Qualitative: roundtables and listening sessions.
- **Exposure definition:** likelihood a work activity could be **aided or replaced** by gen AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini). O*NET rates the importance of 41 work activities; each is classed high/medium/low exposure, then weighted by importance to the occupation. The **top 25% of occupations** are "highly exposed." High-exposure tasks include getting/processing information, scheduling, controlling machines/processes, and administrative activities.
- **Scope caveats:** does **not** measure actual job loss/creation/transformation; medium/low-exposure jobs may become more exposed as tools improve; **agentic AI and AI+robotics are out of scope** (flagged for future study).
- **Lower-income defined at the household level:** households below **80% of area median income**; workers are those 16+ with earned income in the past year. By this definition **48.9 million workers (28% of all earners)** lived in a lower-income household in 2023. Lower-income workers overall skew **younger** and **less educated** and cluster in Office/Admin Support, Sales, Food Prep, and Health Care Support (all below-average earnings).
- **Qualitative base:** five roundtables (New Orleans, Phoenix, Portland, Salt Lake City, San Francisco, Apr–Jul 2025, 46 participants) plus two listening sessions with the 11-member Community Advisory Council (Feb & Sep 2025) — ~57 participants across workforce boards, community-based/training orgs, financial institutions, community colleges, philanthropy, and employers.

## Quantitative Findings

### Scale — 6 million-plus lower-income workers are highly exposed

- **30.6 million workers (>17% of earners)** qualified as highly exposed; **20% (one in five)** lived in a lower-income household. Most exposed workers (any income) are in the private sector, but exposed lower-income workers are relatively more likely to be **self-employed or in the nonprofit sector**.

| Class of employment | All highly exposed | Lower-income highly exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Private | 67.5% | 64.4% |
| Government | 15.7% | 14.4% |
| Self-employed | 8.9% | 11.6% |
| Nonprofit | 7.7% | 9.2% |

### Occupations — concentrated in office/administrative support

- Leading exposed occupations are the same regardless of income (Office and Administrative Support, then Business and Financial Operations, then Architecture and Engineering). But while **~1 in 3 of all exposed workers** are in office/admin jobs, **fully half of exposed lower-income workers** are.
- Most common lower-income exposed jobs in that category: secretaries/administrative assistants, receptionists/information clerks, office clerks. Other exposed jobs span HR workers, computer support specialists, cashiers, engineers, software developers. Annual earnings for these lower-income exposed workers ranged from **<$14,000 (cashiers) to ~$53,000 (software developers)**.

### Industries — over-represented in service sectors

- Largest exposed industry overall is **Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services** regardless of income. But lower-income exposed workers diverge — more likely in **Health Care and Social Assistance, Transportation and Warehousing, Retail**, and other service sectors.

| Industry | Lower-income exposed | All exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Professional, Scientific & Technical Services | 17.8% | 22.1% |
| Health Care & Social Assistance | 12.6% | 9.1% |
| Finance & Insurance | 10.0% | 11.8% |
| Manufacturing | 8.3% | 12.1% |
| Educational Services | 7.4% | 5.7% |
| Transportation & Warehousing | 6.9% | 4.3% |

- For the ~50% of lower-income exposed workers in office/admin roles, **Health Care and Social Assistance leads** (>1 in 5); ~21% work for **nonprofits** — more than double the share for exposed lower-income workers overall.

### Demographics — older, more educated, higher-earning (within the lower-income group)

- Consistent with prior work (older workers, women, White/Asian workers over-represented among exposed): exposed lower-income workers are more likely to be **over 25**, **female (62% vs 51%)**, and White or Asian.
- More likely to hold a **bachelor's degree or some college**; **median income $28,000 vs $23,000** for lower-income workers overall; **20%** of exposed lower-income workers earned **>$50,000** in 2023.

### Geography — wide variation

- The lower-income share of the exposed workforce ranged from **~23.4% (Delaware)** down to **15.9% (North Dakota)**, exceeding 40% in some AZ counties and below 5% in Mississippi's South Delta. Office/admin share of exposed lower-income jobs ranged from **61% (Hawai'i)** to **37.1% (Utah)**, shaped by local labour markets.

## Qualitative Findings

- **Varied adoption:** nearly all organisations described themselves as in a **"learning phase."** A minority avoided AI (risk/data concerns — one noted they "still don't know who actually holds the data"); a few built their own tools, aided by **flexible philanthropic funding**. Most were in between — ad hoc employee use of free/low-cost tools, or intentional org-wide adoption for notetaking, communications, fundraising/grant-writing, customer service. AI is increasingly **embedded in platforms already in use**: one leader said, "We were naïve enough to think we had a choice, but it's all happening so fast anyway."
- **Early employment impacts:** for adopters, a common driver was **resource constraints** — nonprofits facing funding shortfalls, businesses backfilling hard-to-recruit roles or relieving skilled staff of administrative burden. Impacts already included **shifting tasks within roles, recalibrating experience requirements, holding off on planned hires, and eliminating positions** to reduce headcount.
- **Critical thinking as the new essential skill:** participants agreed facility with AI is becoming essential ("If you work with a computer, you'll need to know how to work with AI"), but workforce/training orgs varied widely in how much they had adapted services. Concern that clients use AI as an **input, not the output** — e.g. English-language learners can be helped by AI, but copy-pasting output without comprehension "can actually worsen their chances of getting hired." Employers had **not yet** posted many AI-specific skill requirements; emphasis was on transferable "human/soft skills."
- **Uneven impacts / vulnerable workers:** participants worried downside impacts could **worsen outcomes for vulnerable workers unless adequate guardrails and supports are in place**. Specific worries: erosion of office/admin jobs (often held by women and the better-paid among lower-income workers); **older workers** and non-"AI-native" workers needing literacy support; entry-level workers unable to find the office jobs that build career-ladder skills.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Participants **"pointed to the existing digital divide,"** noting that workers with digital-literacy gaps or without reliable internet "risk being left further behind," and that many lower-income workers rely on **mobile devices**, limiting common work functions. This is the report's clearest articulation of an AI/digital divide: exposure lands on workers least equipped — by tools, training, connectivity, and organisational resources — to convert it into benefit.

- Suggested supports: lessons from past transitions, including something akin to **Trade Adjustment Assistance for the age of AI** — buffering both lower-income workers and higher-earners at risk of downward mobility.

## Conclusion

- Although higher-earning white-collar workers are among the most exposed, that is **not where impacts end**: **6M+ lower-income workers (20% of the exposed)** are also highly exposed, largely in administrative-support and business/financial roles at the lower end of the earnings distribution, disproportionately in nonprofit or self-employment.
- AI in these roles could **augment** skills and earnings, but stakeholders fear the task mix lends itself to **automation** and erosion of some of the better-paid jobs lower-income workers hold.
- Individually rational firm decisions could **aggregate into systemic, uneven outcomes**; mitigating them needs training, job-search assistance, and **wraparound supports** across the earnings spectrum. The authors call for further research on exposure beyond the top quartile and on whether exposure becomes **augmentation or replacement**.

## Relation to the book

This brief supports the argument in §6.5 that **those most exposed to AI are least able to acquire the skills to benefit** — the hardening "AI divide." It supplies the *exposure* side of that argument: more than 6 million lower-income workers (20% of the exposed workforce) face on-the-job AI exposure, concentrated in office/administrative and service roles, yet — as the authors' prior work establishes — these workers "have the fewest resources to navigate any such AI-related job or earnings losses." The qualitative sections make the divide explicit: adopters with tools, training, digital access, and flexible funding capture augmentation benefits, while workers with digital-literacy gaps, mobile-only internet, or no engagement with formal training "risk being left further behind." Read alongside the OECD brief (which shows the reskilling pipeline is scarce, advanced-skill-tilted, and gated by above-average prerequisites toward the already-high-skilled), the two together document both halves of the divide: vulnerable workers are exposed **and** the training meant to help them is least accessible to them.
