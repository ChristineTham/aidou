# How the U.S. Public and AI Experts View Artificial Intelligence

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [pew-2025-public-experts-ai.pdf](../../sources/ai-futures/pew-2025-public-experts-ai.pdf) — Pew Research Center (Colleen McClain, Brian Kennedy, Jeffrey Gottfried, Monica Anderson and Giancarlo Pasquini), *How the U.S. Public and AI Experts View Artificial Intelligence*, Pew Research Center report, 3 April 2025. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/03/how-the-us-public-and-ai-experts-view-artificial-intelligence/
> This is an independent, nonpartisan opinion-survey report from a nonadvocacy "fact tank" (a subsidiary of The Pew Charitable Trusts). It is NOT vendor, employer or lab-leader data — it reports what two populations *believe*, not measured outcomes. Note the two samples differ in kind: the public sample is a weighted, nationally representative probability panel; the "AI experts" sample is an unweighted convenience sample of conference authors/presenters, representative only of those who responded.

## Abstract

This Pew Research Center report compares how the American public and people with AI expertise view artificial intelligence, drawing on a survey of 5,410 U.S. adults (12–18 Aug 2024), a separate survey of 1,013 U.S.-based AI experts (14 Aug–31 Oct 2024), and 30 in-depth expert interviews (18 Oct–26 Nov 2024). Its central finding is a large optimism gap: AI experts are far more positive and enthusiastic about AI than the public, yet the two groups share substantial common ground on wanting more personal control and on distrusting both government regulation and corporate self-governance. The single most citable number: **56% of AI experts, versus only 17% of U.S. adults, expect AI to have a very or somewhat positive impact on the United States over the next 20 years.** As the report puts it: "These surveys reveal both deep divides and common ground on AI. AI experts are far more positive than the public about AI's potential, including on jobs. Yet both groups want more personal control of AI and worry about lax government oversight." It matters for a chapter on AI's future because it is one of the few rigorous data points separating expert forecasts from public sentiment, and it shows that agreement on governance failures coexists with sharp disagreement on outcomes.

## Method and the two samples

- **Survey of U.S. adults:** 5,410 adults surveyed 12–18 Aug 2024, drawn from the American Trends Panel (ATP), recruited by national random sampling of residential addresses. Weighted to be representative of the U.S. adult population by gender, race, ethnicity, partisan affiliation, education and other factors.
- **Survey of AI experts:** 1,013 U.S.-based AI experts surveyed 14 Aug–31 Oct 2024. The sample was built from a list of authors and presenters at 21 AI-related conferences held in 2023 or 2024 (covering research, application, business, policy, social science, identity and ethics), identified via public information and an email-finder service. Eligibility required confirming that one's work/research relates to AI or machine learning and that one lives in the U.S. **Responses are unweighted and representative only of experts who responded** — there is no population benchmark for this group.
- **In-depth interviews:** 30 experts interviewed 18 Oct–26 Nov 2024; not representative of any group; quotes lightly edited.
- Limitations the authors state: too few Black or Hispanic experts responded to report expert views by race/ethnicity (reflecting the field's makeup); too few experts from nonprofits, government or self-employment to break those out. The survey was fielded before the 2024 U.S. elections.

## The headline gap: AI's impact on the U.S. over 20 years

- **56% of AI experts** say AI will have a very or somewhat positive impact on the U.S. over the next 20 years, versus **17% of the public.**
- Public concern has grown over time (concern greater than excitement rose from about four-in-ten in 2021 and 2022 to roughly half by 2023).

## Excitement versus concern

- More excited than concerned about increased AI use in daily life: **47% of experts vs. 11% of the public.**
- More concerned than excited: **51% of the public vs. 15% of experts.**
- Equally concerned and excited: **38% in each group** (identical shares).

## Expected personal benefit

- Experts: **76%** think AI will benefit them personally; **15%** think it will harm them.
- Public: **24%** think it will benefit them, **43%** think it will harm them; roughly one-third are unsure.

## Jobs

- AI's impact on how people do their jobs over the next 20 years seen as very/somewhat positive: **73% of experts vs. 23% of the public.**
- Will AI lead to fewer jobs over the next 20 years? **64% of the public** say yes, versus **39% of experts.**
- Extremely/very concerned about job loss: **56% of the public vs. 25% of experts.**
- Common ground on specific occupations: about three-quarters of both groups expect fewer jobs for cashiers; about half or more say the same for journalists, software engineers and factory workers.
- Divergences: **62% of experts vs. 33% of the public** expect fewer jobs for truck drivers; the public is more likely than experts to expect job loss for musicians, teachers and medical doctors (fewer than half in either group for these). Public uncertainty ranges 13%–26% depending on the job.

## Concerns: bias, misinformation, human connection

- Highly concerned that people get inaccurate information from AI: **66% of adults, 70% of experts.**
- Highly concerned about bias in AI decisions: **55% each** (public and experts).
- Highly concerned about AI leading to less human connection: **57% of the public vs. 37% of experts.**
- Inaccurate information, impersonation and data misuse are common worries for both groups.

## Representation in AI design

- Both groups see men's views as better represented than women's: **75% of experts** say designers account for men's perspectives at least somewhat well, but only **44%** say this of women's views. The public sees the same pattern, though about four-in-ten are unsure.
- White adults' views seen as best-represented: ~three-quarters of experts say White adults' perspectives are well-accounted-for; half say this of Asian adults; smaller shares for Black or Hispanic adults. Among the public, four-in-ten say White adults' views are well-represented; a quarter or fewer say so for Asian, Black or Hispanic adults. The public is far more unsure (about four-in-ten or more).

## Control (area of agreement)

- **55% of the public and 57% of experts** say they want more control over how AI is used in their lives.
- Say they have little or no control over whether AI is used in their life: **59% of the public vs. 46% of experts.** Say they have a great deal / quite a bit of control: **14% of the public vs. 23% of experts.**
- Comfortable with the amount of control they have: **38% of experts vs. 19% of the public.** Unsure how much control they want: **26% of the public vs. 4% of experts.**

## Regulation and responsible use (area of agreement on distrust)

- More concerned the government will *not go far enough* than that it will go too far in regulating AI: **about six-in-ten U.S. adults and 56% of experts.** Among the public this holds across parties (Democrats 64% vs. Republicans 55%).
- Little/no confidence the government will regulate AI effectively: **62% of the public, 53% of experts.**
- Little/no confidence in U.S. companies to develop and use AI responsibly: **59% of the public, 55% of experts.**
- By sector among experts: **60%** of those at colleges/universities lack confidence in companies' responsible use, versus **39%** at private companies/businesses; confidence in government is similar across the two.

## Longer-horizon predictions and autonomy

- Extremely/very likely AI will make humans more productive over 20 years: **74% of experts vs. 17% of the public.**
- AI developing independent thought in the next 20 years: **40% of the public** think it possible; **48% of experts** say it is unlikely.
- AI causing major harm to humans: **35% of the public vs. 20% of experts** think it likely; **46% of experts vs. 18% of adults** think it unlikely.
- AI making humans happier: only **6% of adults** think it highly likely; about half say unlikely. Experts also skeptical (35% unlikely vs. 22% likely).
- Trusting AI with important personal decisions: **63% of the public** say AI will *never* reach a point where they'd trust it with important decisions (just 13% think it will); roughly half of experts think it one day will.

## Demographic patterns

- Gender gaps, wider among experts: men see AI's U.S. impact as positive more than women (public 22% vs. 12%; experts 63% vs. 36%). Among experts, men are more likely than women to be more excited than concerned (53% vs. 30%) and to expect personal benefit (81% vs. 64%). Women (experts) are more likely to want more control (67% vs. 54%). Men more likely to say they'd trust AI with important decisions (experts 58% vs. 30%; public 18% vs. 9%).

## Everyday interaction

- **79% of experts** think people in the U.S. interact with AI almost constantly or several times a day; only **27% of the public** think they interact with AI that often (43% say less often than daily).
