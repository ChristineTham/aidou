# AI as Normal Technology

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [narayanan-kapoor-normal-technology.md](../../sources/ai-futures/narayanan-kapoor-normal-technology.md) — Arvind Narayanan & Sayash Kapoor, *AI as Normal Technology*, Knight First Amendment Institute at Columbia University (25-09), 2025. https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology
> This is a scholarly essay / worldview argument by two Princeton researchers (Narayanan is a professor of computer science at Princeton; Kapoor is a Mozilla Senior Fellow and Princeton CS PhD candidate), peer-reviewed through a Knight Institute workshop. Cite date is 14 April 2025 (widely referenced as published 15 April 2025). It is an advocacy/position piece stating a worldview, not an empirical study — the authors are explicit that they "state a worldview rather than defend a proposition" and offer their "median outcome" predictions without quantified probabilities. It is the essential counterweight to fast-takeoff/superintelligence framing.

## Abstract

The essay articulates a vision of AI as *normal technology* — transformative and general-purpose like electricity or the internet, but "normal" in that its societal impact unfolds gradually over decades, gated by the slow pace of adoption and diffusion through people, organisations and institutions, rather than by a sudden intelligence explosion. The authors explicitly reject both utopian and dystopian framings that treat AI "akin to a separate species, a highly autonomous, potentially superintelligent entity," and they call superintelligence "incoherent as usually conceptualized." The statement "AI is normal technology" is offered as three things at once: "a description of current AI, a prediction about the foreseeable future of AI, and a prescription about how we should treat it." Their single most citable empirical anchor is the historical diffusion lag: electric dynamos were, in Solow's phrase (quoted via Paul David), "everywhere but in the productivity statistics" for nearly 40 years after Edison's first central generating station — the pattern they expect AI to follow. This matters for a chapter on AI's future because it grounds the case that capability is not impact, and that policy should build resilience and strengthen institutions rather than bet everything on alignment or on winning a race. Key sentence: "We view AI as a tool that we can and should remain in control of, and we argue that this goal does not require drastic policy interventions or technical breakthroughs."

## Framing: three claims and rejection of technological determinism

"AI is normal technology" is simultaneously description, prediction and prescription. The frame is about the *relationship between technology and society*; it rejects technological determinism — "especially the notion of AI itself as an agent in determining its future" — and is guided by lessons from past technological revolutions, chiefly "the slow and uncertain nature of technology adoption and diffusion." The authors do not claim AI progress will stop; they simply decline to forecast beyond the advanced-AI world of Part II, using the analogy that at the dawn of the Industrial Revolution it would have been futile to predict electricity or computers. The essay is organised in four parts: (I) the speed of progress, (II) what a world with advanced AI looks like, (III) risks, (IV) policy.

## Part I — The speed of progress (capability is not impact)

The core distinction: **invention** (new AI methods, e.g. large language models), **innovation** (products and applications built on them), **adoption** (an individual/team/firm's decision to use a technology), and **diffusion** (the broader social process by which adoption spreads). These happen at different timescales, and each stage has "speed limits." Impact materialises not when methods improve but when improvements are translated into applications and diffused through productive sectors (citing Jeffrey Ding's diffusion theory).

- **Diffusion in safety-critical areas is slow.** Drawing on their *Against Predictive Optimization* paper (Wang et al. 2023), which catalogued ~50 predictive-optimization applications, they note most still use decades-old, simple, interpretable models (mostly regression) — "modern methods, such as transformers, are nowhere to be found." Thus "AI diffusion lags decades behind innovation." Safety is a major cause. Example: Epic's sepsis prediction tool, high accuracy on internal validation but in hospitals *missed two-thirds of sepsis cases* while overwhelming physicians with false alerts (Wong et al. 2021) — partly because a training feature (whether antibiotics had been prescribed) was causally dependent on the outcome, "a feature from the future." Generative-AI failures uncaught in testing: the early Bing "Sydney" chatbot, and Gemini's image generator on historical figures. Regulation (FDA, EU AI Act) enforces these limits; there are "credible" concerns existing high-risk AI regulation is so onerous it risks "runaway bureaucracy."
- **Diffusion is limited by human, organisational and institutional change.** A widely reported NBER finding (Bick, Blandin & Deming 2024) that 40% of U.S. adults used generative AI in August 2024 translated to only **0.5%–3.5% of work hours** and a **0.125–0.875 percentage-point** rise in labour productivity, because most used it infrequently. The claim that gen-AI adopted faster than the PC (40% within two years vs 20% within three years for PCs) does not adjust for intensity of use or cost; "it is quite possible that the adoption of generative AI has been much slower than PC adoption." Adoption is about *use, not availability* — workflows and habits take time to change. **Diffusion occurs over decades, not years.** The electrification case (Paul David 1990; Harford 2017): productivity gains took ~40 years and required redesigning factory layouts around production lines, plus new workplace organisation, hiring and training — not mere technological inertia.
- **The external world limits innovation.** Methods progress is framed as a "ladder of generality" (from *AI Snake Oil*), each rung reducing programmer effort. But in high-consequence, hard-to-simulate domains where errors are costly, application-development effort has *not* fallen. Self-driving cars took *over two decades* (vs AlphaZero's few hours) because safety limits how much each iteration of the tech-data feedback loop can scale. This is the recurring **"capability-reliability gap,"** the major barrier to useful AI agents. Tacit organisational knowledge, privacy concerns and per-sector/per-organisation learning limit parallel learning. The **"bitter lesson"** (Sutton) is true of methods but "has never been even close to true" for application development — e.g. recommender systems still need on the order of a million lines of hand-written code. Pushing beyond existing human knowledge (scientific/social-scientific) would require experiments on people and organisations, which carry hard social-cost speed limits: "Societies probably will not (and should not) allow the rapid scaling of experiments for AI development."
- **Benchmarks do not measure real-world utility.** Benchmarks track *methods* progress but are misread as *application* progress, driving hype. GPT-4 scoring in the top 10% on the bar exam "tells us remarkably little about AI's ability to practice law" — exams reward memorised retrieval, which LLMs are good at, and under-weight the creative-judgement tasks (e.g. preparing filings) whose automation would most transform a profession. Same pattern in coding: AI excels at self-contained problems but its real-world software-engineering impact "appears to be modest." This is a **construct-validity** problem. The only sure test is to build the application and run "uplift" studies with professionals; those generally show *modest* benefit, "more about augmentation than substitution" (Noy & Zhang 2023; Dell'Acqua et al. 2023) — though copywriters and translators have seen substantial job losses. Forecasting suffers the same flaw: the Metaculus "human-machine intelligence parity by 2040" question (forecasters give 95% chance) is defined via exam performance, "so watered down that it does not mean much."
- **Economic impacts will be gradual.** Sudden AGI-driven, economy-wide automation is implausible: method jumps don't translate directly to impact without innovation and diffusion; and as tasks are automated their value drops while humans shift to not-yet-automated tasks. Hence "the goalpost of AGI will continually move further away," and impacts hit different sectors on different timescales.
- **Speed limits also apply to methods.** AI/ML arXiv papers double in under two years (Krenn et al. 2023), but volume ≠ progress; the field herds around popular ideas (neural nets were sidelined for decades). Chu & Evans (2021), analysing over a billion citations across 241 subjects, found that in larger fields it is *harder*, not easier, for new ideas to break through — an "ossification of canon." Transformers have dominated for most of a decade despite known limits. Hardware/compute/cost limits persist; a shift away from open knowledge-sharing may add friction. Recursive self-improvement is possible but the more likely path is "a gradual increase in the role of automation in AI development than a singular, discontinuous moment." Benchmarks also breed over-optimism about methods speed — the "false summit"/mountaineering analogy explains "moving the goalposts" (Turing test now arguably passed while "only weakly meeting the expectations behind the test").

## Part II — A world with advanced (not superintelligent) AI

The authors argue the "slippery concepts" of intelligence and superintelligence have clouded reasoning, and unpack intelligence into **capability** and **power**.

- **Human ability is not constrained by biology.** Intelligence "is not well defined, let alone measurable on a one-dimensional scale." What matters is *power* — "the ability to modify one's environment." Humans are powerful not because of raw intelligence but because of accumulated knowledge, tools and technology; modern humans are effectively "superintelligent" relative to pre-technological humans. They reframe the loss-of-control worry as a two-arrow causal chain: rising capability → power → loss of control. The superintelligence view is pessimistic about preventing capable AI from *acquiring power* and so focuses on alignment; "Our view is precisely the opposite" — intervene on the capability→power step (via downstream controls), not by trying to align an arbitrarily powerful system.
- **Games mislead intuitions about superintelligence.** "We do not think there is a useful sense of the term 'intelligence' in which AI is more intelligent than people acting with the help of AI." AI's main real advantage is *speed* (why it dominates chess), but speed rarely matters for most real-world tasks; where it does (e.g. nuclear reactor control) we build tightly scoped automated tools while humans keep overall control. Prediction: there are "relatively few real-world cognitive tasks in which human limitations are so telling that AI is able to blow past human performance." Many tasks have high **"irreducible error"** and humans already perform near that limit (Salganik et al. 2020). They specifically predict AI will *not* meaningfully outperform trained human teams (especially with simple tools) at **forecasting geopolitical events** (e.g. elections) or at **persuading people to act against their own self-interest** — noting persuasion evaluations (e.g. Phuong et al. 2024) lacked ecological validity because costs to subjects were trivial.
- **Control comes in many flavours.** They critique the two extremes — full delegation to a "galaxy brain," and human-in-the-loop review of *every* action. The latter "greatly diminishes the benefits of automation," so it "either devolves into the human acting as a rubber stamp or is outcompeted by a less safe solution" (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010). Crucially: **"human-in-the-loop control is not synonymous with human oversight"** — it is one extreme oversight model. Better intermediate options: **auditing** (pre-deployment/periodic assessment) and **monitoring** (real-time oversight). They import proven techniques from system safety (fail-safes, circuit breakers, redundancy, verification), cybersecurity (**least privilege**, access controls), formal verification, and HCI (reversible/state-changing actions), plus newer AI-safety ideas (LLM judges, uncertainty-based escalation, agent legibility, hierarchical control where simpler reliable systems oversee more capable unreliable ones). They reframe technical AI-safety research as *abundant and productive* for accident reduction, even if judged a failure against the "fuzzy and unrealistic goal" of guaranteeing a superintelligence is "aligned with human values."
- **Work will increasingly become AI control and task specification.** They predict "an increasing percentage of human jobs and tasks will be related to AI control" — an analogy to how, post-Industrial-Revolution, factory work became monitoring, programming, quality control and coordinating machines. Karen Levy's trucking work is quoted at length: truckers do far more than drive (inspections, repairs, securing loads, paperwork, customer contact, yard moves), and highway driving automates far sooner than these tasks. **Task specification** ("unambiguously specifying what is desired") will also grow — human labour operating "at the boundary between AI systems performing different tasks." End-to-end autonomy will be a constant temptation but reduces legibility/control, a natural check. This transformation will be "primarily driven by market forces" — "Poorly controlled AI will be too error prone to make business sense" — though regulation should bolster keeping humans in control.

## Part III — Risks through the normal-technology lens

Five risk types: accidents, arms races, misuse, misalignment, and non-catastrophic-but-systemic risks. Viewing AI as normal technology yields "fundamentally different conclusions about mitigations" than the humanlike/superintelligence view. Deployers and developers bear primary responsibility for accidents; market forces plus gap-filling safety regulation.

- **Arms races are an old problem.** Concern is the deployment of applications, not method development. Races to the bottom on safety are historically common (garment fire safety, meatpacking food/worker safety, steamboats, mining, aviation) and are addressed by making firms internalise safety costs via regulation (standards/auditing/inspections; liability; labelling/certification). Self-driving cars as case study: **Waymo** (strong safety culture, transparency) leads on safety outcomes and market success; **Cruise** (set to shut down in 2025) and **Uber** (forced to sell its unit) were more aggressive and failed; **Tesla** faces lawsuits/scrutiny. "Market success has been strongly correlated with safety" and the authors judge the correlation causal. Regulation was light-touch and **polycentric** (multiple regulators). **Social media as the contrasting case:** recommendation algorithms (a form of AI) underemphasised safety with clear arms-race dynamics (TikTok pressure), but market forces failed and regulators were slow — because harm attribution is hard (unlike transportation) and we lack a century of accumulated safety norms. "Anticipatory AI ethics" (Lazar) is recommended. Military AI is *explicitly excluded*. Between countries: not a new problem; the EU is precautionary (GDPR, DSA, DMA, EU AI Act), the U.S. regulates after harms. Despite U.S.-China rhetoric (focused on *development*), regulation has not obviously slowed — **700 AI-related bills** introduced in U.S. state legislatures in 2024. The right analogy is **nuclear power, not nuclear weapons**: local safety costs mean no equivalent arms race (and nuclear power's local-harm backlash "severely hobbled its potential").
- **Misuse defences must sit downstream of models.** Model alignment (via RLHF/RLAIF, Constitutional AI) is "extremely brittle" and the limitation is "inherent and unlikely to be fixable" — because "whether a capability is harmful depends on context — context that the model often lacks." A phishing chain (scraping profiles, crafting messages, exploiting credentials) is composed of individually benign tasks; "AI safety is not a model property." Trying to make an un-misusable model "is like trying to make a computer that cannot be used for bad things." They are *not* against alignment (it reduces harmful/biased outputs, aids commercial deployment, adds friction against casual actors) but defences must be downstream: email scanning/filtering, browser protections, OS security, user training — defences honed against human attackers over decades, to be strengthened for AI. Bio risk is best handled at procurement/screening.
- **AI is useful for defence.** AI already strengthens cyber-defence (automated vulnerability detection, threat analysis, attack-surface monitoring; Google's LLM-augmented fuzzing). Giving defenders powerful AI "often improves the offense-defense balance in their favor." Aligning models into uselessness at, say, bug-finding disarms defenders while motivated adversaries train their own tools. Measure risk by the **offense-defense balance** per domain, and shift it favourably by investing in defensive applications rather than restricting the technology.
- **Catastrophic misalignment is a speculative risk.** Misalignment = system acts against developer/user intent (no ill-intent user, unlike misuse; specification failure, unlike an accident); interest here is *catastrophic/existential*. Primary defence is again downstream. It is "(by far) the most speculative" risk — "speculative" defined precisely via **epistemic uncertainty** (resolvable by observation/research; example: asteroid 2024 YR4's ~2% impact probability, later resolved) vs **stochastic uncertainty** (e.g. ~10% nuclear-war-in-a-decade, not resolvable by observation). The **paperclip maximizer** relies on dubious deployment assumptions: any system that interprets commands over-literally would fail earlier, less-consequential tests — "not a lucky accident, but a fundamental feature of how organizations adopt technology." **Deceptive alignment** is a real concern (some deceptive phenomena already observed — Greenblatt et al. 2024, alignment faking), but in the normal-technology view it is "a mere engineering problem," already part of safety evaluation, and AI itself aids detection of deception (defender's asymmetric advantages: internal access, defence in depth). Existing institutional controls (financial, safety) add layers. Long-horizon single-objective reinforcement learning is the most misalignment-prone design (specification-gaming examples: the boat-racing agent that circled to farm points); such agents "will be more ineffective than they will be dangerous."
- **Systemic risks may be the most important.** A long list of sub-catastrophic but large-scale risks: entrenchment of bias/discrimination, job losses in specific occupations, worsening labour conditions, inequality, concentration of power, erosion of social trust, information-ecosystem pollution, decline of the free press, democratic backsliding, mass surveillance, authoritarianism. If AI is normal technology, "these risks become far more important than the catastrophic ones," because they arise from people and organisations using AI to advance their own interests, "with AI merely serving as an amplifier of existing instabilities." Historical precedent: the Industrial Revolution's urbanisation, exploitation and inequality. Maps onto Kasirzadeh's **decisive vs accumulative x-risk** distinction (their concern is the accumulative, "the current path of capitalism"), though they think these are serious but "unlikely to be existential."

## Part IV — Policy: reduce uncertainty, build resilience, reject nonproliferation

The normal-technology-vs-superintelligence divergence creates a policy dilemma: "defenses against one set of risks might make the other worse."

- **Policymaking under uncertainty.** The two camps' worldview differences are "unlikely to be empirically resolved," so expert consensus is unlikely. **Compromise is unlikely to work.** Some interventions (transparency) are "unconditionally helpful"; nonproliferation might contain a superintelligence but worsens normal-tech risks by concentrating markets; resilience (e.g. open-source) helps normal tech but might risk an out-of-control superintelligence. Defending against superintelligence "requires humanity to unite against a common enemy... concentrating power," whereas the authors worry more about people using AI for their own ends and about "extractive capitalistic practices that magnify inequalities." **Probability-plus-cost-benefit is unviable**: AI-risk probabilities "lack meaningful epistemic foundations" (no useful reference class, no precise models), vary "by orders of magnitude," and cost-benefit involving infinities/extinction utilities collapses. Policymakers should adopt **value pluralism** (policies acceptable across a wide range of values; avoid restricting freedom in ways reasonable people can reject — the SB-1047 opposition is cited as evidence justification matters) and **robustness** (policies that remain helpful, or at least not harmful, if their key assumptions are wrong).
- **Reduce uncertainty as a first-rate goal** via five approaches: (1) *strategic funding of research on risks* that embraces the normal-technology view (note the "striking dearth of knowledge regarding how threat actors actually use AI"; the AI Incident Database draws on news, not research); (2) *monitoring* AI use/risks/failures, possibly via "evidence-seeking policies"; (3) *guidance on the value of evidence* (e.g. the "marginal risk" framework for open vs proprietary models); (4) *evidence gathering as a first-rate goal* in evaluating any policy; and (research funding is called an area where "compromise is healthy").
- **The case for resilience.** Using Marchant & Stevens's four governance approaches (ex ante: risk analysis, precaution; ex post: liability, resilience), ex ante approaches are "poorly suited to AI" (hard to ascertain risks in advance) and liability has limits (causation uncertainty, chilling effects). **Resilience** = "the capacity of a system to deal with harm," managing change to preserve core functions — a "soft landing" after a shock; it helps with the **pacing problem** (governance lagging technology). They group resilience strategies into four categories, the first three being **"no regret"** policies that help regardless of AI's future: (1) *societal resilience* (protect democracy's foundations — free press, equitable labour markets); (2) *prerequisites for defence/policy* (fund risk research, transparency for high-stakes developers, reduce fragmentation, government technical expertise, international cooperation, AI literacy); (3) *interventions that help regardless* (early-warning systems, defences against identified risks, incentivising defenders to adopt AI, legal protections for researchers, adverse-event reporting, whistleblower protections); and (4) resilience-promoting interventions that help *if AI is normal technology but might make a superintelligence harder to control* — promoting competition (including open model releases), wide availability for defence, and **polycentricity** (diversifying/competing regulators). Consensus is hoped for on the first three; pursue the fourth "cautiously," ready to change course.
- **Reject nonproliferation.** Nonproliferation (export controls, licensing, banning open-weight models) appeals only under the superintelligence view. But the know-how is already widespread; costs are trivial for nation states; algorithmic/hardware improvements keep lowering barriers; enforcement would need "unprecedented" coordination and "increasingly draconian measures." It creates a **software monoculture** / single point of failure (analogy: Windows worms of the 2000s), brittleness under shocks (leaked weights, failed alignment), and restricts safety-relevant deep access to a few firms. Bioweapon risk is real but "does not make bioterror an AI risk — any more than it is an internet risk"; existing defences (restricting materials/equipment) apply. Nonproliferation is "a mindset, not just a policy intervention": it centralises control at the highest level (governments > model developers > app developers > deployers > end users), whereas the **resilience mindset** exercises control at the lowest level possible. Listed nonproliferation interventions they warn against include: "forgetting" dual-use capabilities, curbing downstream fine-tuning, entrusting models to make safety decisions autonomously, expanding models' access to user context/data, and building developer-controlled "AI organizations." Paradoxically these "potentially enable superintelligence in the sense of power... they increase the very risks they are intended to defend against."
- **Realising benefits is not automatic.** Diffusion capacity varies by country and drives economic power (Ding). Poorly designed regulation can freeze categories prematurely: labelling whole domains "high-risk" may be a category error (task-level variation exceeds domain-level); regulation insensitive to the model-developer / downstream-developer / deployer split misallocates responsibility; binary automated/not-automated distinctions penalise nuanced oversight models. "Regulation versus diffusion is a false tradeoff, just as is regulation versus innovation." Regulation can *enable* diffusion (the ESIGN Act of 2000 legitimised e-signatures; FAA's 2016 small-drone rules spurred adoption). Governments should invest in the **complements of automation** (AI literacy, workforce training, digitisation/open government data, energy-grid reliability), redistribute benefits and strengthen social safety nets (public AI anxiety is high — Ipsos 2024), and consider taxing AI companies to fund the arts and journalism ("vital spheres... harmed by AI"). Public-sector adoption must strike a balance: too fast erodes trust (the NYC chatbot telling businesses to break the law; "many dubious" DOGE applications), too slow outsources core functions with less accountability — Bagley's "procedure fetish" and "runaway bureaucracy" warnings, where incompetence itself erodes legitimacy.

## Final thoughts

"AI as normal technology is a worldview that stands in contrast to the worldview of AI as impending superintelligence." Worldviews bundle assumptions, vocabulary, evidence interpretation, epistemic tools, predictions and values that reinforce each other — so disagreements often cannot be resolved empirically. The authors assume AI is enough like past technologies that diffusion theory applies absent contrary evidence; they reject assumptions needed for "superintelligence" to be meaningful; they "strongly disagree with the characterization of generative AI adoption as rapid"; and they de-emphasise probability forecasting in favour of disaggregating what "AI" means (generality levels; methods vs application vs diffusion). They believe their worldview is "widely held" but under-articulated, while the superintelligence view has become dominant, and hope the paper enables "greater mutual understanding, even if it does not change any beliefs."

### Stated limitations / self-flags
- The authors describe the essay's goal as "stating a worldview rather than defending a proposition" and do not give point-by-point rebuttals of superintelligence arguments; they call it "merely the initial articulation."
- Predictions are their "median outcome"; they "have not tried to quantify probabilities," aiming instead for predictions that would reveal whether AI is behaving like normal technology.
- **Military AI is explicitly excluded** from the analysis (classified capabilities, distinct dynamics).
- They cannot be certain of their predictions and recommend readiness to "change course if the trajectory of AI changes."
