# The Uneven Impact of Generative AI on Entrepreneurial Performance

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [otis-uneven-impact-genai.pdf](../../sources/ai-futures/otis-uneven-impact-genai.pdf) — Nicholas G. Otis, Rowan Clarke, Solène Delecourt, David Holtz & Rembrand Koning, *The Uneven Impact of Generative AI on Entrepreneurial Performance*, Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-042, 2023 (PDF compiled 27 Feb 2024). Cite as Otis et al., 2023.
> **This is a WORKING PAPER (not yet peer-reviewed).** It reports a pre-registered randomised field experiment. Treat its point estimates — especially the sub-group effects — as provisional; the authors themselves note the study was under-powered for several of the analyses and flag many results as exploratory or non-pre-registered.

## Abstract

This is a randomised field experiment testing whether a GPT-4-powered AI business mentor, delivered over WhatsApp to 640 Kenyan small-business entrepreneurs, improved their revenues and profits over roughly five months. The central finding is a null on average but a strongly uneven one underneath: the authors "are unable to reject the null hypothesis that generative AI access has no impact" overall, yet "high performers benefited by just over 15% from AI advice, whereas low performers did about 8% worse with AI assistance." The gap between the two groups is real and significant (0.267 s.d., p = 0.013). The most citable mechanism is that this divergence was *not* explained by differences in the number, length, or human-rated quality of the questions each group asked — those were statistically indistinguishable — but by *which tasks* each group brought to the AI: low performers "sought help on more challenging business tasks" that neither an AI nor a human mentor could readily fix. This matters for a chapter on AI's future because it is direct field evidence, from outside the lab and outside the rich world, that generative AI can *widen* rather than narrow performance gaps — the benefit accrues to those already equipped to judge and act on advice. Verbatim: "not only are the impacts of AI uneven, but that they radically shift depending on the tasks that AI tools are used for."

## What the study is (Design)

- **Setting and sample.** A field experiment with **640 Kenyan entrepreneurs** running small and medium businesses, recruited via Meta/Facebook ads in partnership with the Busara Center from May 2023. The final sample completed all three pre-treatment survey waves. The median entrepreneur was 26 years old, had run their business one year, and held a college degree. Businesses ranged from fast-food joints to poultry farms to cybershops. Pre-treatment monthly profits ranged from ~2,650 Kenyan Shillings (5th percentile, ~$15 USD) to over 55,000 KSh (95th percentile, ~$340 USD).
- **The intervention.** Treated entrepreneurs received free, unlimited access to a custom **GPT-4-powered "AI mentor" over WhatsApp** (chosen because ~90% of Kenyans use WhatsApp). The tool differed from raw GPT-4 in three ways: a system prompt giving Kenyan-SME context (e.g. steering "raise capital" toward family/friends or a "chama" informal cooperative rather than venture capital); an instruction to return **three to five practical pieces of advice** per question, each with benefits and implementation details; and formatting that invited follow-up questions. Development took four months with user testing. The mentor **does not learn** from interactions.
- **The control.** Rather than a pure control, the control group received a **standard business guide** (ILO training materials for developing-economy SMEs) plus reminders to use it — a placebo-like active control. This design isolates the effect of AI access net of "simply being given a business resource" and helps rule out survey demand effects. Control participants were told nothing about generative AI or the mentor. The authors note prior research shows such asynchronous guides are rarely used, so the design is, if anything, conservative.
- **Timeline and retention.** ~5 months total: three months of pre-treatment surveys, then two months post-treatment with four post-treatment survey waves. Entrepreneurs were **block-randomised**, stratified by gender and pre-treatment performance. Retention was very high: 634/640 (99%) completed at least one post-treatment survey, 622/640 (97%) completed all four.
- **Outcome and analysis.** The pre-registered outcome is a standardised **index combining weekly/monthly revenue and profit**. Estimation used OLS conditioning on averaged pre-treatment performance, a double-LASSO for additional covariates, and pooled data across all four post-treatment periods for power.
- **Cost framing.** The mentor cost "a few dollars per participant"; conventional training programs cost hundreds of USD per participant — the pitch is scalable, low-cost mentorship.

## Results

### No average effect — and large effects are ruled out
- The estimated **average treatment effect (ATE) is null**: δ = 0.05 s.d. (p = 0.36, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.16]) at 99% winsorisation, δ = -0.01 s.d. (p = 0.92) at 95%. Robust to dropping the 18 non-completers.
- Via equivalence tests, they **strongly reject** the presence of an effect ≥ 0.26 s.d. — the minimum detectable effect of Noy & Zhang (2023) (Z = -3.89, p = 0.00005). Recent AI studies have reported effects approaching 0.5 s.d.; this study rules those out.
- They **cannot** reject smaller effects: at a 0.09 s.d. threshold (from Campos et al. 2017 initiative training) the equivalence test fails (Z = -0.69, p = 0.28). So effects on the order of human business-training programs remain possible. The preferred-specification ATE is positive at +3.2% to 4.4% of profit/revenue — roughly one-third the size of human training effects — but the study was not powered to detect it.

### The uneven effect (the headline)
The null masks opposing, pre-registered heterogeneity by baseline (pre-treatment) performance, split at the median:
- **Low performers (below-median baseline):** AI access *reduced* average performance, δ = **-0.09 s.d.** (p = 0.007, 95% CI [-0.15, -0.03]). No significant effect at the median (QTE δ = -0.03, p = 0.21) — the harm concentrates in the upper quantiles of their change distribution.
- **High performers (above-median baseline):** point estimate on the mean δ = **+0.19 s.d.** (p = 0.07, not significant, noisy), but a significant *median* effect (QTE δ = +0.10 s.d., p = 0.03, 95% CI [0.01, 0.18]).
- **The gap is significant:** difference in ATEs = **0.267 s.d.** (p = 0.013, 95% CI [0.06, 0.48]).
- In plain terms: the low-performer estimate is **~8% drop** in profits/revenue; the high-performer estimate is **~15% increase**.
- This runs **opposite** to prior AI studies (Noy & Zhang; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond; Peng et al.; Dell'Acqua et al.), which mostly found AI *narrowing* productivity gaps by lifting the weakest most. Here AI *widened* the gap.
- No heterogeneous effects were found by prior ChatGPT use or by gender. (Note: one-third of the sample reported using ChatGPT at least weekly pre-treatment.)

## Mechanism: it's the choice of task, not the questions

The authors ran exploratory analyses of the WhatsApp interaction logs (~1,300 business-related questions) to explain the divergence. Their key move is to rule out the obvious explanations first:
- **Not the quantity of questions.** Both groups' distributions were highly skewed and "extremely similar"; ~15% of entrepreneurs asked no business question at all, and those who did asked roughly one a week. High performers sent 0.59 more messages on average — not significant (p = 0.33).
- **Not the quality or length of questions.** Human coders (three-point scale) found no significant difference in question quality (δ = -0.07, p = 0.29) or length (δ = -0.11, p = 0.14) between low and high performers.
- **It IS the difficulty of the task chosen.** Using word-embedding methods, they built a "challenge-focused" measure of each question (closeness to a benchmark "business in dire straits" vs "business facing few challenges"). **High performers asked *less* challenge-focused questions** (δ = -0.25 s.d., p = 0.04); on a continuous baseline measure, a 1 s.d. higher baseline performance is associated with ~0.5 s.d. less challenge-focused questions (δ = -0.48, p = 0.03). Low performers brought harder problems — bankruptcy, collapsing demand, a competitor undercutting prices — while high performers asked about expansion and growth.
- **The interpretation.** The AI generated well-structured advice for everyone, but the hard problems low performers faced often require capital or other complements the advice couldn't supply, and some of the recommendations were themselves costly to implement — so acting on the advice could *lower* profits. Returns to AI advice *within a given task* may be equal or higher for low performers (consistent with prior work); the divergence arises because low and high performers *endogenously select different tasks* to bring to the AI. As the paper puts it, "whether by choice or by necessity, low-performing entrepreneurs in our sample asked the AI mentor for assistance with more challenging tasks."

> Pairing note for the chapter: this dovetails with the Bastani learning finding — AI helps those already equipped to judge and deploy it. Here the differentiator is not the advice given or the questions asked but *which advice each group ends up acting on*; strong performers steer the tool toward problems where good advice pays off.

## Discussion and the authors' own limitations

- **Contribution framing.** To the authors' knowledge, this is the first RCT of generative AI's impact on firms in a developing economy. It shows GPT-4-based tools *can* deliver useful personalised feedback in low- and middle-income settings (countering the worry that US-centric training data makes LLMs useless there) — but *also* that AI can **widen the gap** between weak and strong firms. The 15% high-performer estimate is comparable to the best human mentorship/peer-learning programs (which raise profits ~15%, sales ~11%), suggesting AI mentors can already advise some businesses as well as human mentors, at a fraction of the cost.
- **Stated limitations (authors' own):**
  1. The mentor was "only a relatively short system prompt" on top of GPT-4, with just a 5-minute training survey for users — fine-tuning, better prompting, or more user training might improve high-performer gains and reduce/reverse the low-performer harm.
  2. Despite being longer than most AI experiments (5 months), it may miss longer-term effects, including entrepreneurial learning — which could itself be heterogeneous by baseline.
  3. The experiment ran May–November 2023, when AI familiarity in Kenya was still low; results could differ as AI use becomes widespread among participants, controls, and competitors alike.
  4. The mechanism analysis is exploratory: they don't know whether low performers chose hard questions "by choice or by necessity," why some entrepreneurs kept using AI despite negative returns, or how much of the heterogeneity stems from GPT-4 training-data bias.
- **Ethics.** Because a negative effect on low performers was detected, the authors discuss ethical considerations (Appendix F). The study was approved by the UC Berkeley Office for the Protection of Human Subjects.
- **Closing claim (verbatim):** the findings "highlight that not only are the impacts of AI uneven, but that they radically shift depending on the tasks that AI tools are used for."
