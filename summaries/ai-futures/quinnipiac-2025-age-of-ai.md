# The Age of Artificial Intelligence — Quinnipiac University National Poll — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [quinnipiac-2025-age-of-ai.md](../../sources/ai-futures/quinnipiac-2025-age-of-ai.md) · Quinnipiac University Poll (dir. Doug Schwartz, Ph.D.), release #3923, 16 April 2025 · https://poll.qu.edu/poll-release?releaseid=3923.
> Study-guide summary of an independent, non-partisan national opinion poll. It reports what Americans *believe* about AI, not measured outcomes. Fielded 3–7 April 2025; n = 1,562 U.S. adults (±2.5 pts); employed-adults subsample n = 867 (±3.3 pts). Conducted with Quinnipiac's School of Computing & Engineering and School of Business.

## Abstract

A national survey of 1,562 U.S. adults (3–7 April 2025) finding that Americans are broadly wary of AI's effect on daily life, expect it to harm education, but expect it to help medical advances — and that attitudes split sharply by income. Two findings are the reason to cite it. First, an **income divide**: 60% of households earning over $200k say AI will do more good than harm in daily life, while 59% of households earning under $50k say more harm than good (just 26% more good) — the affluent, better positioned to benefit, are optimistic; the low-income, more exposed to displacement, are not. Second, a **workforce paradox**: 56% of Americans expect AI to reduce job opportunities overall, yet 78% of employed adults are *not* worried their own job will be made obsolete (only 21% concerned). People foresee harm in the aggregate while feeling personally safe.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The two headline facts for the book: (1) optimism about AI rises with income — 60% "more good" among >$200k households vs 59% "more harm" (26% "more good") among <$50k households; (2) 56% expect AI to cut jobs generally, but 78% of employed adults are not worried about their *own* job.

## Day-to-day impact (more good vs more harm)

- Overall: **44%** more harm than good; **38%** more good than harm; **18%** no opinion. On balance, wary.
- **By household income** (the decisive breakdown):

| Household income | More good than harm | More harm than good |
|---|---|---|
| Over $200,000 / year | **60%** | 30% |
| Under $50,000 / year | 26% | **59%** |

- The gradient is the point: optimism tracks income. Those most likely to capture AI's gains view it favourably; those most exposed to its costs view it as harmful. Verbatim faculty gloss: "Americans draw lines based on trust and who stands to gain or lose" (Chetan Jaiswal).

## Education and medical advances (domain split)

- **Education:** 54% more harm than good; 32% more good; 15% no opinion — net negative.
- **Medical advances:** 59% more good than harm; 24% more harm; 17% no opinion — net positive.
- Americans distinguish by domain: wary where AI touches how the young learn, hopeful where it advances medicine.

## Jobs — the workforce paradox

- **Aggregate forecast:** 56% think AI advancements are likely to lead to a *decrease* in job opportunities; 24% no difference; 13% expect an increase.
- **Personal risk (employed adults only, n = 867):** just **21%** are concerned their own job could be made obsolete (6% very, 15% somewhat), while **78%** are not concerned (22% not so, 56% not at all). Essentially unchanged since 24 May 2023.

> [!NOTE]
> The paradox is an expectation gap, not a contradiction in the data: the same public that predicts broad job loss overwhelmingly exempts *itself*. "Americans Expect AI Will Cause Job Losses But Workers Don't Think Their Own Jobs Will Disappear" (release headline).

## Comfort with AI making consequential decisions

Share comfortable with AI being used to:

| Use of AI | Comfortable | Not comfortable |
|---|---|---|
| Screen health insurance claims | 23% | 71% |
| Screen loan applications | 27% | 67% |
| Screen job applications | 30% | 64% |
| Assist police in identifying suspects | 53% | 42% |

- Resistance is highest where AI decides personal outcomes — coverage, credit, employment — and lowest for the policing task. "The public draws the line at AI influencing personal outcomes — jobs, loans, and healthcare" (Tamilla Triantoro).

## Method (scope and limits)

- 1,562 U.S. adults nationwide, 3–7 April 2025, ±2.5 pts; employed subsample 867, ±3.3 pts.
- Independent, non-partisan poll; measures *stated attitudes*, not behaviour or economic outcomes. A single snapshot; U.S.-only; sub-group splits (income) carry larger error than the topline.

## Relation to the book

This poll gives §6.5 ("AI and Society") a piece of hard public-opinion evidence for the chapter's argument that AI is a lever which rewards those already positioned to use it and can widen the gap between winners and losers. The income divide is that argument seen in attitudes: the households best placed to capture AI's gains (>$200k, 60% "more good") are optimistic, while those most exposed to its costs (<$50k, 59% "more harm") are not — the felt version of §6.5's "AI rewards the person who can already judge its output and quietly penalises the one who cannot." It also supports §6.5's opening move — that a technology "trusted this unevenly is a governance problem before it is a technical one" — with a second, income-based cut alongside the Pew public-vs-expert gap already cited there. The workforce paradox (56% expect job cuts; 78% of workers unworried about their own job) is a useful, honest complication for the chapter's hedged claim that people who bear AI's costs without its benefits may turn against it: expected *collective* harm coexists with *personal* security, so grievance today is diffuse and second-hand rather than felt at the individual level — which is exactly why the Anelli–Colantone–Stanig robot study is needed to show what can happen once that exposure becomes personal.
