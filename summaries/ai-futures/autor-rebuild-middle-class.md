# Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs

> [!NOTE]
> Source: [autor-rebuild-middle-class.pdf](../../sources/ai-futures/autor-rebuild-middle-class.pdf) — David Autor (MIT & NBER), *Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs*, NBER Working Paper No. 32140, February 2024. <http://www.nber.org/papers/w32140>
> **What this is:** a normative advocacy essay by a leading labour economist, not an empirical study or a forecast. It was drafted for *NOEMA Magazine* (published there as "How AI Could Help Rebuild The Middle Class") and circulated as an NBER working paper — **not peer-reviewed**. Autor explicitly frames the argument as "**not a forecast but an argument about what is possible**" — a scenario contingent on human choices. It is the optimistic counterweight to techno-pessimist visions of AI-driven job loss. Autor discloses support from Google, among other funders.

## Abstract

This is David Autor's argument that AI, unlike the computerisation that preceded it, could **reverse** four decades of labour-market polarisation rather than deepen it. His central thesis: information was never the scarce, valuable thing — **decision-making** was, and computerisation concentrated decision-making power among "elite experts" (the ~40% of US adults with college degrees) while automating away the middle-skill "mass expertise" stratum. AI's distinctive capability is the inverse of the computer's: it is weak at rules and facts but strong at tacit knowledge and judgment, so it can *extend expert judgment* to a broader set of mid-skill workers who possess complementary foundational training. If used well, this could let non-elite workers perform higher-stakes tasks "currently arrogated to elite experts" — his running example is the Nurse Practitioner — thereby lowering the cost of scarce services (healthcare, education, law), moderating inequality, and rebuilding the middle class. The single most citable framing, verbatim: *"My thesis is not a forecast but an argument about what is possible: AI, if used well, can assist with restoring the middle-skill, middle-class heart of the US labor market that has been hollowed out by automation and globalization."* Autor stresses this outcome is "**not an inevitable or intrinsic consequence of AI development**" but depends on institutional and political choices.

## Framing: the fear is misplaced, the real risk is different

Autor opens against the techno-pessimists — Elon Musk ("the most disruptive force in history … there will come a point where no job is needed") and Geoffrey Hinton (advising people to "get a job in plumbing"). A 2023 Gallup poll found **75% of US adults believe AI will lead to fewer jobs**. Autor calls this fear "misplaced." His demographic counter-argument: the industrialised world is "long on jobs and short on workers." Four years after Covid's onset, US unemployment had returned to its pre-Covid low and total employment was "nearly three million above its pre-Covid peak." Because of plummeting birth rates, "all the people who will turn thirty in the year 2053 have already been born" — a "demographic fact," not a prediction — so rich countries "will run out of workers before we run out of jobs."

He reframes the real danger. AI's risk to labour markets "is not that of a technologically jobless future. The risk is the **devaluation of expertise**." A world where humans supply only "generic, undifferentiated labor" is one where "no one is an expert because everyone is an expert" — labour becomes disposable and wealth accrues to "owners of Artificial Intelligence patents" ("'WALL-E' meets 'Mad Max'").

## Expertise as the source of labour's value

Autor defines **expertise** as "the knowledge or competency required to accomplish a particular task," which "commands a market premium if it is both necessary for accomplishing an objective and relatively scarce." His illustrative pairing: **air traffic controllers** (median $132,250 in 2022) versus **crossing guards** (median $33,380) — "in broad strokes … the same job" (rapid life-or-death collision-avoidance decisions), but paid nearly 4× differently because one requires scarce, years-long training and the other requires none. Expertise is "in constant flux": farriery, typesetting, fur-trapping and spell-checking once commanded premiums; oncologists, software engineers and patent lawyers did not exist until innovations created a need for them.

## Historical arc: artisanal → mass → elite expertise

Autor's historical framing structures the argument as three technological epochs:

1. **Artisanal expertise (pre-Industrial).** Skilled artisans (wheelwrights, tailors, cobblers, blacksmiths) combined two forms of expertise: **procedural expertise** (practised steps) and **expert judgment** (adapting procedures to variable instances). Mass production in the 18th–19th centuries "decimated" artisanal value by breaking complex work into simple mechanistic steps. The Luddites' fear "was justified": handloom weavers were "quite rapidly wiped out by factories after 1815," and working-class living standards took roughly five decades to begin rising.
2. **Mass expertise (Industrial era).** Complex machinery generated demand for a new middle-skill worker expertise — machinists, welders, electricians, telephone operators, typists, bookkeepers — much of it novel, requiring literacy and numeracy. A workforce newly endowed with high-school diplomas met this demand, building a new middle class. But (per Taylor's 1911 scientific management) mass-expert work required following rules while exercising "little discretion," making it "uniquely vulnerable to technological displacement."
3. **Elite expertise (Information Age).** The computer's power was cheap, reliable execution of tasks "encoded in explicit, deterministic rules" — economists' "routine tasks" (Autor, Levy & Murnane, 2003). This **eroded mass expertise** (automating clerical/administrative/production work) while it was "a divine gift" to elite professionals, freeing them from acquiring/organising information to spend more time on decision-making, thus magnifying the value of expert judgment. **Polanyi's Paradox** ("We can know more than we can tell") — the existence of non-routine tasks humans perform but cannot verbalise — kept high-judgment professional work out of computers' reach. Result: not a new era of mass expertise but "a four-decade-long trend of rising inequality." Workers displaced from middle-skill jobs were "shunted into" low-paid, non-routine hands-on service work, pushing those wages down further.

## Why AI is different: complementing judgment

Autor's pivot: "Artificial Intelligence's capabilities are precisely the inverse" of the computer's. In "a case of cosmic irony, AI is not trustworthy with facts and numbers — it does not respect rules. AI is, however, remarkably effective at acquiring tacit knowledge." His analogy: a traditional program is "a classical performer playing only the notes on the sheet music"; AI is "more like a jazz musician — riffing on existing melodies … humming new tunes." Because AI can "weave formal knowledge (rules) with acquired experience" to support one-off, high-stakes decisions, it can "engage in expert judgment — a capability that, until now, has fallen within the province of elite experts."

**This is the crux of the tool-vs-replacement distinction.** Autor insists AI is "a tool, like a calculator or a chainsaw, and tools generally aren't substitutes for expertise but rather levers for its application." Tools "enable workers with proper training and judgment to accomplish tasks that were previously time-consuming, failure-prone or infeasible" but are "useless at best — and hazardous at worst — to those lacking relevant training" (a nail gun is a time-saver for a roofer, "a looming impalement hazard for a home hobbyist"). AI's primary role, he argues, "will be to advise, coach and alert decision-makers as they apply expert judgment."

**The inversion technology.** Where computerisation devalued middle-skill procedural expertise while enriching elite decision-makers, AI could "invert this process": "support and supplement judgment, thus enabling a larger set of non-elite workers to engage in high-stakes decision-making," while "temper[ing] the monopoly power that doctors hold over medical care, lawyers over document production, software engineers over computer code, professors over undergraduate education." This would improve non-degree job quality, moderate earnings inequality, and lower the cost of key services. Federal Reserve economists Dohrman and Fallick (2020) estimate healthcare and education prices rose ~**200%** and ~**600%** respectively relative to household incomes over four decades — driven partly by the cost of employing scarce elite experts. AI could reduce that scarcity.

## The Nurse Practitioner: the anchoring example (deliberately non-AI)

Autor's central concrete case is offered explicitly as "an example that is not from the AI realm." **Nurse Practitioners (NPs)** are Registered Nurses with an additional master's degree, certified to order/interpret diagnostic tests, diagnose patients, and prescribe medications — tasks "once exclusively within the domain of physicians." NP employment "nearly tripled" between 2011 and 2022 to ~**224,000** (from ~81,000 per BLS/FRED), projected to grow ~**40%** over the next decade; median NP salary was **$125,900** in 2022. The NP is "an uncommonly large-scale case where high-stakes professional tasks — diagnosing, treating and prescribing — have been reallocated (or co-assigned) from the most elite professional workers (MDs) to another set of professionals (NPs) with somewhat less elite (though still substantial) formal expertise."

Crucially, what made this reallocation possible was **institutional** (new training programmes, a certification regime, scope-of-practice regulatory change won after "a hard-fought … battle with … the American Medical Association") — plus a complementary role for **information technology**: electronic medical records and better communication tools "enabled NPs to make better decisions" (Li et al., 2012). Autor's projection: "AI could ultimately supplement the expert judgment of NPs" for a broader scope of care, and the point generalises — "from contract law to calculus instruction to catheterization."

## Proof-of-concept evidence (four studies)

Autor cites four recent studies as "'proof-of-concept'" — noting the pattern that gains concentrate among **less-skilled/novice** workers, compressing productivity gaps:

- **GitHub Copilot** (Peng et al., 2023): the treatment group completed a programming task **~56% faster** than the control group.
- **ChatGPT for writing** (Noy & Zhang, *Science*, 2023): time on task fell **~40%** across the board; "the biggest quality improvements were concentrated at the bottom" — the least effective writers with ChatGPT were "about as effective as the median writer without." ChatGPT "did not eliminate the role of expertise"; the best writers stayed on top.
- **Customer service** (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2023): **~14%** productivity improvement, "most pronounced among novice workers"; novices reached experienced-agent capability "in three months rather than 10," and quit rates fell.
- **Radiology** (Agarwal et al., 2023) — "an exception that arguably proves the rule": AI did **not** improve radiologists' diagnostic quality even though its predictions were "at least as accurate as nearly two-thirds of the doctors studied," because doctors didn't know how to use it — overriding confident (correct) AI predictions and deferring to uncertain (worse) ones. Autor's lesson: complementary use "will require training and the acquisition of additional expertise," not that AI is contraindicated.

In the first three, "AI tools supplemented expertise rather than displaced experts," via a combination of automation (time savings — AI drafts the code/copy/response) and augmentation (quality). Workers still "apply their expertise and judgment to produce the final product."

## Substitution vs complementarity — the foundation requirement

Autor's guardrail against over-reading his optimism: AI generally "will not … enable untrained, non-expert workers to carry out high-stakes tasks." His **YouTube analogy**: how-to videos are useful to skilled tradespeople but hazardous to amateurs — replacing his own home fuse box from a video would risk "a nasty shock or electrical fire" because he lacks "foundational expertise." "Rather than making expertise unnecessary, tools often make it more valuable by extending its efficacy and scope. And the more powerful the tool, the higher the stakes" ("a little learning is a dang'rous thing"). AI "can extend the reach of expertise by building stories atop a good foundation and sound structure. Absent this footing, it is a structural hazard."

On robots doing jobs "solo": Autor thinks cost-effective deployment of robots in "unpredictable real-world environments" remains "distant" — witness the faltering of autonomous driving despite ">$100 billion" of investment; the hard part isn't operating controls but "interpreting and responding … to a world of unpredictable pedestrians."

## The Twilight of Expertise? — automation vs new work

Autor grants that "tools can and do encroach on their users' expertise": smartphone navigation apps made London cabbies' hard-won "Knowledge" "technologically obsolete and economically superfluous." But the opposite is "just as often true" — radar, GPS and two-way radios *created* the expert work of air traffic control. AI "will automate the core tasks of some occupations, eliminate others … [and] simultaneously instantiate new goods and services, generate new demands for expertise." Agriculture fell from ~**35%** of US employment in 1900 to ~**1%** in 2022 without the economy running out of work.

He is candid about the downside, and this is an important caveat against reading him as a pure optimist: "There is no economic law that says that the forces of automation and new work creation are equal and offsetting; in fact, recent evidence indicates that the former is outpacing the latter" (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2022; Autor et al., forthcoming). "These countervailing effects will create winners and losers, and the adjustment may be wrenching" — and "it is unlikely that workers whose expertise will be displaced by AI will be the same ones whose expertise is made newly valuable."

## A Scenario, Not a Forecast — the explicit contingency caveat

The essay's closing section is Autor's strongest statement that this is a normative argument, not a prediction. Outcomes "are determined foremost by the institutions in which [technologies] were created and the incentives under which they are deployed." His analogy: controlled nuclear fission yielded both weapons and carbon-free power; North Korea chose weapons and no civilian plants, Japan the reverse. "Artificial Intelligence is far more malleable and broadly applicable than nuclear technology." He warns the same AI is already used by some states to surveil populations and hunt dissidents, and elsewhere to accelerate drug discovery and tutor learners.

He argues the pessimist visionaries are "likely wrong" — OpenAI's charter defining AGI as "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work," and Suleyman's "how will humans compete?", both flatten "the complexity of innovation into a single dimension of automation." His rejoinder: "Airplanes are not our competitors; we simply couldn't fly without them." "Replicating our existing capabilities, simply at greater speed and lower cost, is a minor achievement. The most valuable tools complement human capabilities and open new frontiers."

The final, load-bearing caveat, verbatim: *"This alternative path is not an inevitable or intrinsic consequence of AI development. It is, however, technologically plausible, economically coherent and morally compelling. Recognizing this potential, we should ask not what AI will do to us, but what we want it to do for us."* Autor invokes Zuboff's "inevitabilism" as the error to avoid — the belief in technological inevitability that "deprives citizens of agency."

## Limitations / how to use this source

- **It is an argument, not evidence.** Autor states repeatedly that this is "a claim about what is attainable," "a scenario, not a forecast." Cite it as a *normative possibility* framed by an authoritative labour economist, not as a prediction or finding.
- **Not peer-reviewed** (NBER working paper / magazine essay). The empirical support is four cited studies (three of which are themselves working papers), used illustratively.
- **Autor's own hedges** cut against naive optimism: automation may be *outpacing* new-work creation; displaced and newly-valued workers likely differ; the transition "may be wrenching"; complementarity requires *foundational expertise* and *training* (radiology counter-case).
- On NP data, Autor himself flags: "It is challenging to find consistent time-series data on NP employment in the U.S." (BLS vs OOH figures differ).
- Google is disclosed among the essay's funders.
