# Bridging the AI Skills Gap: Is Training Keeping Up? — Summary

> [!NOTE]
> **Source:** [oecd-bridging-ai-skills-gap.pdf](../../sources/ai-futures/oecd-bridging-ai-skills-gap.pdf) · OECD, 2025 · *OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers*, No. 66, OECD Publishing, Paris (policy brief, 24 April 2025) · <https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/bridging-the-ai-skills-gap_66d0702e-en.html>.
> Study-guide summary of the full 6-page policy brief. See the original for exact wording and Figure 1.

## Abstract

A six-page OECD policy brief arguing that adult-learning systems are **not yet equipped** to deliver the AI skills the workforce will need — training supply is thin, skewed toward advanced/professional skills over general AI literacy, and structured in ways that favour already-high-skilled adults. It draws on a companion OECD report analysing policy in 21 countries and training-course catalogues in Australia, Germany, Singapore and the United States.

**Key points**

- **One in three job vacancies** in OECD economies have high AI exposure, yet only about **1%** of jobs require specialist, complex AI skills; the vast majority of exposed workers need only **general AI literacy**, not to build or maintain AI models.
- Only a **small percentage of training courses deliver AI content** — between **0.3% and 5.5%** across the four countries analysed (Australia 0.3%, Germany 2.5%, Singapore 5.5%, United States 0.6%) — signalling that supply must scale up, especially for literacy.
- The **majority of AI training focuses on advanced/professional skills** rather than general literacy; of 14 (of 21) countries with AI-specific programmes, 9 target AI professionals and only 7 target general-public literacy.
- **AI-related training carries more prerequisites than the average training course**, so supply is "more targeted towards higher skilled adults" — meaning the workers most vulnerable to AI-driven displacement (low-skilled, high-automation-risk) are **least likely to access or participate** in it.
- Countries lean heavily on **financial incentives** (subsidies, grants) and underuse non-financial levers (career guidance, public-private collaboration, train-the-trainer), and AI training remains relatively **rigid** (few online/hybrid options).

**Takeaways**

- Because AI training is scarce, tilted to advanced skills, gated by prerequisites, and inflexible, current supply tends to **reinforce rather than close the skills gap** — it reaches the already-skilled while the most exposed and vulnerable are left behind.
- The remedy the OECD proposes is to **better target and broaden** provision: tie funding directly to AI skills, remove high entry requirements, expand entry-level bootcamps and flexible (online/part-time) options, and embed AI upskilling in wider workforce strategies with attention to those most at risk of transition.

## Key messages (brief's own summary)

- AI is increasingly important in the workplace, driving demand for **both** specialised AI professionals **and** workers with a general understanding of AI.
- A growing number of OECD countries are implementing upskilling/reskilling strategies, including publicly-funded AI training — but "more can be done" to fit programmes to the future AI-driven workplace.
- A subset-of-countries analysis shows **only a small percentage** of training courses deliver AI content; supply needs scaling.
- Most programmes with AI content focus on **advanced** skills; most countries could benefit from broader courses promoting **general AI literacy**.
- Policymakers should expand and better target both literacy and advanced-skills initiatives using financial and non-financial incentives, industry collaboration, and more inclusive learning pathways.

## Understanding the current supply of AI training

- Skills studies usually examine the **demand side** (skills employers cannot source) and neglect the **supply side** (how providers/governments actually deliver relevant courses). A new OECD report (OECD, 2024, *Training Supply for the Green and AI Transitions*) addresses the supply side.
- **Scale of exposure:** with one in three vacancies highly AI-exposed, a significant share of OECD jobs are influenced by AI. Only ~**1%** require specific, complex AI skills (Green, 2024).
- Advanced-skills training supports a highly skilled **AI professional** workforce, but the **vast majority of exposed workers will not need specialised skills** — they need general AI literacy: understanding AI well enough to communicate and collaborate with it, including AI ethics, awareness of risks/harms, and the ability to critically evaluate AI tools in use.
- Evidence base: policy analysis of **21 countries** plus novel analysis of training-course catalogues in **Australia, Germany, Singapore and the United States** (share of courses with AI content, and their accessibility/flexibility).

## Training supply is not yet well-equipped

- Many countries have training incentives, but they **often lack a direct focus on AI**. Some (not all) of the 21 have dedicated AI strategies. Employer subsidies and worker/jobseeker financial support usually are **not explicitly tied to AI skills**; publicly-funded digital-skills training without an explicit AI focus is common.
- Of the 21 countries, **14 have invested in AI-specific training**: **9 focus on developing AI professionals**, **7 aim to build general-public AI literacy** (a couple do both).
- Countries **rely heavily on financial incentives** and underutilise other tools; an inclusive transition needs a more holistic approach using non-financial levers (career guidance, public-private collaboration, train-the-trainer) to reach a wider, more diverse audience.

## Policy-initiative examples

**General AI literacy (Table 1):**

| Country | Initiative |
|---|---|
| Austria | *Digital Everywhere (Digital Überall)* — planned 3,500 workshops across all municipalities in 2024 (youth centres, retirement homes) to build basic digital, AI and cybersecurity competencies across the population. |
| Germany | AI Strategy *"AI Studios" (KI-Studios)* — mobile/stationary studios helping SMEs adopt AI via interactive demonstrators; collaboration of research institutes, universities and the Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs. |
| Singapore | *SkillsFuture for Digital Workplace 2.0* — two-day courses on automation, cybersecurity risk, data analytics and digital tools; **targeted at adults in AI-affected, low-skill jobs**; subsidised via SkillsFuture Credit (SGD 500 for Singaporeans 25+). |
| Hungary | *Hungarian AI Challenge* (2020) — gamified 2–3 hour beginner course; goal to give ~1% of the population (≈100,000 people) basic AI understanding, plus reach 1 million adults via exhibits/events. |

**AI professional training (Table 2):**

| Country | Initiative |
|---|---|
| Poland | *AI Tech* — tech companies + universities co-create AI master's programmes; five universities launched AI/cybersecurity/ML programmes in 2021 with EU funding. |
| United Kingdom | *Skills Bootcamps* (2020) — up to 16-week free, sector-specific training (incl. AI) for employed and unemployed adults; employer-designed; completion offers a job interview. |

## More inclusive training options can expand participation

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This is the brief's central equity finding. Inclusive, flexible training is key to managing AI's unequal impacts, "yet AI-related training remains relatively rigid." **Low-skilled adults and workers in high-automation-risk jobs** — the most vulnerable to adverse employment effects — "are often also the least likely to access and participate in training."

- OECD (2024) finds **most AI-related training is for AI professionals**, with **"more pre-requisites demanded for AI-related training than the average training course."**
- This "suggests that the current supply of AI-related training may be **more targeted towards higher skilled adults**." Beginner-level courses exist, but there is room to expand entry-level options.
- AI courses are only slightly less likely to be in-person than average, but the share of **online/hybrid courses remains relatively low**, limiting flexibility and access.

> [!WARNING]
> The combined picture — scarce supply, an advanced-skills tilt, above-average prerequisites, and low flexibility — means the training that does exist flows disproportionately to those **already** high-skilled, while the most exposed and least-resourced workers are the least able to reach it. Training supply, as currently structured, risks **widening** the skills gap rather than bridging it.

## What can policymakers do?

- **Better target financial support** (subsidies, grants) so it is directly linked to AI skills — both specialised competencies and general literacy — with particular attention to those most vulnerable to transition risks.
- **Increase non-financial incentives** (advice/career guidance, public-private collaboration, train-the-trainer), which remain underused.
- **Foster government–education–industry collaboration** to co-design curricula aligned with current and future workplace demand.
- **Reduce or remove high entry requirements** and promote entry-level initiatives (bootcamps, short courses) so people with minimal prior qualifications can gain AI literacy — a more inclusive transition.
- **Expand flexible learning** (online, part-time) to broaden accessibility and draw in underrepresented groups.
- **Integrate AI training into broader workforce strategies**, embedding upskilling/reskilling in workforce-development plans and encouraging a lifelong-learning mindset among employers.

## Relation to the book

This source is direct evidence for the argument in §6.5 that **those most exposed to AI are least able to acquire the skills to benefit** — the hardening "AI divide". The OECD documents the *supply-side* mechanism behind that divide: AI-related training is scarce (0.3–5.5% of courses), tilted toward advanced professional skills rather than the general literacy most workers actually need, and — crucially — carries **more prerequisites than the average course**, so it is "more targeted towards higher skilled adults." Meanwhile the workers most vulnerable to displacement (low-skilled, high-automation-risk) are "often also the least likely to access and participate in training." In other words, the very training meant to bridge the gap flows to those who already have the most skills, so supply as currently structured tends to **widen rather than narrow** the divide. It pairs naturally with the FRBSF brief (which documents the *exposure* side of the same divide among lower-income workers): together they show both that vulnerable workers are exposed and that the reskilling pipeline is not reaching them.
