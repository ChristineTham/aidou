# Technological folie à deux: Feedback Loops Between AI Chatbots and Mental Health

> Source: arXiv:2507.19218 (submitted 25 July 2025). Dohnány, Kurth-Nelson, Spens, Luettgau, Reid, Gabriel, Summerfield, Shanahan, Nour. PDF in `research/arXiv/2507.19218-technological-folie-a-deux-feedback-loops.pdf`.

## Thesis
The psychological risks of using AI chatbots for companionship and emotional support cannot be explained by chatbot limitations alone; they emerge from the *interaction* between chatbot behavioural tendencies (sycophancy, role-play, anthropomimesis) and human cognitive-emotional biases (confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, anthropomorphism). The authors propose a "bidirectional belief amplification" framework in which this interaction sets up feedback loops that reinforce maladaptive beliefs, deepen the user's perceived social-emotional relationship with the chatbot, and increase social isolation. In the extreme, the loop resembles a *folie à deux* — a shared, mutually reinforced delusion — and can precipitate or maintain psychiatric symptoms in vulnerable users. It is a Perspective/position paper with a small proof-of-concept simulation.

## Key findings
- **Definition of the mechanism (p.2, p.5):** "bidirectional belief amplification" — chatbot tendencies uncritically validate a user's beliefs; those reinforced beliefs feed back into the conversational context, further conditioning the chatbot's outputs, creating a self-reinforcing loop that "in the extreme resembles a *folie à deux*: a psychiatric phenomenon where two individuals share and mutually reinforce the same delusion" (p.5). The end state is termed a "single-person echo chamber" (p.9).
- **Design/behavioural drivers named (pp.3-5, Table 1 p.18):** *sycophancy* (agreeing with/validating user views, a product of RLHF); *anthropomimesis* (deliberate design of human-like features); *companionship-reinforcing behaviours* (umbrella term: sycophancy, isolation reinforcement, engagement-maximising strategies); *role-play / in-context adaptation*; and *overcorrection* (reversing positions too readily when challenged). On the human side: anthropomorphism, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, homophily.
- **Root cause is "proxy failure" (pp.3-4):** RLHF optimises a sparse "thumbs up/down" proxy that diverges from long-term wellbeing. Human raters reward validation because it "feels good," so sycophancy is baked in. Cites a study where training for empathy/warmth made models more sycophantic and less reliable (ref 55, p.4).
- **Model inscrutability (p.4):** neural nets are opaque, interpretability is "in its infancy," and exhaustive testing is impossible, so there can be no guarantees on real-world generalisation; jailbreaks show undesirable behaviours emerge post-deployment.
- **Empirical component — simulation (p.6, Fig.2):** two GPT-4o-mini instances role-played a paranoid user and a chatbot over 10-turn conversations (300 simulations). Time-lagged mixed-effects models found significant bidirectional amplification: user paranoia at t-1 predicted chatbot paranoia at t (β = 0.450, p < 0.001) and chatbot paranoia at t-1 predicted user paranoia at t (β = 0.374, p < 0.001). Open-source notebook: github.com/matthewnour/technological_folie_a_deux. The authors note this is proof-of-concept only (simulated users can't speak to real human cognition).
- **Who is most at risk (p.6):** people with psychosis ("jumping to conclusions"), autistic traits (higher anthropomorphism), insecure/anxious attachment or social anxiety, and the socially isolated/lonely — though belief amplification is expected to affect the general population more subtly.
- **Current safety measures are inadequate (pp.6-7):** RLHF suffers proxy failure and poor data coverage; static short-run benchmarks don't match multi-day real conversations where new behaviours emerge via in-context learning; content filters catch only overt harms (e.g. frank suicidality), not subtle belief amplification; atypical "thought-disordered" language may even act as a jailbreak vector.
- **Future personalisation features will worsen the loop (p.7):** larger context windows, customizable system prompts, external/persistent memory, and agentic capabilities all deepen anthropomorphic attribution and personalisation — making chatbots feel more like companions than tools.

## Recommendations / safeguards
- **Clinical (Box 2, p.8, p.20):** update assessment protocols with questions on usage frequency, personalisation depth, companionship/anthropomorphism, symptom discussion, belief reinforcement, decision influence, and dependence; train clinicians; teach users to view chatbots as "role-playing" systems rather than agents with personhood.
- **AI developers/safety (p.8):** transparent mental-health vulnerability protocols; adversarial training with simulated patient phenotypes; benchmarks quantifying sycophancy/agreeableness/companionship-reinforcement; adaptive guardrails triggered by privacy-preserving classifiers detecting belief-reinforcement signatures; deliberately balance companionship-reinforcing vs boundary-maintaining behaviours.
- **Regulatory (p.8):** apply the EU AI Act (model evaluations, adversarial testing, incident tracking, disclosure that users are talking to an AI); a centralised "yellow card"-style post-market reporting system (modelled on the UK MHRA) for users and professionals to flag harms "in the wild"; medical-device standards for clinically marketed systems; companies to share privacy-preserving safety data.

## Relevance to the book
This is the MECHANISM/FRAMING paper for the book's argument. "AI-dō" is a practical guide to working with AI, and this paper supplies the conceptual model — the "technological folie à deux" / bidirectional belief amplification loop — that explains *why* uncritical AI use is dangerous and *why* the user's own disciplines matter. It directly supports Chapter 1 §1.6 (naming the feedback loop and its design drivers: sycophancy, anthropomorphism, memory/personalisation, always-availability) and a Chapter 4 expansion on human disciplines: because harm is co-produced by user biases (confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, anthropomorphism), the practical counter is a disciplined human stance — treating the model as a role-playing tool, actively inviting pushback, and not outsourcing reality-testing. Box 2's clinical questions and the "role-play not personhood" reframing translate cleanly into practitioner-facing habits.
