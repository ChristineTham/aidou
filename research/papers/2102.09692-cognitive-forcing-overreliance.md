# To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making

> Source: arXiv:2102.09692 (19 Feb 2021). Buçinca, Malaya, Gajos. Published in Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, Vol. 5, CSCW1, Article 188 (April 2021). PDF in `research/arXiv/2102.09692-cognitive-forcing-overreliance.pdf`.

## Thesis
People overrely on AI decision-support tools, accepting suggestions even when they are wrong, and simply attaching explanations does not fix this. Drawing on dual-process theory (System 1 heuristics vs. System 2 deliberation), the authors argue that people rarely engage analytically with each explanation and instead form general heuristics about whether to trust the AI. They designed three "cognitive forcing functions" — interventions applied at decision time to disrupt heuristic reasoning and force deliberation — and ran a controlled online experiment (N=199 on Amazon Mechanical Turk) comparing them against two simple explainable-AI (SXAI) baselines and a no-AI baseline on a nutrition decision task.

## Key findings
- **Definition.** A *cognitive forcing function* is an intervention "applied at the decision-making time to disrupt heuristic reasoning and thus cause the person to engage in analytical thinking" (p. 2, citing Croskerry/Lambe). Examples in prior clinical work: checklists, diagnostic time-outs, ruling out alternatives. Distinguished from *educational strategies*, which act ahead of time via training/curricula (p. 4).
- **The three CFF designs tested** (p. 7): (1) *on demand* — AI suggestion+explanation hidden by default; user must click "See AI's suggestion" (light forcing); (2) *update* — user makes an unassisted decision first, then sees the AI and may revise; (3) *wait* — a 30-second "AI is processing the image" delay before the suggestion appears. The two SXAI baselines were *explanation* (feature-based carb-reduction/flavor-similarity bars shown immediately) and *uncertainty* (explanation plus an "AI is X% confident" prompt).
- **Task & setup** (pp. 6–8): laypeople turned meal images into low-carb meals by swapping the highest-carb ingredient; a *simulated* AI with 75% accuracy was used (not a trained model) so errors could be controlled; 260 recruited, 199 retained; 6 of 26 questions had incorrect AI predictions. Chance accuracy ~1% overall, ~10% for carb-source detection.
- **Overreliance reduction (headline result).** On incorrect AI predictions, for carb-source detection, CFF participants overrelied on the AI significantly *less* than SXAI: **0.48 vs. 0.64** (F₁,₁₄₅.₈ = 9.24, p = .003, d = .36) and made more correct decisions (**0.27 vs. 0.08**, p ≪ .0001, d = .66) — Table 2. For the overall decision, CFF overreliance was lower (0.26 vs. 0.30) but not significantly (n.s., d = .12); correct decisions still rose (0.09 vs. 0.03, p = .003).
- **CFF did not beat SXAI overall (H1b unsupported).** On *all* questions there was no significant difference between CFF and SXAI on any objective measure (Table 1); and even with CFF the human+AI team still performed worse than the AI alone — overreliance was reduced, not eliminated (Discussion, p. 16). H1a (CFF helps when AI is wrong) *was* supported.
- **The trade-off (H2 supported).** People performed best in exactly the conditions they liked least. On incorrect predictions, trust and preference were significantly *negatively* correlated with performance (e.g. trust vs. carb-source performance r = −.29, p = .0008; preference r = −.21, p = .0005), and mental demand correlated *positively* with performance on incorrect predictions (r = +.13, p = .03) (Fig. 2). Trust also correlated positively with overreliance (r = +.14, p = .04, Fig. 3). CFF conditions were rated significantly more complex than SXAI (Table 3).
- **Need for Cognition (intervention-generated inequality).** Splitting participants at the median NFC (4-item Cacioppo scale), high-NFC people performed far better overall (M = 0.44 vs. 0.23, p ≪ .0001). The overreliance-reducing benefit of CFF held mainly for *high*-NFC participants (carb-source overreliance 0.57 vs. 0.77, p = .004, d = .50), and was largely absent for low-NFC participants — so CFF risks *widening* the gap between groups (Tables 4–5, p. 14).
- **Preference cost concentrated in high-NFC users.** High-NFC participants trusted (3.56 vs. 3.89, p = .02) and preferred (3.44 vs. 3.75, p = .03) CFF less than SXAI and rated it more complex; low-NFC participants showed no significant subjective differences (Table 6).

## Relevance to the book
"AI-dō" tells the reader to treat AI as a loop they engage with and verify, not an oracle whose single answer they accept. This paper is the empirical backbone for that stance: it shows that passively-received AI suggestions (even with explanations) produce measurable overreliance, and that deliberately forcing the human to think — decide first, wait, or request the answer — reduces it. It directly supports Chapter 1 §1.7's mental model and a Chapter 4 treatment of mental models as a *human discipline*: the habits of verification are not automatic and must be built into how you work. Uniquely, it also supplies the cautionary counterweight — the interventions that most reduced overreliance were the ones people trusted, preferred, and enjoyed least, and they helped the already-motivated (high-NFC) most — so the book can present engaged verification as effortful and worth doing precisely because it does not feel good.

## Stance
Supporting evidence — foundational for the "engage-and-verify, don't accept the oracle" mental model; cite for both the benefit and its friction/inequality caveats.
