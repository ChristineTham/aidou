# Tabular Data Understanding with LLMs: A Survey of Recent Advances and Challenges

> Source: arXiv:2508.00217 (31 Jul 2025). Wu, Ritter, Xu. PDF in `research/arXiv/2508.00217-tabular-understanding-llm-survey.pdf`.

## Thesis
Tables are inherently two-dimensional, but LLMs and MLLMs require one-dimensional input, so tables must be converted into some representation before a model can read them — and that conversion loses structural information. The survey builds a taxonomy of tabular input representations (serialization, database/dataframe schema, image, and specialised table encoders) and shows that model performance is highly sensitive to which representation is chosen. It argues the field lacks robust, unbiased comparisons because benchmarks pick formats "for convenience" rather than principle, and that no single representation generalises across tasks.

## Key findings
- **Serialization is the dominant way to feed tables to LLMs.** Section 3.1 states serialization "has long been a common method for representing tabular data" and that "most current benchmarks rely on serialization" (p. 5). Its appeal is compatibility with standard models and the fact that these formats already exist in the wild — "HTML or Markdown tables on the web, LaTeX tables in PDF documents, and JSON or key-value pairs in code environments" (p. 5).
- **The serialization formats compared** are HTML, Markdown, LaTeX, JSON, XML, and X-/pipe-separated (CSV-like) text (taxonomy Figure 4, and Figure 5 which contrasts LaTeX vs "| - separated" vs JSON for the same semi-structured table, p. 4–5). Benchmark tables (Tables 1–3, p. 6+) tag each dataset's format — a mix of HTML (WTQ, SQA, MULTIHIERTT), JSON (HybridQA, OTT-QA, MMQA, Rotowire), XML/JSON (DART), and row-/X-separated text.
- **Format choice is task- and data-dependent, not universal.** Performance on common benchmarks "can vary by up to 5% depending on how closely input formats align with the data encountered during pretraining" (Sui et al. 2024, cited p. 4). Different formats suit different structures: LaTeX's `\multicolumn` "effectively captures hierarchical structures, whereas other formats may ignore this type of relationship during serialization" (Figure 5, p. 5). Even within one nominal format like JSON, "the internal structures can vary greatly," biasing evaluations (p. 4).
- **Input design matters as much as the format label.** Beyond the serialization choice, layout details drive large swings: omitting marked partitions or altering input order caused performance drops of "up to 20%," and removing example shots led to accuracy losses "as much as 50%" (Sui et al. 2024, cited p. 5).
- **Token/length limits constrain serialized tables.** Long or multi-table inputs "pose challenges for serialization due to model input length limitations, often resulting in truncation or data loss" (p. 5). The survey's remedy is sampling/augmentation: embedding-based (centroid, semantic) row/column sampling plus balanced augmentation "has proven effective in achieving better overall performance within token limits" (Sui et al. 2023, cited p. 5).
- **Schema representations trade content for compactness.** Providing only the schema (SQL DDL, pandas dataframes) "allows models to bypass input length limitations by focusing only on the structural blueprint," at the cost of losing detailed values; omitting primary/foreign keys sharply degrades results, and ~3 example rows worked best on GPT-3.5 (Zhang et al. 2023a; Chen et al. 2024, cited p. 5).
- **The precise per-format token-cost and accuracy numbers are NOT in this survey.** The comparative claim that some formats are far more verbose (e.g. HTML) and that different formats win on different GPT table-QA tasks originates in the cited **Sui et al. 2024, "Table meets LLM" (arXiv:2305.13062)** and related standardisation work (Lei et al. 2023, which offers Markdown and flattened-text as standardised options). This survey references those results but does not restate the raw token counts.
- **Open gap flagged:** "serialization-to-serialization" conversion (e.g. JSON → LaTeX/Markdown) is underexplored, and there are no robust, unbiased head-to-head comparisons of representations for complex tables (p. 5).

## Relevance to the book
Supports Chapter 2 §2.4 "Everything Becomes Markdown." Its unique contribution is authoritative, peer-reviewed evidence that (a) serialization into text formats is the standard mechanism for getting structured data into an LLM at all, and (b) Markdown sits among the small handful of go-to serialization formats precisely because it already exists on the web and is a "standardised" readable option (Lei et al. 2023, cited here). It also gives the book the honest nuance §2.4 needs: the best format is task-dependent, format choice can move accuracy by several to tens of percent, and length/token limits are a real pressure that pushes toward compact representations. For the specific "HTML is ~3x the tokens / CSV is cheapest / different formats win different tasks" figures, cite the downstream source it points to — Sui et al. 2024, arXiv:2305.13062 — rather than this survey.

## Stance
Supporting (contextual) — establishes serialization/Markdown as the norm and format-sensitivity as real; defers exact token-cost numbers to Sui et al. 2024.
