# The Rise of AI-Native Software Engineering: Implications for Practice, Education, and the Future Workforce

> Source: arXiv:2606.12986 (2026-06-11). Alenezi, M. PDF in `research/arXiv/2606.12986-rise-of-ai-native-se.pdf`.

## Thesis
A PRISMA-inspired systematic review of 48 verified peer-reviewed studies (2016–2026) argues that GenAI, LLMs, and agentic systems are shifting the locus of software engineering from code authorship to intent specification, collaboration, and verification. The evidence is consistent on direction but internally contradictory on the magnitude and even the sign of productivity effects, which the author says are strongly moderated by expertise, task novelty, and codebase maturity. From this synthesis the paper derives an AI-native framework (intent, collaboration, verification), a nine-dimension competency model, and a phased curriculum roadmap. The central claim is that the scarce, teachable human capability is now judgment, not code production.

## Key findings
- LLM-for-SE research grew roughly five-fold after ChatGPT: 7 studies (2020), 13 (2021), 56 (2022), 273 (2023), citing Hou et al. (pp. 4, 6).
- Productivity evidence is genuinely split: Copilot users finished a bounded HTTP-server task 55.8% faster and three firm RCTs (4,867 developers) showed ~26% more completed tasks, yet a 2025 METR RCT found experienced developers ~19% *slower* on mature repositories while believing themselves faster (pp. 8-10).
- Security/quality is sobering: ~40% of 1,689 Copilot-generated programs across MITRE Top-25 CWEs were vulnerable, and AI-assisted users wrote *less* secure code while rating it more secure (overconfidence) (pp. 7, 10).
- An adoption–trust divergence: AI-tool usage rose from ~76% (2024) to ~84% (2025) of developers even as self-reported trust in AI output declined, motivating a "trust paradox" (p. 4).
- SWE-bench exposed the toy-vs-real gap: on 2,294 real GitHub issues the best model initially solved only ~2%, versus benchmark success on isolated functions (pp. 3, 7).
- Three structuring tensions: a productivity paradox, a competence paradox ("illusion of competence"), and a trust paradox; all converge on judgment, specification, and calibrated trust as the pivotal skills (pp. 10-11).

## Relevance to the book
This is exactly the peer-reviewed ballast Chapter 3 needs to balance a single opinion writer: it is a systematic review, not a hot take, and it directly frames "AI-native software engineering" around intent, collaboration, and verification—overlapping the chapter's intent-driven / ICE material and the SE 3.0 vision (Hassan et al.). Its contradiction-first stance is a corrective to both breathless vibe-coding optimism and blanket skepticism, giving the chapter empirically grounded nuance (novices gain, experts on mature code can lose). The competency and curriculum material also supports any "AI-native software engineering" and education framing.

## Stance
AI-native-framing (with strong empirical-vibe and quality-skeptic threads) — organizes the field around intent/collaboration/verification and treats "AI-native SE" as a reorganization of human–machine responsibility rather than mere tooling; refuses a universal productivity narrative, foregrounding context-dependence, security regressions, and the trust paradox.
